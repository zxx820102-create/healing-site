# The Gut Textbook v1 — The Microbiome, What to Feed It, and the Evidence Boundaries

> **Readers and how to use this book**: self-learners and health workers read systematically from Chapter 1; if you only want to act now, Chapter 0 is enough. Every conclusion carries a confidence tier (the four plain-language tiers), and the [L3:id] at the end of each section leads back to the evidence-layer source text (assertion + falsifier). This book never states observational evidence as causation, and never states animal mechanisms as human outcomes — epistemic states are aligned verbatim with L3, zero promotion.

## Chapter 0. The one-page action version (L1)

> The full version is `docs/healing/textbook/gut-l1-80yi-20260912.md` and can be distributed on its own; the essentials follow.

**The doctrine in one line: feeding your microbes stands on two legs — fermented foods add microbes, diverse fiber feeds them. Probiotics have evidence in only a few scenarios; testing can describe, it cannot prescribe.**

**Six action cards**: ① fermented food every day — start at 1 serving, target 2-6 servings (refrigerated, genuinely fermented varieties); in the 10-week trial the microbiome became more diverse and multiple inflammatory markers fell — the high-fiber arm of the same trial did not achieve this (confirmed by research · single trial) ② fiber 25-38 g/day, rotating across the three types; increase gradually to prevent gas (fairly well established) ③ about 30 different plants a week (still being studied) ④ keep ultra-processed food to a minimum (fairly well established · animal mechanism + human observation) ⑤ antibiotics: use them when they are indicated; after the hit most microbes return within months, but in a small trial 9 common species were still undetected at half a year (confirmed by research · single small trial) ⑥ buy probiotics by scenario and by strain: Saccharomyces boulardii to prevent antibiotic-associated diarrhea, and to shorten childhood diarrhea by about 1 day, has reasonably good evidence; for everything else the default is don't buy (confirmed by research · meta-analysis).

**The care-seeking line**: for severe diarrhea (high fever / bloody stool / dehydration) and acute-phase inflammatory bowel disease, seek medical care first — do not manage it on your own; if you are immunocompromised, have heart valve disease, or carry a deep venous catheter, ask a doctor before using any probiotic; fecal microbiota transplantation is done only in medical settings (there has been a case in which donor microbes killed a recipient).

**Myth quick-check**: everyone should take probiotics (no) ｜ hundreds of billions is stronger (a dose only means something inside a trial's context) ｜ yogurt alone covers your microbes (fiber is the staple food) ｜ take probiotics to top up brain serotonin (serotonin does not enter the brain) ｜ a test can write your meal plan (it is only a snapshot) ｜ transplant a lean person's microbes to lose weight (it does not hold in humans).

**Not known**: the total weight of the microbiome (a few grams); what a "depressed-type" microbiome does to healthy people; whether donor traits pass to the recipient long-term with the transplant.

## Chapter 1. What the microbiome is: numbers and boundaries

**Learning objectives**: be able to state the true scale figures, the composition rules, and the colonization windows; be able to use "association ≠ target" to see through diversity marketing.

### 1.1 Scale: 38 trillion, not "outnumbered ten to one"

The old claim that "bacteria outnumber human cells ten to one" is the outdated framing. A 2016 re-estimate: about **38 trillion** bacteria versus about 30 trillion human cells — a ratio close to **1:1** (fairly well established · re-estimate framing) [L3:A1 MODEL]. The gap at the gene level is the dramatic one: the first version of the gut microbial gene catalog listed about **3.3 million** genes, against roughly 22,000 human genes — about **150-fold** (confirmed by research; the sample was predominantly European subjects, and the catalog keeps expanding) [L3:A1 FACT]. "Hundreds of grams" for the total mass of the microbiome is only an order-of-magnitude estimate — **not known** [L3:A1 UNKNOWN].

### 1.2 Composition: stable at the phylum level, widely varying between individuals

The adult gut is dominated by Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, accompanied by archaea, fungi, bacteriophages, and others; composition at the genus-and-species level varies enormously between individuals (confirmed by research · sequencing consensus · descriptive) [L3:A2 FACT]. **There is no single gold-standard blueprint of a "healthy microbiome."** Alpha diversity (species richness and evenness within one person) often correlates with better metabolic/inflammatory markers, but it is an **associational indicator, not a treatment target** — pursuing diversity for its own sake has not been shown in controlled trials to improve clinical outcomes (still being studied) [L3:A2 INFERENCE]. "Key species decline in illness" — butyrate-producing Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, Roseburia, Akkermansia, among others — is a reliable observation, but **≠ "replace them and you are well"** (still being studied) [L3:A2 INFERENCE].

### 1.3 Colonization windows: the birth window and after an antibiotic hit

Birth is the first colonization window: vaginally delivered babies start with a microbiome close to the mother's vaginal/gut flora, while cesarean-delivered babies lean toward skin-derived and environmental microbes; the difference can persist for months (confirmed by research · cohort) [L3:A3 FACT]. Ages 0-3 are the immune-programming window: in infancy Bifidobacterium dominates (driven by human milk oligosaccharides); antibiotic exposure inside this window is associated, in **observational studies**, with later allergy and obesity risk — human causation unproven (still being studied) [L3:A3 INFERENCE].

Recovery after antibiotics in adults: after a 4-day broad-spectrum antibiotic course, most species in healthy adults returned to baseline within about 1.5-180 days, but **9 common species were still undetected at 180 days** (in some individuals), and some genera showed signals of permanent loss (confirmed by research · single trial, 12 people); the direction "cumulative hits → long-term ecological damage" is credible, its quantification is unknown (fairly well established) [L3:A3 FACT/MODEL].

|---|---|---|

**Summary**: the scale has been corrected (38 trillion · 1:1); stable at phylum level, widely varying between individuals; diversity is an associational indicator, not a target; birth and antibiotics are the two great perturbation windows.

## Chapter 2. What to eat to feed your microbes

**Learning objectives**: be able to rank the "feed your microbes" priorities; be able to restate the fermented-food trial's findings and its three boundaries.

### 2.1 Fermented foods: the single strongest-evidenced action of this round

A 10-week randomized controlled trial (RCT; 17+13 participants): the **high-fermented-food arm** (about 6 servings of fermented food daily) raised microbiome diversity, and 4 of 19 inflammatory markers fell significantly with an overall downward trend; the **high-fiber arm** (18 high-fiber foods, target about 30 g/day) showed no significant diversity change within 10 weeks (confirmed by research · **single trial, small sample, surrogate markers**) [L3:B1 FACT].

Three boundaries (the honesty clause): ① small sample, short duration, and surrogate markers were observed (intermediate markers, not disease outcomes); ② "fermented beats fiber" **is not** a conclusion — the authors read it as poor fiber response in people with low baseline diversity; the baseline decides the response; ③ heat processing kills the strains, but the fermentation metabolites remain [L3:B1]. Actionable: 2-6 servings a day of **live** fermented food (refrigerated, genuinely fermented yogurt / kefir / kimchi / miso, etc.), starting from 1 serving; increase gradually if you are prone to gas [L3:B1].

**Table 2-1 The two arms of the same trial**

| Arm | Intervention | 10-week result | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| High-fermented arm | about 6 servings of fermented food daily | diversity ↑; 4/19 inflammatory markers significantly ↓ | confirmed by research (single trial · small sample) |
| High-fiber arm | about 30 g/day · 18 high-fiber foods | no significant change in diversity | same as above (the negative side of the same trial) |

### 2.2 Fiber: the three types' roles and the doses

The three fiber types play different roles (mechanism-level consensus, confirmed by research) [L3:B2 FACT]: ① **soluble fermentable fiber** (oats, barley, legumes, pectin, inulin) → colonic fermentation produces short-chain fatty acids, and butyrate is the colon epithelium's main energy source; ② **insoluble fiber** (wheat bran, whole grains, vegetable roughage) → bulk and motility; ③ **resistant starch** (cooled rice/potatoes, green bananas, legumes) → strongly butyrate-directed. Doses: dietary guidelines recommend **25-38 g/day**; the modern average is about 15 g; studies of inulin/fructooligosaccharides commonly use 5-15 g — **start at 3-5 g to prevent gas** (fairly well established · consensus framing) [L3:B2 MODEL]. **About 30 different plants a week** comes from a large observational project: the more species you eat, the higher the diversity — an observational lead, causation undecided (still being studied) [L3:B2 INFERENCE].

### 2.3 Polyphenols and ultra-processed food

Polyphenols (berries, green tea, coffee, dark chocolate, olive oil, etc.) are poorly absorbed as-is and mostly act after being metabolized by the microbiome; your microbiome's structure in turn decides your polyphenol response — a two-way interaction; hard human outcomes unproven (fairly well established · mechanism) [L3:B3 MODEL]. Ultra-processed food: common emulsifiers (carboxymethylcellulose, polysorbate 80) disrupt the mucus layer's segregation of the microbiome and provoke low-grade inflammation in mice; a human micro-dose trial showed mucus-erosion signals (fairly well established · **animal causation strong, human dose extrapolation undecided**); UPF intake is observationally consistent with lower diversity and a pro-inflammatory profile (still being studied) [L3:B4 MODEL/INFERENCE].

**Summary**: the priority order = fermented food (confirmed · single trial) > enough fiber of all three kinds (mechanism + observation) > varied polyphenols (mechanism) > keep ultra-processed food down (strong in animals + observation pointing the same way).

## Chapter 3. The gut-brain axis: microbes and mood

**Learning objectives**: be able to dismantle the "take probiotics to feed your brain serotonin" leap; be able to state the gap between animal and human evidence.

### 3.1 The serotonin figure: about 90% made in the gut, but it cannot enter the brain

About **90-95% of the body's serotonin is produced in the gastrointestinal tract** — a widely cited order-of-magnitude estimate (fairly well established · order-of-magnitude estimate) [L3:C1 MODEL]. But peripheral serotonin **does not cross the blood-brain barrier** (the gatekeeping structure between the blood and the brain's inner environment): gut-derived serotonin acts on gut motility, platelets, and immunity, and is a separate compartment from the brain's own serotonin (confirmed by research · barrier mechanism) [L3:C1 FACT]. So "take probiotics / feed your microbiome = directly top up brain serotonin" is a marketing leap; the microbiome influences mood through **indirect routes** (vagal / immune / metabolite / hormone axes).

### 3.2 The evidence gradient: strong in animals, weak in humans

Anxiety-like behavior in germ-free mice can be transferred by microbiome transplantation; specific strains influence brain activity and stress hormones via the vagus nerve — **animal-grade causation** (fairly well established · animal) [L3:C2 MODEL]. On the human side, the evidence for "microbiome → depression/anxiety" consists mostly of **observational associations**, and reports of microbiome differences in people with depression show poor reproducibility and inconsistent directions (still being studied / still contested in the field) [L3:C2 INFERENCE/CONTESTED]. Whether a "depressed-type" microbiome can make a healthy person's mood worse — not known (unethical to run, so it will not be done) [L3:C2].

### 3.3 Probiotics × depression: an adjunct with a small effect

A meta-analysis (2023, adjunctive treatment of major depression): well tolerated; the depression-scale improvement is a **small effect**, with large heterogeneity between studies and inconsistent strain combinations (confirmed by research · meta-analysis · small effect) [L3:C3 FACT]. Boundaries: only an **adjunct to standard treatment**; no evidence as standalone monotherapy; effect size ≈ a light behavioral intervention [L3:C3]. There is also the downward spiral (still being studied): low mood → worse eating → microbiome shift + inflammation ↑ → more fatigue and gloom — the lever that breaks the cycle is diet and behavior, not adding microbes back [L3:C2 INFERENCE].

**Summary**: the 90% gut-derived serotonin cannot enter the brain; animal causation holds, human causation is open; probiotics for depression are a small-effect adjunct.

## Chapter 4. Immunity · weight · sleep

**Learning objectives**: know where the evidence for "the microbiome trains immunity" sits; know that FMT's animal stories must not be extrapolated to humans.

### 4.1 Immunity and allergy

About **70-80% of the body's immune cells sit in the gut-associated lymphoid tissue** (widely cited framing · fairly well established) [L3:D1 MODEL]. The microbiome trains the early immune system (regulatory T cell differentiation, IgA antibody induction, barrier education); early-life colonization perturbations (cesarean delivery / early antibiotics) are associated, in observational studies, with childhood allergy and asthma risk (still being studied · human observation; animal causation holds) [L3:D1 INFERENCE/MODEL]. Some eczema-prevention trials came back negative — the evidence is mixed (still contested in the field) [L3:D1]. Naming the microbiome the "cause" of autoimmune disease lacks sufficient evidence; molecular mimicry / barrier leakage are mechanism candidates (fairly well established · candidate mechanisms) [L3:D1 MODEL/INFERENCE].

### 4.2 Weight and the honest boundaries of FMT

Germ-free mice given the microbiome of an "obese donor" gained more weight than those given a lean donor's — **causation holds within the animal model** (fairly well established · animal) [L3:D2 MODEL]. Human fecal microbiota transplantation for metabolic syndrome: small controlled trials showed short-term improvement in insulin sensitivity (some trials), with little effect on weight and **effects that fade over time** — donor microbes fail to colonize the recipient long-term (colonization resistance) (confirmed by research · single trial) [L3:D2 FACT→比较确定]. **Works in mice ≠ works in humans; FMT is not a weight-loss tool.**

### 4.3 Sleep and circadian two-way feedback

Microbiome composition shows circadian oscillation; host rhythm disruption (shift work / jet lag) perturbs the microbiome; acute sleep deprivation aggravates inflammation via the gut-brain axis; the microbiome participates in sleep regulation through short-chain fatty acids, tryptophan metabolism, and more (fairly well established · mechanism with animal / small-sample evidence) [L3:D3 MODEL]. The practical feedback loop: **irregular eating, especially late-night eating**, harms both your rhythm and your microbes (still being studied · same direction as the chrononutrition conclusions) [L3:D3 INFERENCE].

**Summary**: immune training happens in the early window; do not count on transplants for weight loss; eating at regular hours is setting the clock for your microbes and your body clock at the same time.

## Chapter 5. Probiotics / prebiotics / postbiotics: the graded buying guide

**Learning objectives**: be able to filter marketing with the "strain-level" iron law; know which scenarios have evidence and which are paying money for comfort.

### 5.1 The iron law: effects are strain-level

Probiotic effects are **strain-level** properties: genus-level/dose marketing such as "contains Lactobacillus" or "tens of billions of CFU" **does not constitute evidence for any indication**; different strains of the same species can have opposite effects; a CFU number only means something in the context of "that strain, at that trial's dose" (confirmed by research · trial-design logic) [L3:E FACT].

### 5.2 The grading table (Table 5-1)

| Grade | Scenario | Intervention | Evidence and tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| A-/B+ | **preventing** antibiotic-associated diarrhea | Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-745, LGG (the Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG strain), among others | meta-analyses positive (confirmed by research) [L3:E] |
| B | acute infectious diarrhea in children (treating an episode that has occurred) | Saccharomyces boulardii CNCM I-745 | supported by RCTs for particular strains (confirmed by research · strain-specific) [L3:E] |
| D (negative) | treating acute gastroenteritis that has already occurred in children | LGG | high-quality RCT **no benefit** — coexisting with the row above = strain-specificity is the honest conclusion (confirmed by research) [L3:E] |
| B- | adjunctive treatment of major depression | multi-strain combinations | meta-analysis · small effect (confirmed by research) [L3:C3/E] |
| B | prebiotics (inulin/GOS/FOS) | short-chain-fatty-acid-directed | mechanism + surrogate-marker support, weak hard outcomes (fairly well established) [L3:E MODEL] |
| C | postbiotics (inactivated microbes / metabolites) | — | early evidence, definitions immature (not known / early) [L3:E UNKNOWN] |
| Not recommended | FMT for irritable bowel syndrome / inflammatory bowel disease / weight loss | — | authoritative guidelines: recommend only for recurrent C. difficile; for IBD/IBS **recommend against** (confirmed by research · guidelines) [L3:E/F1] |

### 5.3 The care-seeking boundary (the medical-safety framework)

**Severe** infectious diarrhea (high fever / bloody stool / dehydration) and the **acute phase of inflammatory bowel disease**: seek medical care first; this book provides no self-management protocol — this item is a clinical safety consensus, not listed in L3 as a standalone assertion, and is handled as a safety boundary. Probiotics are no substitute for seeing a doctor. If you are immunocompromised, have heart valve disease, or carry a deep venous catheter, ask a doctor before using any probiotic (clinical consensus) [L3:F2 MODEL].

**Summary**: before buying a supplement ask three things — which strain number, which indication, which trial dose; if the three questions have no answers, you are paying for comfort.

## Chapter 6. The risk side and testing marketing

**Learning objectives**: know the populations genuinely at risk from probiotics/FMT; know the testing "can / cannot" list.

### 6.1 FMT: a real death case (stated as it happened)

Reported in 2019: fecal material from the same donor caused bloodstream infections with drug-resistant E. coli in two immunosuppressed recipients, **1 of the two died**; regulators subsequently required donor screening and strengthened informed consent (confirmed by research) [L3:F1 FACT]. Guideline boundaries: contraindicated with bowel perforation/obstruction; not advised with severe immunosuppression; for milder indications, cautious use only for recurrent C. difficile (confirmed by research · guidelines) [L3:F1]. The donor's long-term risks (whether metabolic and psychiatric phenotypes can travel through the microbiome) — **not known** (in animals, some phenotypes are already proven transferable) [L3:F1 UNKNOWN].

### 6.2 The risk populations for probiotics

Bloodstream infections with Lactobacillus are rare but real; reported cases concentrate in people who are immunocompromised, in neonatal intensive care, with prosthetic heart valves, with deep venous catheters, and after dental procedures (fairly well established · case-report and case-series level) [L3:F2 MODEL]. In healthy adults, commonly used probiotics have a good safety record (confirmed by research · surveillance consensus) [L3:F2 FACT]. The real risk population for D-lactic acidosis is people with short bowel syndrome / bowel dilation / abnormal gut transit (confirmed by research · a recognized clinical entity) [L3:F3 FACT]; the case series on "brain fog after probiotics" in fact showed that D-lactic acidosis from ordinary doses in people with anatomically intact guts is **unproven** (fairly well established · single series) [L3:F3 MODEL]. People with short bowel / post-surgical anatomical abnormalities should use probiotics and large fermentable loads with caution [L3:F3].

### 6.3 Testing marketing: it can describe, it cannot prescribe

**Can**: describe your current stool microbiome profile — a snapshot description, usable as a research and taxon-level reference (confirmed by research · descriptive) [L3:F4 FACT]. **Cannot (currently)**: ① diagnose disease (no microbiome profile has guideline endorsement); ② precisely guide a "personalized diet prescription" (poor generalization across populations); ③ derived metrics such as "gut age / leaky-gut score / detox packages" have no clinical validation (fairly well established / still contested in the field) [L3:F4 MODEL/CONTESTED]. One snapshot ≠ dynamic function; treat every marketing word with suspicion.

**Summary**: transplantation is a medical procedure with a death precedent; supplement risks concentrate in specific populations; testing describes, it does not prescribe.

## Chapter 9. FAQ (10 questions)

1. **Should everyone take probiotics?** No. The evidence that healthy people benefit from routine use is thin; the evidence that exists is for specific scenarios and specific strains. Do not take them as a general "wellness" habit — take them for an indication [L3:E].
2. **Does supermarket yogurt count as a fermented food?** The refrigerated, genuinely fermented, live-culture kinds count; in shelf-stable pasteurized yogurt the strains are dead, though the metabolites remain. Read the ingredient list and the storage instructions [L3:B1].
3. **Are hundreds of billions of CFU stronger?** Not necessarily. A dose only means something in the context of "that strain, at that trial's dose"; piling up numbers out of thin air has no evidential value [L3:E].
4. **Can probiotics treat depression?** The most that can be said is "adjunct": a small improvement on depression scales on top of standard treatment (meta-analysis); no evidence as standalone monotherapy; effect size ≈ a light behavioral intervention [L3:C3].
5. **Is a gut test worth doing?** If you want the snapshot, it is fine to spend a little money; counting on it to diagnose disease or write a personalized meal plan — that is not currently possible; "gut age / leaky gut / detox" has no validation [L3:F4].
6. **While on antibiotics, should you add probiotics?** You may ask your doctor about Saccharomyces boulardii and the like to prevent antibiotic-associated diarrhea — the hardest-evidence scenario; after the course, recovery runs on time and diet [L3:E/A3].
7. **Do cesarean-delivered babies need microbial supplementation?** The colonization difference is confirmed, but "whether and how to intervene" is not listed standalone in L3 — do not buy preparations on your own; follow pediatric follow-up care (the honest tier: not known) [L3:A3].
8. **Can you lose weight with microbiome transplants?** No. It holds in mice, not in humans: the effect is small and fades; transplantation also has a death case on record and is used only in medical settings [L3:D2/F1].
9. **Should you buy prebiotics such as inulin?** Food first: legumes, oats, and onions naturally contain the same components; buying preparations falls under "mechanism + surrogate-marker support, weak hard outcomes" (fairly well established). Start inulin at 3-5 g to prevent gas [L3:E/B2].
10. **How do you recognize microbiome marketing talk?** Treat these words as alarms on sight: gut age, leaky gut, detox, hundreds of billions of CFU, "everyone should take it," a single "miracle microbe." Check the grading table and the tier before paying [L3:E/F4].

## Chapter 10. Self-test (8 questions · with answers)

1. The findings and the three boundaries of the 10-week fermented-food trial? **Answer**: diversity up, 4/19 inflammatory markers down; single trial, small sample, surrogate markers; "fermented beats fiber" is not a conclusion (the baseline decides the response). [L3:B1]
2. What role does each of the three fiber types play? Give one example each. **Answer**: soluble fermentable fiber yields short-chain fatty acids (oats / legumes); insoluble fiber promotes motility (wheat bran); resistant starch is strongly butyrate-directed (cooled rice/potatoes). [L3:B2]
3. Why does "90% of serotonin is made in the gut" not imply "take probiotics to feed your brain"? **Answer**: peripheral serotonin does not cross the blood-brain barrier; gut-derived serotonin acts on gut motility / platelets / immunity; the microbiome influences mood through indirect routes. [L3:C1]
4. In the probiotic grading table, which row is a negative result? **Answer**: LGG for treating acute gastroenteritis that has already occurred in children — a high-quality RCT showed no benefit; coexisting with "particular strains shorten the course" = strain-specificity. [L3:E]
5. FMT's guideline indication and the fatal lesson? **Answer**: recommended only for recurrent C. difficile; not used for IBD/IBS; in 2019 a drug-resistant organism transmitted via a donor caused 1 death → donor screening + informed consent. [L3:F1/E]
6. What microbiome recovery after antibiotics actually looks like? **Answer**: most species return within 1.5-180 days; in a single trial 9 common species were undetected at 180 days and some genera showed signals of permanent loss (n=12). [L3:A3]
7. Why is alpha diversity not a treatment target? **Answer**: its link to better metabolic/inflammatory markers is an observational association; pursuing diversity for its own sake has not been shown in RCTs to improve clinical outcomes. [L3:A2]
8. Who must ask a doctor before using probiotics? **Answer**: people who are immunocompromised, have endocarditis / prosthetic valves, or carry central venous catheters (the bacteremia case populations); people with short bowel / anatomical gut abnormalities additionally carry the D-lactate risk. [L3:F2/F3]
