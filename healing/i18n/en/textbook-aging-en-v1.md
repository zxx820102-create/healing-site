# The Aging Textbook v1 — An Honest Ledger of Lifespan

> **Readers and how to use this book**: self-learners and health workers, read systematically from Chapter 1; if you only want to act right away, Chapter 0 alone is enough. What sets this volume apart from the other volumes is one honest cornerstone: **to date, the hard evidence for extending human lifespan is zero** — so the whole book holds the word "confirmed" tight: every conclusion carries a confidence tag, and the section-end notes lead back to the evidence-layer canon to check the original wording and the citation numbers.

## Chapter 0. One-page action version (L1)

> The full one-pager lives at `docs/healing/textbook/aging-l1-80yi-20260912.md`; key points below.

**In one sentence**: no supplement, drug, or way of eating has been proven to extend human lifespan (this point is confirmed); what is worth the effort are the cheap things — move, sleep, get the diet right.

**Seven action cards**: ① movement is first in value for money: aerobic + resistance, with grip strength and gait speed trained up (confirmed) ② protein as a floor for older adults: 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day when healthy, above 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day when ill, 25–30 g per meal (guideline consensus) ③ older adults, don't starve yourselves: extreme undereating = the triple risk of sarcopenia + osteoporosis + weakened immunity (confirmed) ④ midlife calorie control: a 10–15% small deficit is enough — the two-year trial shows it is doable without losing muscle strength (confirmed); whether it extends lifespan is not known ⑤ the cheapest aging check-up = grip strength + gait speed + cardiorespiratory fitness (fairly well established) ⑥ three blood tests along the way of a routine draw: an inflammation marker, glycated hemoglobin, lipoprotein(a); don't buy "aging panels" (confirmed) ⑦ no smoking, less alcohol: alcohol wrecks sleep — see the Sleep volume; smoking is not separately itemized in this volume's ledger.

**Myth quick-check**: supplements extend lifespan (endpoints unmoved) ｜ young-blood transfusion (no human data) ｜ the fewer the calories, the longer the life (reversed in older adults) ｜ biological age can be measured and reversed (a moving number ≠ a younger person) ｜ rapamycin and metformin as wonder drugs (not known in humans) ｜ home sauna rooms (the evidence tier does not justify a large outlay).

## Chapter 1. The honest cornerstone: hard evidence for human lifespan extension is zero

**Learning objectives**: understand why every "lifespan-extension" claim must pass the same gate; learn to recognize the "surrogate marker" sales pattern; master the four plain-language confidence tiers used throughout the book.

### 1.1 The gate every claim must pass

First, one fact — the foundation of this volume: **to date, no intervention — eating less, drugs, supplements — has ever completed a randomized controlled trial in humans with "death or a hard aging outcome" as the endpoint**. The two-year human calorie-restriction trial (CALERIE) had no death endpoint; the famous metformin anti-aging trial (TAME) is still at the "seeking evaluation" stage and has not been completed; all anti-aging drugs exist in humans only as small-sample, short-term, surrogate-marker-level studies. [L3:F2][L3:B1][L3:B6]

This is not "science failing" — lifespan research is inherently hard: people live for decades, trials run for a few years, and no one can randomize the rest of a person's life by command. So the honest question was never "can this thing extend lifespan" (nobody knows) but "at which layer has it been demonstrated in humans". [L3:F2]

### 1.2 The surrogate-marker trap: the blood marker moved, the person didn't

A **surrogate marker** is a "stand-in actor": what we actually want to know is "more years lived, fewer years sick" (hard outcomes), but what the experiment measures is "some molecule in the blood went up, some score went down". Can a stand-in replace the lead? This volume's most important case is NAD precursor supplements (NR/NMN): by mouth they do raise whole-blood NAD+ to about 2-fold (confirmed by research), but in a 12-week phase II randomized trial the primary endpoints — cognitive function, whole-brain blood flow, blood pressure — showed no improvement at all (confirmed by research). The blood marker moved, the endpoint did not — this is the standard opening of the commercial pipeline "surrogate marker proven → clinical benefit unproven → sold to healthy people". [L3:B7][L3:E1]

The same trap runs through the whole volume: commercial telomere-length testing, oxidative-stress panels (predictive increment unproven); epigenetic-clock "reversal" claims (detailed in Chapter 4); "young-blood transfusion" (a signal in animals, zero data in humans). The recognition rule is one sentence: **ask for the endpoint, not the stand-in**. [L3:C3][L3:C1][L3:B9]

### 1.3 Species stratification: a mouse's lifespan extension is not a human's

Mice started on rapamycin even in midlife live longer — the hardest anti-aging evidence at the mammal level (confirmed by research · animal). Between "works in mice" and "how should a human take it, how much, how safe is it" lie three unresolved gaps: dose translation, the side-effect spectrum, and decades of follow-up (not known). This volume's iron rule: **animal evidence is never automatically extrapolated to humans**; at "experiments prove lifespan extension", first ask "which experiment, which species". [L3:B5][L3:A]

### 1.4 Glossary (Table 1 · definitions first, development after)

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| Hard outcome | Endpoints such as death or major disease that need no interpreting; the opposite of a "stand-in" |
| Surrogate marker | An intermediate measurement used in place of a hard outcome (blood markers, scores, imaging) |
| Randomized controlled trial (RCT) | A rigorous trial that randomly assigns people to an intervention group and a control group |
| Caloric restriction (CR) | Long-term reduction of calorie intake on a nutritionally adequate basis |
| CALERIE | The first two-year human RCT of CR |
| Time-restricted eating (TRE / 16:8) | A light-fasting form that compresses the day's eating into a fixed window |
| Hallmarks of aging | Twelve cell- and molecular-level changes that describe "why we age" |
| Epigenetic clock | A research tool that estimates "biological age" from DNA methylation patterns |
| DunedinPACE | A clock metric that measures "the pace of aging" |
| mTOR | One of the master switches of cell growth; rapamycin inhibits it |
| Rapamycin | An immunosuppressive prescription drug; the strongest animal lifespan-extension evidence |
| Metformin | A glucose-lowering prescription drug; its anti-aging use is unconfirmed by hard trials |
| TAME | The metformin anti-aging clinical trial; still unfinished |
| NAD+/NR/NMN | A cellular energy-metabolism molecule and its oral precursor supplements |
| Senolytics | Drugs that clear "zombie cells"; no hard-outcome data in humans |
| Autophagy | The mechanism by which a cell recycles its worn-out parts |
| Telomere | The protective cap at the end of a chromosome, worn down with each division |
| Chronic low-grade inflammation | A low-fever-like inflammatory state smoldering year-round in the older body |
| Sarcopenia | The disability-prone loss of muscle mass and strength with age |
| Anabolic resistance | The blunted muscle-building response of older muscle to protein/exercise |
| Grip strength / gait speed / VO2max | The three cheapest measurements of cardiorespiratory and muscular fitness |
| hsCRP / HbA1c / Lp(a) | Three blood markers: inflammation, average blood sugar, inherited cardiovascular risk |
| Lean body mass | The part of body weight that is not fat — mostly muscle |

**Summary**: ask for the endpoint, not the stand-in; do not extrapolate from animals; treat every "extends lifespan by N years" as an extrapolation.

## Chapter 2. Why we age: the twelve hallmarks are a map, not a check-up sheet

**Learning objectives**: know what the explanatory framework of aging research looks like, what it can be used for, and what it cannot.

### 2.1 The framework itself

The 2023 formal edition proposed twelve "hallmarks of aging" with three inclusion criteria: it emerges with age; aggravating it in the laboratory accelerates aging; intervening on it can slow aging (reversible in animals). The framework itself is reliable academic consensus (confirmed by research). The twelve in one sentence: DNA repairs never finish, the chromosome protective caps wear shorter with use, the gene-switch atlas drifts, worn-out proteins go uncollected, the cellular recycling plant grinds to a halt, the nutrient-sensing accelerator and brake fail, the power plants age, "zombie cells" neither move out nor stop leaking poison, the reserve army that replaces worn parts runs dry, the neighborhood chatter goes wrong, low-grade inflammation smolders year-round, and the gut-flora product spectrum turns for the worse. [L3:A]

### 2.2 How to read this map (Table 2)

| Reading | Right or not |
|---|---|
| As an explanatory skeleton of "why humans age" | Right (confirmed by research · framework level) |
| As a research map of intervention targets | Right: every intervention in Chapter 3 maps back to a few of the hallmarks |
| As a check-up sheet where "measuring a few hallmarks = knowing how old you are" | Not right (fairly well established · the field's own critique) |
| Believing "reverse one hallmark = reverse aging" | Unproven in humans (not known) |

Two honest footnotes: animal and human evidence differ widely on every hallmark (for example, clearing "zombie cells" improves function in mice, while humans have only small pilots); and the academic community's own critique of the framework has reached the major journals — hallmarks are context-dependent regulatory mechanisms that do not always fit disease definitions, and any hallmark that fails the three criteria should be delisted. [L3:A]

**Summary**: the map is useful, but treating the map as a check-up sheet and treating animals as yourself are the two errors this chapter guards against.

## Chapter 3. The tiered intervention ledger: who sits at which evidence tier

**Learning objectives**: line the common "anti-aging methods" up into a one-page ledger by evidence tier; know each item's confirmed face, fairly-well-established face, and not-known face.

### 3.1 Exercise: first place in the ledger

The observational evidence points one way with a clear dose-response across populations: the higher the cardiorespiratory fitness, the lower the all-cause mortality (fairly well established · strong association); at the randomized-trial level, aerobic training does raise cardiorespiratory fitness (confirmed by research), and resistance exercise with protein improves gait speed and grip strength in older adults (confirmed by research · pooled level). The canon's characterization: "the anti-aging intervention with the best evidence-per-cost at present". For concrete training methods and strength prescriptions, see the Move volume `docs/healing/textbook/move-textbook-v1-20260912.md`. [L3:B3]

### 3.2 Sleep: the nearly free anti-aging axis

Sleep duration/quality shows a U-shaped association with all-cause mortality (fairly well established · association level); the mechanisms and prescriptions are not repeated in this volume — see the Sleep volume `docs/healing/textbook/sleep-textbook-v1-20260912.md` — with one item of direct relevance to older adults: hypnotic drugs raise the risk of falls and fractures in older adults (confirmed by research). [L3:B4]

### 3.3 Caloric restriction: the strongest human evidence; the lifespan endpoint is still not known

The fact face of the two-year strict trial (CALERIE): non-obese adults sustained about a 15% calorie deficit, lost about 8 kg by 12 months, kept micronutrients adequate, and lost no muscle strength (confirmed by research); metabolic surrogate markers improved, but the effect receded with adherence (confirmed by research). The effect on lifespan itself — not known (no death endpoint; the animal 40% restriction cannot be extrapolated). The actionable tier is a 10–15% moderate deficit + nutrition quality first, and only for non-obese adults; older adults and low-body-weight people are the reverse case (Chapter 5). Money to save: extreme-restriction boot camps, "fasting meal-replacement packs", subscription apps with no adherence validation — the real cost of CR is a food scale plus patience. [L3:B1]

### 3.4 Fasting windows add no extra magic

Put calorie restriction inside a 16:8 window and the weight loss comes from spontaneously eating less; isocalorically there is no difference from ordinary calorie restriction (confirmed by research); the effect of light fasting on human lifespan is not known. The key in the older direction was never "fast harder" but protein timing — see Chapter 5. [L3:B2]

### 3.5 The prescription-drug twin stars: the animal king and the unfinished trial

Rapamycin: started in midlife mice, it extended median and maximum lifespan in both sexes, replicated across centers (confirmed by research · animal); humans have only small-sample immune-surrogate-endpoint trials, no aging-outcome RCT (not known); the side effects are real — mouth ulcers, immunosuppression, glucose and lipid perturbations, delayed wound healing — and no dosing regimen has human consensus. Metformin: the molecular mechanism holds (fairly well established), but its anti-aging trial TAME remains unfinished, and how the observational benefit in people with diabetes extrapolates to people without diabetes is not known; the side effects of taking it on your own (B12 deficiency, gastrointestinal trouble) are, by contrast, confirmed. The shared trap of the two drugs: reseller-purchased prescription drugs, self-set doses, unmonitored "longevity clinics". [L3:B5][L3:B6][L3:E3]

### 3.6 NAD precursors and IV infusions: this volume's clearest "not worth it"

That oral NR raises blood NAD+ about 2-fold is true, but the main clinical endpoints do not move (confirmed by research); benefit in healthy people is not known; IV NAD infusion lacks clinical validation. Tens to hundreds of US dollars a month to buy one blood number, several hundred US dollars per visit for a drip — the clearest consumer case of the "surrogate-marker trap". [L3:B7][L3:E1]

### 3.7 Sauna and the candidate list

Sauna's associative evidence comes from a Finnish cohort of middle-aged men followed for about 28 years: high-frequency users had lower all-cause and cardiovascular death (fairly well established · self-reported frequency, one sex, one country); that a 30-minute sauna mobilizes immune cells is a real physiological event, but that is not outcome evidence (fairly well established). A high-end home sauna = staking a large capital outlay on associative evidence; a gym sauna already covers the actionable face. The remaining candidates, one line each: senolytics, spermidine, epigenetic reprogramming — hard human data are all zero (not known). One more caution: longevity interventions often act differently in the two sexes, so any "effective for the average person" claim deserves suspicion (fairly well established). [L3:B8][L3:B9]

| Intervention | Strongest evidence tier | Action for your person |

## Chapter 4. What to measure: the surrogate-marker trap and the cheap trio

**Learning objectives**: be able to choose tests — know which measurements are cheap and strong, and which are expensive and unclear.

### 4.1 The "biological age" clock: a research gold standard, not yet a decision tool

The epigenetic clock is today's most mature quantification tool for biological age, and it correlates with death-risk prediction (fairly well established). But one key limitation has been empirically demonstrated: **the clock responds dynamically** — a 12-week dietary intervention can move the "pace of aging" trajectory (confirmed by research). That means "the clock's number got smaller" cannot tell "genuinely younger" from "short-term metabolic adaptation". Academic critique has reached the major journals: the clock captures molecular signatures, and its explanatory power over disease and intervenability is context-dependent (fairly well established). Practical tier: about US$200–500 self-paid; fit only for baseline tracking at multi-year intervals — not a one-shot decision basis, and still less a reason to buy supplements to "lower the clock". [L3:C1][L3:E1]

### 4.2 The cheap strong-predictor trio

Grip strength: confirmed across large cohorts in 17 countries as a simple stratifier of cardiovascular death (fairly well established · strong); gait speed: a pooled analysis of more than 30,000 people shows strong stratification of expected survival, and improvement in gait speed within a year itself predicts better 8-year survival (fairly well established · strong) — being "improvable" is what turns it from fortune-telling into an action signal; VO2max: the measurable hard metric of fitness (watch estimates are proxies). One line: a grip dynamometer at the hundred-yuan level + a phone stopwatch over a 4-meter walk + yearly cardiorespiratory fitness — better value than any expensive "biological age" test (fairly well established · inference). [L3:C2]

### 4.3 Blood tests already in passing, and the panels not recommended

hsCRP (the chronic low-grade inflammation reading), HbA1c (the metabolic axis), Lp(a) (genetically determined; one test stratifies risk for life) — all within reach of a routine check-up (confirmed by research · testing consensus). Not recommended: commercial telomere-length testing, oxidative-stress panels, and other unvalidated "aging panels" (predictive increment unproven). [L3:C3]

### 4.4 Decision tree (Figure 4): the three cheap things you can do now

```
[Ask: which tier are you in?]

│   ├─ Second thing: start resistance + aerobic, aiming at grip strength and gait speed
```

## Chapter 5. For older adults: the protein floor and the sarcopenia defense line

**Learning objectives**: master the chapter with this volume's most practical action value — how older adults should eat, how to train, and what not to do.

### 5.1 Flip the protein-ceiling mindset into a protein-floor mindset

When young people discuss protein, the question is "could it be too much"; the older-adult question is exactly the opposite: older muscle carries **anabolic resistance** — the same meal builds muscle less efficiently, so each meal needs about 25–30 g of protein to cross the "anabolic threshold"; 1.0–1.2 g per kg of body weight per day in good health, raised above 1.2–1.5 g in stress states such as illness, hospitalization, or injury (guideline consensus; part of the upper-range figures follow the guideline body text, verified at abstract level). Muscle and bone are a coupled unit — mechanical and endocrine — and protein props up both (confirmed by research). [L3:B2][L3:D]

### 5.2 Reverse restriction for older adults: the explicit prohibition list

Calorie restriction and fasting patterns applied to older adults = the triple risk of sarcopenia + bone density + immunity (confirmed by research · each evidence form as flagged in the canon); for an older adult of low body weight, "one more bite restricted" is the wrong direction; an older adult at BMI 22–28 need not chase calorie restriction (fairly well established); one-meal-a-day OMAD collides head-on with the per-meal protein threshold and is prohibited for older adults and anyone at sarcopenia risk (guideline-level). [L3:E2][L3:B2]

### 5.3 New risks in the era of weight-loss drugs

Weight-loss drugs (the GLP-1 class) come with lean-mass loss; older adults, people with low muscle mass, and rapid weight-losers are the high-risk groups; the prevention trio = baseline body-composition assessment + resistance exercise + adequate protein (confirmed by research · review level). Caloric restriction's own muscle signal was already visible in the two-year trial — slight leg lean-mass loss with no loss of muscle strength; young adults can bear it, **in older adults the same method magnifies the risk** (confirmed by research → the older-adult extrapolation is model inference). [L3:D]

### 5.4 The medical boundary

Older adults with multiple coexisting conditions often take several prescription drugs at once; any change in diet structure, exercise intensity, or fasting arrangement can alter drug effects and fall risk — **ask the doctor first, then act**; the canon's side-effect face (self-prescribed drugs, unmonitored longevity clinics) applies to older adults especially. What this volume offers is an evidence map, not a prescription. [L3:E3][L3:F4]

**Summary**: the whole older-adult chapter compresses into one sentence — protein as the floor, resistance training to keep muscle, no reverse restriction, and medications as your doctor directs.

## Chapter 6. The spending guide: how to recognize a market bubble

**Learning objectives**: you need not memorize every product — only one pattern.

The bubble's standard pipeline: **surrogate marker proven → clinical benefit unproven → sold to healthy people**. Three documented cases: NAD precursors (the blood marker moves, the endpoint doesn't); IV NAD clinics (zero validation); the "reverse your biological age" literature disaster zone (mostly small samples, no controls, the endpoint being the clock's number rather than an outcome). Structural backdrop: the testing and supplement industries share the "biological age" narrative, and even the industry's own reviews have independence-of-interest problems (fairly well established · self-flagged in the canon). Three rules: animal models ≠ human facts; surrogate markers ≠ outcomes; single-arm uncontrolled ≠ effective. The anti-aging "don't spend" list: NAD supplements and infusions, commercial telomere testing, home-sauna capital outlays, reseller-purchased prescription drugs, unmonitored longevity-clinic packages. For the universal supplement-sieving method, see the Supplement Truth volume `docs/healing/textbook/supplement-textbook-v1-20260912.md`. [L3:E1][L3:C1]

## Chapter 7. Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

1. **So is there any way at all to live longer?** A direction, not a number. Exercise, sleep, protein, and moderate calorie management each have support at their own tier, and the evidence for healthspan (years without disability) is stronger than for absolute lifespan; "extends lifespan by N years" is all extrapolation. [L3:F1][L3:F2]
2. **Is it really true that not a single human lifespan-extension trial exists?** RCTs with death or a hard aging outcome as the endpoint are zero to date: CALERIE had no death endpoint and TAME is unfinished — this is the canon's settled finding, not this book being conservative. [L3:F2]
3. **NAD supplements are so popular — are they really useless?** Raising blood NAD+ is real (confirmed); the cognitive, cerebral-blood-flow, and blood-pressure clinical endpoints don't move (confirmed); benefit in healthy people is not known. "Paying money for a blood marker" is the most accurate description. [L3:B7]
4. **My "biological age" tested younger than my real age — should I be happy?** A reference point, not an achievement. The clock's number responds to short-term diet and cannot tell true youthfulness from metabolic adaptation; still less should you buy supplements to "lower the clock". [L3:C1]
5. **Can older adults do 16:8 light fasting?** Not recommended. It conflicts with the 25–30 g per-meal protein threshold, and older age = the triple risk of muscle + bone + immunity; for low-body-weight older adults it is an explicitly wrong direction. [L3:B2][L3:E2]
6. **How much protein does an older adult need? How to split it?** 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day (healthy) up to above 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day (illness/stress), split across three meals, 25–30 g per meal. Special conditions such as kidney disease are individualized by the doctor. [L3:B2][L3:D]
7. **What should an older adult on weight-loss drugs watch out for?** Lean-mass loss — the trio of baseline body-composition assessment + resistance exercise + adequate protein; older adults are a high-risk group. [L3:D]
8. **Many people online take rapamycin — should I follow?** Don't. The animal king is real; human dose, safety, and effect are all not known, and the side effects (immunosuppression, glucose and lipids, wound healing) are real; buying and dosing a prescription drug on your own is gambling on the not-known. [L3:B5][L3:E3]
9. **Is a sauna worth keeping up?** Worth it in passing, not worth buying equipment for. The associative evidence comes from a Finnish cohort of middle-aged men; a gym sauna already covers the actionable face, and a high-end home sauna is a large outlay staked at the "fairly well established" tier. [L3:B8]
10. **How do I self-test grip strength and gait speed?** Grip dynamometer (hundred-yuan level), measure the maximum per the manual; 4-meter usual gait speed with a phone stopwatch; VO2max from the yearly check-up or an exercise laboratory. The point is not any single reading but the year-over-year trend and "can it improve". [L3:C2]
11. **What about "young-blood transfusion"?** A signal in animals, zero data in humans; the canon files it under "prefer an empty cell to an invented one" — any claim by commercial operations has no human basis. [L3:B9]
12. **My parents have multiple chronic conditions and take seven or eight medications — does this book apply to them?** The principles apply (protein, muscle training to prevent falls), but dose, intensity, and diet-structure changes must go through their doctors — multiple conditions plus multiple medications is this book's explicit medical boundary. [L3:E3]

3. Why can't "the clock's number got smaller" prove a person got younger? **Answer**: the clock responds dynamically; a 12-week dietary intervention moves the number, and true rejuvenation cannot be told apart from short-term metabolic adaptation. [L3:C1]
4. The two iron rules for using animal evidence? **Answer**: animal models are not automatically extrapolated to humans; at "experiments prove lifespan extension", first ask the species and the endpoint. [L3:A][L3:B5]
5. The three numbers of the older-adult protein prescription? **Answer**: healthy 1.0–1.2 g/kg/day; illness/stress above 1.2–1.5 g/kg/day; 25–30 g per meal to cross the anabolic threshold. [L3:B2]
6. Why is the older-adult stance "floor thinking"? **Answer**: anabolic resistance — the same protein builds less muscle, and the cost of too little protein (sarcopenia, falls, disability) far outweighs the worry of "eating too much". [L3:B2][L3:D]
7. What is the cheap strong-predictor trio, and what does it beat? **Answer**: grip strength, gait speed, VO2max; better value than expensive biological-age tests, and gait-speed improvement itself predicts better survival. [L3:C2]
8. The bubble pipeline's three stages? **Answer**: surrogate marker proven → clinical benefit unproven → sold to healthy people. [L3:E1]
9. True or false: the fewer the calories, the longer the life. **Answer**: False. A 10–15% moderate deficit for non-obese adults has support; extreme restriction in older adults = the triple risk of sarcopenia + osteoporosis + weakened immunity, and low-body-weight older adults are the reverse case. [L3:B1][L3:E2]
10. The three boundaries for older adults with multiple conditions using this book? **Answer**: diet-structure changes, exercise intensity, and any fasting arrangement go through the doctor first; no self-purchasing or self-dosing of prescription drugs; this book is an evidence map, not a prescription.
