# The Sleep Textbook v1 — From Mechanism to Action

> **Readers and how to use this book**: self-learners and health workers, read systematically from here; if you only want to act right away, Chapter 0 alone is enough. Every conclusion in this textbook is tagged with a confidence level (the four plain-language tiers), and the section-end anchors lead back to the evidence-layer canon so you can check the original wording and citation numbers.

## Chapter 0. One-page action version (L1)

> The full one-pager lives at `docs/healing/textbook/sleep-l1-80yi-20260912.md`; key points below.

**In one sentence**: sleeping well rests on rhythm, not pills — anchor your wake-up time; for insomnia, behavioral therapy comes first; don't trust "sleep shortcuts."

**Seven action cards**: ① a fixed wake-up time matters more than a fixed bedtime — get light after waking, dim things down at night (guideline-recommended); ② the first step for insomnia is cognitive behavioral therapy, not sleeping pills: falling asleep about 19 minutes faster on average, about 26 fewer minutes awake at night, no adverse events reported across the trials (confirmed); ③ naps capped at 20–30 minutes: napping beyond 1 hour every day tracks with higher cardiovascular risk (confirmed), short naps are the steadier pattern (fairly well established); ④ melatonin is confirmed useful for jet lag and for late-running body clocks; as an ordinary sleeping pill its effect is small, and long-term self-dosing safety is not known; ⑤ stop caffeine by the afternoon: 400 mg drunk even 6 hours before bed still measurably disrupts sleep (confirmed); ⑥ alcohol is not a sleep aid: faster sleep onset is paid for with a shattered second half of the night and dream sleep cut back (confirmed); ⑦ finish dinner 3 hours before bed, and eat more vegetables and whole grains by day: more fiber, more deep sleep; high sugar and high fat, shallower sleep (fairly well established).

**Myth quick-check**: the 8-hour dogma (it is a range, not a prescription) ｜ alcohol as a sleep aid (a losing trade) ｜ counting sheep (the harder you try, the less you sleep) ｜ weekend catch-up sleep to repay the debt (= social jet lag) ｜ "older people sleep less, so it's fine" (insomnia should still be treated, and sleeping pills are more dangerous for older adults) ｜ turkey makes you sleepy (it's the overeating plus alcohol, not the tryptophan).

## Chapter 1. How sleep works (mechanisms)

**Learning objectives**: read the two systems behind "sleepiness" and the "body clock"; tell apart the sleep stages of a night; know which mechanisms are confirmed and which are still being studied.

### 1.1 Two systems decide when you get sleepy (the two-process model)

**Sleep pressure** works like an hourglass: while you are awake, a substance in the brain called **adenosine** keeps accumulating — the more it builds, the sleepier you get; during sleep it is cleared, and the hourglass resets. **The circadian rhythm** is like an internal clock: a "master clock" in the brain called the **suprachiasmatic nucleus** drives roughly 24-hour waves of alertness, so you wake when it is time to wake and grow sleepy when it is time to sleep. How sleepy you are, and when you sleep, is set jointly by the "hourglass level" and the "clock hand." This **two-process model** framework is broadly validated (confirmed by research); specific details still show deviations and open debate (fairly well established · details still being studied). [L3:SLEEP-A1]

### 1.2 The stages of a night: deep sleep first, dreaming later

After sleep onset you pass through light sleep, then enter **slow-wave sleep** (commonly "deep sleep" — brain waves large and slow), concentrated in the first half of the night; nighttime **growth hormone** secretion pulses are coupled to the deep-sleep phase — deep sleep is one of the main drivers of nighttime growth hormone, and this coupling shifts with age and hormonal state, weakening after menopause, for example (confirmed by research). [L3:SLEEP-A3] The second half of the night belongs to **REM sleep** (rapid eye movement sleep, the stage where dreaming is most concentrated). On memory: deep sleep supports the consolidation of declarative memory (memory of facts and events) — this direction is established (confirmed by research); as for REM's contribution to memory, the field itself admits it is "still searching" (still being studied); what REM's indispensable function is has no single consensus (not known). [L3:SLEEP-A2][L3:SLEEP-A4]

### 1.3 Glossary (Table 1 · definitions first, development after)

| Term | One-line definition |
|---|---|
| Sleep pressure | The drive that makes you sleepier the longer you stay awake; it dissipates during sleep |
| Adenosine | The representative signaling substance of sleep pressure; caffeine works by blocking it |
| Circadian rhythm | The body's roughly 24-hour physiological rhythm, driven by the body clock |
| Suprachiasmatic nucleus | The "master clock" in the brain; headquarters of the circadian rhythm |
| Two-process model | The model in which sleep pressure × circadian rhythm jointly determine sleep timing |
| Sleep-onset latency (SOL) | Time from lights-out to falling asleep |
| Sleep efficiency (SE) | The share of time in bed actually spent asleep |
| Wake after sleep onset (WASO) | Total time spent awake during the night after falling asleep |
| Slow-wave sleep (SWS / deep sleep) | The stage with deep, slow brain waves; first half of the night; handles repair and memory consolidation |
| REM sleep | Rapid eye movement sleep; dreaming at its most concentrated; second half of the night |
| Sleep fragmentation | Repeated brief awakenings that cut the night into pieces |
| CBT-I | Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia; first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults |
| Stimulus control | A CBT-I component: the bed is for sleep only; fixed wake-up time |
| Sleep restriction | A CBT-I component: compress time in bed to bank sleepiness before getting into it |
| Sleep hygiene | A set of routine-and-environment tips; weak evidence alone, not the same thing as CBT-I |
| Chronotype | The innate tendency to run early ("early bird") or late ("night owl") |
| Social jet lag | The mismatch between the body clock and social time (e.g., weekend catch-up sleep) |
| Melatonin | The body clock's signal hormone; effective for jet lag and for the late sleep phase |
| Delayed sleep-wake phase disorder (DSWPD) | A phase disorder in which the whole clock runs late — you want to sleep but can't |
| IARC Group 2A | An evidence-strength grade: "probably carcinogenic to humans" (not "will certainly cause cancer") |

**Summary**: sleepiness = sleep pressure + body clock combined; deep sleep handles repair and memory consolidation (confirmed); the function of the dreaming phase is still being studied (not known).

## Chapter 2. What to do about insomnia

**Learning objectives**: know what the first step for insomnia is, how large CBT-I's real effect is, and when sleeping pills enter the picture.

### 2.1 First line is CBT-I, not sleeping pills

**CBT-I** is a combination of methods: cognitive therapy (dismantling wrong beliefs about sleep) ＋ stimulus control ＋ sleep restriction ＋ sleep hygiene ＋ relaxation training — only a combination with at least three components counts as CBT-I; stand-alone "less screen time before bed" education does not (confirmed by research · the combination defines it). A pooled analysis of 20 randomized controlled trials, 1,162 people in total: sleep-onset latency shortened by 19.03 minutes on average, time awake at night reduced by 26.00 minutes, sleep efficiency up 9.91% (≈ about 47 more minutes of sleep per 8 hours in bed); total sleep time rose only 7.6 minutes and did not reach significance — it mainly repairs "falling asleep and sleeping solidly," it does not stretch sleep; and the benefit persists after treatment ends, with no adverse outcomes reported across all trials (confirmed by research). The American College of Physicians guideline lists CBT-I as the initial treatment for chronic insomnia in adults (guideline-recommended). [L3:SLEEP-B1]

**Decision tree (Figure 1)**: insomnia ≥3 months, ≥3 nights a week, daytime impact? → yes → **step one is CBT-I** (outpatient care / self-help books / digital therapeutics all qualify) → still heavy after 4–6 weeks → return visit: screen for other sleep disorders; assess whether short-term adjunctive medication is warranted (extra caution in older adults, see 2.3). → no (occasionally bad nights) → start with action cards 1/5/6/7 in Chapter 0; don't rush to treat it as a disease.

### 2.2 The harder you try, the less you sleep

Compulsive effort around "I must get X hours" itself induces insomnia anxiety (fairly well established · model layer) — that is why "counting sheep" and "computing how many hours of sleep are left" get worse the more you use them. The starting point of behavioral therapy is precisely letting go of the numbers and of the effort. [L3:SLEEP-B1][L3:canon-E4]

### 2.3 The other side of sleeping pills

Sedative-hypnotic use in older adults is associated with elevated fall and fracture risk (confirmed by research · multiple observational faces); dependence, tolerance, and rebound on discontinuation are clinical consensus (fairly well established). In controlled trials CBT-I reported no adverse outcomes — it is the only first line with no pharmacological side effects (confirmed by research). [L3:SLEEP-E3]

**Summary**: for insomnia choose CBT-I first; the effect size is "19 minutes to fall asleep, 26 minutes less awake at night" — real, and without side effects; sleeping pills are the older-adult fall-risk face, kept as an option after evaluation.

## Chapter 3. The day-to-day of sleeping well

**Learning objectives**: line up light, temperature, caffeine, alcohol, and food into a rhythm you can run day by day.

### 3.1 Light and temperature

Morning light anchors the body clock; dim things down at night (guideline-recommended). A warm bath or shower 1–2 hours before bed improves sleep (confirmed by research · a systematic review exists; the mechanism — the post-bath drop in core body temperature — is fairly well established). A cooler bedroom sleeps better (fairly well established · guideline level; no standalone large trial yet). [L3:SLEEP-B2a][L3:canon-B2]

### 3.2 Caffeine: even 6 hours is no guarantee

With 400 mg of caffeine taken at 0 / 3 / 6 hours before bed, all three doses objectively and significantly disrupt sleep — "a cup in the late afternoon is fine" does not hold; even 6 hours ahead is not safe (confirmed by research). And caffeine clearance speed varies from person to person (variation in CYP1A2 enzyme activity): "half-life about 5 hours" is only a population approximation (fairly well established). In practice: individualize your cutoff, starting by moving earlier than "6 hours before bed" (fairly well established · inference). [L3:SLEEP-B2b][L3:SLEEP-C5]

### 3.3 The full spectrum of alcohol (Table 2)

| Alcohol's effect | Spectrum | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Falling asleep faster (the source of the "sleep aid" illusion) | all doses | confirmed |
| First half of the night deeper, deep sleep paradoxically elevated | most marked at high doses | confirmed |
| Second half of the night chopped to pieces | all doses | confirmed |
| First episode of dream sleep delayed | most identifiable at all doses | confirmed |
| Nightly share of dream sleep reduced | medium-high doses | confirmed |

In one sentence: alcohol as a sleep aid = trading dream sleep and the integrity of the second half of the night for the speed of falling asleep (fairly well established · direct inference). [L3:SLEEP-C2][L3:SLEEP-B2c]

### 3.4 Eating and sleep run in both directions

Late eating (strictly isocaloric crossover trial): more hunger, lower energy expenditure and core temperature (confirmed by research); finishing dinner 3 hours before bed improves gastroesophageal reflux (confirmed by research). [L3:SLEEP-C1] What you eat also enters sleep: on the free-choice eating night there was less deep sleep and slower sleep onset than on the controlled-diet night; more fiber, more deep sleep; more saturated fat, less deep sleep; more sugar, more nighttime awakenings (confirmed by research — but the sample was 26 people for a single night; the boundary is honestly labeled). The reverse also holds: sleep restriction changes eating (confirmed by research · direction level). [L3:SLEEP-C3] "Protein / turkey for sleepiness": the tryptophan pathway mechanism exists, but at ordinary meal sizes the effect is tiny; the holiday drowsiness is meal size + alcohol (fairly well established; "turkey makes you sleepy" is an analogy run large — it does not count as evidence). [L3:SLEEP-C4]

**A day's timeline (Figure 2)**: fixed wake time → get light → stop caffeine in the early afternoon → dinner done 3 hours before bed, fiber-rich → warm bath 1–2 h before bed → dim light → fixed bed time (bed only when sleepy).

**Summary**: light governs rhythm; cool and baths govern falling asleep; caffeine governs "don't overestimate the safety window"; alcohol governs "don't trust the illusion"; food governs deep sleep.

## Chapter 4. The truth about supplements and drugs

**Learning objectives**: recognize which supplements have evidence for which purpose, and which are simply not known.

### 4.1 Grading table (Figure 3)

| Supplement | Used for | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| Melatonin | jet lag | confirmed · effective |
| Melatonin | late-running body clock (DSWPD) | confirmed · effective (guideline-recommended + trials) |
| Melatonin | ordinary primary insomnia | fairly well established · narrow effective face, small effect |
| Melatonin | long-term (months and beyond) self-dosing | not known · safety data insufficient |
| Magnesium | sleep aid | not known · no supportive positive-evidence synthesis found |
| Valerian | sleep aid | not known · evidence inconsistent |
| Glycine | subjective daytime performance in sleep-restricted people | fairly well established · small-sample positive signal only |

Principle: the "not known" tier = don't pay money while waiting for evidence, and never put children on long-term courses (long-term outcomes of melatonin in children are likewise not closed). [L3:SLEEP-B4a][L3:SLEEP-B4b][L3:SLEEP-E2]

### 4.2 Prescription sleeping pills

See 2.3: older-adult fall and fracture risk (confirmed), dependence and rebound (fairly well established); CBT-I is the only first line with no pharmacological side effects. [L3:SLEEP-E3]

**Summary**: melatonin is a "phase drug," not a "sleeping pill"; magnesium and valerian stay at "not known"; for diagnosed insomnia, CBT-I first.

## Chapter 5. Special populations

**Learning objectives**: the confirmed conclusions and honest boundaries for older adults, shift workers, and pregnancy.

### 5.1 Older adults

The deep-sleep coupling with growth hormone weakens with age (confirmed by research) — older adults' "lighter sleep" has a physiological basis; but "insomnia in old age needs no care" does not hold: insomnia should still be treated, and the first line is still CBT-I; sedative sleeping pills carry elevated fall and fracture risk in older adults (confirmed by research) — this is the complete answer to the Chapter 0 myth "older people sleeping less is fine." [L3:SLEEP-A3][L3:SLEEP-B1][L3:SLEEP-E3]

### 5.2 Shift work and night work

Night-shift work involving circadian disruption is classified by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) as Group 2A — "probably carcinogenic to humans." Note this is an **evidence-strength grading**, not "night shifts will give you cancer"; the mechanism candidates are melatonin suppression and rhythm disruption (the classification fact is confirmed; the mechanistic attribution is fairly well established). [L3:SLEEP-D3] "Social jet lag" — weekday sleep debt repaid on weekends — is associated with obesity indicators and depressive symptoms (confirmed by research · observational; causal direction not known). Being an early bird or a night owl has a genetic basis, but it is many genes with small effects — don't believe "a single gene decides your chronotype" (confirmed by research; the heritability figure is not known). [L3:SLEEP-D2][L3:SLEEP-D1]

### 5.3 Pregnancy and postpartum (honest boundary)

This textbook's evidence base (sleep-canon v1 · 24 assertions) does not cover intervention or medication conclusions for sleep during pregnancy — **that cell is currently "not known"; do not trust any claim that arrives without a labeled evidence source**; at a visit, defer to the in-person assessment of obstetrics and sleep-medicine specialists.

**Summary**: for older adults with insomnia, don't reach for pills first; the night-shift risk is a grading, not a verdict; for pregnancy — prefer an empty cell to an invented one.

## Chapter 6. Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

1. **Must I get a full 8 hours?** No. 8 hours is a population mean, not a prescription; the authoritative recommendation is a range (adults 7–9 hours). Anchor on "daytime function intact, waking naturally without an alarm." Deliberately sleeping 9+ hours "for wellness" has no evidence. [L3:SLEEP-F2][L3:SLEEP-E1]
2. **How does sleep duration relate to health?** Earlier large syntheses suggest a U-shaped association: both too-short and too-long sleep track with higher mortality risk (confirmed by research · association); but whether "long sleep leads to disease" or "disease leads to long sleep" cannot be told apart (not known). [L3:SLEEP-E1]
3. **Are there people who naturally need little sleep?** Yes, but extremely rare: carriers of certain familial gene mutations sleep under 6.5 hours a day without impairment (confirmed by research). That is a mutation, not a habit — it cannot be trained. [L3:SLEEP-F1]
4. **Does counting sheep help you fall asleep?** No. Compulsive sleep-onset effort itself worsens insomnia anxiety (fairly well established). Get up, do something boring in dim light, and return to bed when sleepy. [L3:canon-E4]
5. **So does alcohol as a sleep aid work or not?** No. Faster sleep onset is real; the price is a chopped-up second half of the night plus dream sleep delayed and reduced (confirmed by research). [L3:SLEEP-C2]
6. **Is melatonin a sleeping pill?** No. It is a phase signal: confirmed effective for jet lag and the late-running body clock; a small effect in ordinary insomnia; long-term self-dosing safety not known. [L3:SLEEP-B4a][L3:SLEEP-E2]
7. **Do magnesium tablets / valerian aid sleep?** Not known. No supportive evidence synthesis has been found — don't pay for "might help." [L3:SLEEP-B4b]
8. **How long should a nap be?** Capped at 20–30 minutes (fairly well established); napping beyond 1 hour every day is associated with elevated cardiovascular risk (confirmed by research · association; it may also be a marker of underlying health problems — causality not known). [L3:SLEEP-B3]
9. **After what time should I skip coffee?** Caffeine drunk even 6 hours before bed already disrupts sleep (confirmed by research); for slow metabolizers, earlier still. There is no single answer — start from "stop in the afternoon" and test. [L3:SLEEP-B2b][L3:SLEEP-C5]
10. **Does dreaming a lot mean I slept well?** Cannot be told apart. The function of the dreaming phase is itself still being studied (not known); don't grade sleep quality by how much you dream. [L3:SLEEP-A4]
11. **Can weekend catch-up sleep repay the debt?** Not fully. Weekday debt repaid on weekends = social jet lag, associated with obesity and depression (confirmed by research). [L3:SLEEP-D2]
12. **Is CBT-I the same thing as meditation and relaxation?** No. Relaxation is only one of CBT-I's five components; the effective ingredient is the multimodal combination (at least three). [L3:SLEEP-B1]

## Chapter 7. Self-test (10 questions · with answers)

1. The two systems that decide "when you get sleepy"? **Answer**: sleep pressure (adenosine accumulating, the hourglass) and the circadian rhythm (the body clock). [L3:SLEEP-A1]
2. The stage that dominates the first half of the night, and one confirmed function of it? **Answer**: slow-wave sleep / deep sleep; supports declarative memory consolidation and drives the nighttime growth-hormone pulses. [L3:SLEEP-A2][L3:SLEEP-A3]
3. The first-line treatment for chronic insomnia in adults, and its representative effect sizes? **Answer**: CBT-I; falling asleep about 19 minutes faster, about 26 minutes less awake at night, sleep efficiency +9.91%, no adverse outcomes reported. [L3:SLEEP-B1]
4. Why does "doing sleep hygiene alone" not equal CBT-I? **Answer**: CBT-I requires a combination of ≥3 components; hygiene education on its own has weak evidence. [L3:SLEEP-B1]
5. True or false: melatonin works for every kind of insomnia. **Answer**: false. The confirmed-effective faces are jet lag and the late-running body clock; the effect in ordinary insomnia is small; long-term self-dosing safety is not known. [L3:SLEEP-B4a][L3:SLEEP-E2]
6. What is alcohol's "trade" with sleep? **Answer**: falling asleep faster, exchanged for a broken second half of the night and dream sleep delayed and reduced. [L3:SLEEP-C2]
7. Why should the caffeine cutoff be individualized? **Answer**: clearance speed varies widely between people (enzyme variation), and "5 hours" is only a population approximation; besides, intake even 6 hours before bed is confirmed to disrupt sleep. [L3:SLEEP-C5][L3:SLEEP-B2b]
8. Where does "older people should sleep less, and insomnia needs no treatment" go wrong? **Answer**: the age-related decline in deep sleep is real, but insomnia should still be treated; sleeping pills raise older-adult fall and fracture risk, and the first line is still CBT-I. [L3:SLEEP-A3][L3:SLEEP-E3][L3:SLEEP-B1]
9. What is the U-shape in the nap evidence, and which part is still uncertain? **Answer**: long naps (≥60 minutes/day) are associated with elevated cardiovascular risk (RR 1.82); the 20–30-minute safety window is an inference; causal direction not known. [L3:SLEEP-B3]
10. IARC lists night-shift work as Group 2A — what is the correct reading? **Answer**: an evidence-strength grading of "probably carcinogenic to humans," not "night shifts necessarily cause cancer"; the mechanistic attribution remains a candidate model. [L3:SLEEP-D3]
