# Headache Canon v1 (English)

> **In one sentence**: Headache is the most common neurological disease on Earth — about 40% of people (3.1 billion) are currently troubled by some form of headache [FACT · WHO 2021 framing]. The three most common primary headaches (migraine, tension-type, cluster) are none of them a trivial "just endure it" matter — and none of them a "worsening pain = worsening brain" doom. Headache's two real sources of harm are, first, mistaking red flags for small things and delaying care, and second, treating painkillers as food and growing a "the more you take, the more it hurts" medication-overuse headache. This volume teaches you to read the three types, manage migraine, and recognize the red lines.
>
> **How to read this volume**: ① the typing face (one table separating migraine / tension-type / cluster) → ② the migraine deep face → ③ the medication-overuse headache (MOH) dedicated face → ④ the tension-type face → ⑤ the cluster face → ⑥ the red-flag chapter → ⑦ the falsification face.

## ① Typing face: one table separating migraine / tension-type / cluster

The three primary headaches are not told apart by "how fierce the pain is" but by this set of features: **location, quality, accompanying symptoms, duration, effect of activity** [FACT · all features taken from ICHD-3 and WHO official framings, verified item-by-item this round]:

| Discriminating feature | Migraine | Tension-type headache (TTH) | Cluster headache (CH) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Location** | Unilateral or bilateral · frontal-temporal most common | Bilateral · band-like distribution | Strictly unilateral · orbital/supraorbital/temporal (fixed to one side within a single bout) |
| **Quality** | Pulsating / throbbing | Pressing / tightening · "a band cinched around the head" | Extremely severe drilling/burning pain · "painful enough that the person cannot lie down and paces back and forth" |
| **Accompaniments** | Nausea/vomiting · photophobia and phonophobia · aura may occur | Usually no prominent accompaniments · neck/shoulder tightness may co-occur | Ipsilateral lacrimation · conjunctival injection · nasal congestion/rhinorrhea · eyelid drooping (ptosis) · facial sweating · agitation |
| **Duration** | 4–72 hours (adults) | 30 minutes to days (episodic hours common) | **15–180 minutes** · from every other day up to 8 attacks per day |
| **Effect of activity** | Worsened by, or avoidance of, routine activity (walking, stairs) | Usually not aggravated by activity | Not relieved by activity during the attack · attacks cluster at fixed times of day |
| **Population** | Far more women than men · heaviest burden in women of reproductive age and 15–49 | Similar in both sexes (WHO framing: women about 50% more than men) | Male predominance (ICHD-3 large clinical series about 3:1 · WHO 2021 framing 6:1) · onset mostly 20–40 |
| **Prevalence scale** | Global about **1.04 billion** (2016) [FACT · GBD] | Global about 1.89 billion · episodic TTH >70% in some populations [FACT] | <1/1000 adults · rare [FACT · WHO] |

**The two most common misreadings**:

1. **"Migraine = one-sided headache"** — a misreading. Of migraine's four ICHD-3 features, "unilateral location" is only one of the choose-two list; **unilaterality is not a necessary condition**. The ICHD-3 text itself states that in children and adolescents migraine is **more often bilateral**, with one-sided pain often appearing only in late adolescence or early adulthood [FACT · ICHD-3 1.1 Comments, verbatim]. Recognizing only "half-head pain" misses more than half of patients.
2. **"The worse the pain, the worse the disease"** — a misreading. Of the three types the **most intense is cluster** (ICHD-3's own text uses "excruciating"), yet it is the rarest; the tension-type headache with the largest prevalence is usually only mild-to-moderate. Intensity and "how much the disease matters" are not one line: **cluster is intense but brief · TTH is mild but widespread · migraine is moderate-to-severe and the most disabling** — GBD 2016 accounting: migraine's disability weight far exceeds TTH's, 45.1 million YLDs against 7.2 million [FACT · PMID 30353868].

## ② The migraine deep face: not a "personality problem" — a manageable episodic brain disease

### The four-phase spectrum (not everyone has every phase)

[FACT · the phase framework is the common ICHD-3/WHO clinical framing; "attack must" items are ICHD-3 verbatim]

1. **Prodromal phase**: in the hours to 1–2 days before the headache — fatigue, neck stiffness, yawning, mood/appetite changes (such as craving sweets) — the body is already sounding the alarm (this phase's manifestations are common clinical observation; ICHD-3's main table does not list it separately · [MODEL]).
2. **Aura phase**: **an aura is not universal** — only about a quarter of patients ever experience aura (the precise proportion varies across populations · [MODEL]; "everyone has an aura" is a myth). The typical visual aura is **reversible**: flashing spots, zigzag/fortification-like lines (fortification spectra), and a visual-field defect (scotoma) gradually expanding, usually self-resolving within 5–60 minutes [FACT · ICHD-3's "reversible visual symptoms" framing for visual aura · WHO text "an aura of reversible, visual, sensory or other symptoms"]. Aura can also present as sensory disturbance or speech disturbance.
3. **Headache phase**: unilateral/bilateral, pulsating, moderate-to-severe, worsened by walking or climbing stairs, with nausea/vomiting, photophobia and phonophobia, lasting 4–72 hours [FACT · ICHD-3 1.1, item by item]. WHO is explicit: **the analgesic should be taken as soon as the first symptoms (such as visual aura) appear**, to stop the headache phase [FACT · WHO Treatment section, verbatim].
4. **Resolution (recovery) phase**: 1–2 days of fatigue, poor concentration, a "hangover-like" feeling after the headache (common clinical description · [MODEL]).

### Trigger identification: the diary method, not blind avoidance

The core action in WHO's official advice is simply **keeping a headache calendar** to identify individual triggers, together with regular sleep/exercise, limiting alcohol, and adequate hydration [FACT · WHO text, "using a headache calendar to identify other triggers"]. Discipline points:

- **Change one variable at a time.** Quit coffee + quit cheese + change your schedule all at once, and if things improve you won't know which change earned it; if they worsen you won't know which one to blame.
- Common triggers [MODEL · common clinical checklist; individual variation is large]: hormonal fluctuation (menstrual cycle), too much/too little sleep, missed meals, weather changes, strong stimuli (light/odors), stress — and **let-down stress: the "weekend headache" that arrives right when you finally relax after a stretch of tension** is a classic migraine pattern.
- A trigger is not a life sentence of avoidance: the diary's purpose is to find **manageable patterns**, not to shrink life ever narrower.

### The correct posture for acute-phase medication

- **Use early, at the full single dose on the label (use at first symptoms)** [FACT · WHO text]; used late, vomiting and gastrointestinal stasis let the drug "chase" the headache in vain (common pharmacologic explanation · [MODEL]).
- **But hold the guardrail: total use of any acute headache medication should not exceed about 10 days per month** — cross that line and you enter medication-overuse headache territory (see §③) [FACT · conservative lower bound of ICHD-3 8.2's "10 or 15 days/month depending on drug"].
- Frequent attacks despite medication, or needing medication every day, is not a "increase the dose" problem — it is the signal that **it is time to discuss preventive treatment**.

### The indication logic for preventive treatment (your doctor decides · this volume writes no prescriptions)

Typical situations in which to discuss prevention with your doctor [MODEL · common guideline consensus · specific drug choice always defers to your doctor]: high attack frequency (e.g., ≥4 days per month affecting life), attacks infrequent but **severe / long-lasting / poorly responsive to acute medication**, acute-medication use approaching the guardrail, or frequent disability (missed school or work). The goal of prevention is not "eradication" — it is **bringing attack frequency and disability down and pulling acute-medication use back inside the safety line** — and those two are precisely the perceptible signals that prevention is working.

## ③ The medication-overuse headache dedicated face: the paradox of painkillers that cause pain

This is the most important dedicated face in this volume, because it is **entirely preventable and mostly reversible after discontinuation**, yet it is the most common secondary headache disorder [FACT · WHO text, "the most common secondary headache disorder"], with prevalence up to 5% in some populations [FACT · WHO].

**A mechanism-level description of the paradox (ICHD-3 8.2 framing · all verified this round)**:

- A person with an underlying primary headache (most often migraine or tension-type) **regularly overuses acute/symptomatic analgesics for more than 3 months** (≥10 or ≥15 days/month depending on drug — ICHD-3 main description verbatim), and the headache evolves into a chronic headache on **≥15 days per month**, sustained and pressing, worst on waking — this is 8.2 medication-overuse headache (MOH), formerly called "rebound headache" [FACT · ICHD-3 8.2 Previously used terms, verbatim].
- **Every drug class has its share**: ICHD-3 sets dedicated subtypes for ergotamines, triptans, non-opioid analgesics (**including "simple analgesics" such as paracetamol**), opioids, and **combination analgesic preparations** (e.g., caffeine-containing combination painkillers, its own subtype 8.2.5); even the case where several drugs are each below their own threshold but together constitute overuse gets its own 8.2.6 [FACT · ICHD-3 8.2 Notes, verbatim]. **There is no exemption for "mild drugs."** The common per-subtype day thresholds (simple single-ingredient analgesics 15 days/month; ergotamine/triptan/opioid/combinations 10 days/month) [MODEL · common clinical framing · the subtype-entry thresholds were not verified against their original pages this round; the main description's "10-or-15 distinction" was verified verbatim].
- **Scale**: epidemiological evidence indicates that among people with headache on ≥15 days per month, **more than half** meet criteria for MOH [FACT · ICHD-3 8.2 Comments, verbatim].
- **The withdrawal evidence, and the fact that it can be endured**: ICHD-3 Comments verbatim — **"the vast majority of patients improve after discontinuation of the overused medication, and responsiveness to prophylactic treatment recovers"**; the description section verbatim — after withdrawal, relief is "usually but not necessarily" complete; WHO text — **simple education** about MOH's causes and consequences is highly effective. In the early withdrawal period (days to weeks) headache often temporarily worsens, with nausea and poor sleep (common clinical observation · [MODEL]) — this is exactly the part of "it can be endured" one needs to know in advance: it has an endpoint, and **outpatient education plus regular follow-up can carry most withdrawals through**; complex cases (opioid/combination dependence, psychiatric comorbidity, repeated failed withdrawal attempts) go to a doctor for alternative planning — care routes in the care-access canon (whose line 49 already mentions this paradox in passing) and the pain volume's drug face.

**In plain words**: painkillers becoming less effective is not automatically "the disease is getting heavier"; the more common script is **the drug manufacturing your next day's headache**. Count how many days last month you took them — past 10–15 days it is time to see a doctor to discuss withdrawal and prevention, not to switch to a stronger painkiller.

## ④ The tension-type face: the most universal, the mildest, and the most ignored

- **Recognition**: bilateral, pressing/tightening quality ("a band cinched around the head," sometimes radiating to the neck/occiput), mild to moderate, not clearly worsened by routine activity, usually without nausea or vomiting [FACT · WHO/ICHD-3 framing]. Episodic TTH (<15 days/month) has been reported by over 70% of people in some populations [FACT · WHO] — it is the archetype of "high prevalence, low intensity": not fierce, but the most numerous.
- **The stress–posture–sleep triangle** [MODEL · common clinical framework]: mental stress, sustained neck/shoulder tension (desk work, head-down posture), and insufficient sleep form a mutually amplifying loop — stress keeps muscles contracted, poor posture keeps the cervical-occipital myofascia firing, and sleep loss lowers pain threshold. Breaking any one leg of the triangle (regular sleep, mid-work neck/shoulder movement, stress management) is often more effective than one more painkiller.
- **"Does massage/heat help?" — downgraded honestly**: manual therapy evidence concentrates on **cervicogenic headache** (a secondary headache type): 14 studies (11 RCTs) show manual therapy is one of the most promising options for cervicogenic headache, but **there is no consensus on technique** [FACT · PMID 35294051 · moderate certainty of evidence]; **for tension-type headache itself, the manual-therapy/heat evidence is insufficient, with small and unstable effects** [MODEL · directional] — you may try it, don't deify it, and never let it replace red-flag screening.
- If "tension-type headache" starts becoming ≥15 days per month and worse in the morning — beware that it has mixed with MOH (§③) or chronicized; seek evaluation.

## ⑤ The cluster face: fierce brief pain on a schedule — not a disease to "endure"

- **The nickname "suicide headache" is a documented, common literature alias, not rhetorical exaggeration**: three independent papers use it (Front Neurol 2025 · PMID 40248013; Neurol Sci 2019 · PMID 30877613; Acta Biomed 2006 · PMID 16856701), and explain it as corresponding to the extreme pain intensity and disease burden [FACT].
- **The picture** [FACT · ICHD-3 3.1, item by item]: extremely severe pain in/behind one orbit, supraorbital or temporal region ("like a red-hot drill boring upward from behind the eye" is a common patient description · [MODEL]), self-resolving within **15–180 minutes**, from every other day up to 8 per day; with ipsilateral eye tearing, conjunctival injection, nasal congestion and runny nose, drooping eyelid, facial sweating, and **agitation — the patient cannot lie down and paces back and forth**. Attacks run like a time bomb: **they cluster at fixed times of day** (especially at night) — whence the name "cluster" — and the mechanistic anchor is activation of the posterior hypothalamic grey matter, where the circadian clock lives [FACT · ICHD-3 Comments, verbatim].
- **During a cluster period even alcohol will trigger attacks** (alcohol is a reliable provocative agent within the cluster period) [FACT · ICHD-3 verbatim]; 10–15% of patients have no remission period (chronic form), and of these **up to 20% resist drug treatment** and need interventional options assessed [FACT · PMID 30877613].
- **Handling principle: acute-phase care takes priority — this is not a "grin and bear it" disease.** The link between cluster's pain intensity and suicide risk makes early diagnosis and early establishment of acute and preventive plans a hard requirement; male predominance (ICHD-3 framing about 3:1, WHO framing 6:1 — both framings coexist, recorded as such), onset at 20–40, easily sent circling the wrong departments as "toothache / glaucoma / sinusitis." After red-flag screening, first visit: neurology. A regular record of attacks (date, time of day, duration, accompaniments) is the first-hand evidence doctors need most.

## ⑥ Red-flag chapter: any one of these means emergency care

Red-flag logic: a primary headache is "an old friend with a stable attack pattern"; **a red flag is the new arrival — progressive, with systemic or neurological signs — the stranger**. The secondary-headache red-flag review's framework — **sudden onset, extremely severe pain, change in the pre-existing headache pattern, focal neurological signs or seizure, systemic signs, provoked by physical exertion** — with the special warning that **"I have an old headache" does not exclude secondary headache; in some situations it is even a predisposition** [FACT · PMID 37456555, verbatim synthesis].

| Red flag | Why it is dangerous | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **The worst headache of one's life / thunderclap headache** (peaking within seconds) | Subarachnoid hemorrhage and other intracranial vascular events | **Emergency department immediately** |
| **Fever + neck stiffness** (chin cannot reach the chest when bowing the head) | Meningitis / encephalitis | **Emergency department immediately** |
| **Neurological signs**: limb weakness/numbness, slurred speech, abnormal vision, altered consciousness, seizure | Stroke, mass lesion, intracranial disease | **Emergency department immediately** |
| **New headache after age 50** (especially new and progressive) | Temporal arteritis, intracranial mass and other age-related causes | Seek prompt medical evaluation |
| **Headache after head trauma** (especially worsening, or with vomiting/drowsiness) | Subdural/intracranial hemorrhage | **Emergency department immediately** |
| **Progressive worsening + morning vomiting** (or worse with coughing/straining) | Raised intracranial pressure | Seek promptly · eye exam for papilledema clues |
| **Papilledema clues**: transient blurred vision, persistent visual decline | Raised intracranial pressure | Seek promptly |

- Red flags = emergency care, and interlink with this system: the first-aid channel is **The first-aid canon** (first-aid-canon-v1-20260912); the child red-flag list (stiff neck, severe headache, repeated vomiting, rash, etc.) is in **The child-health face** (child-health-canon-v1-20260912, the AAP list at line 214); choosing a department, and the reassurance logic that "long-standing same-side headache + trigger-point pressure ≠ brain tumor," are in **The care-access canon** (care-access-canon-v1-20260912, line 49).
- Going to the emergency department for a headache is not a waste: the cost of a missed red flag (death/severe disability) is asymmetric against the cost of mostly "green" outcomes — decide by the asymmetry principle.

## ⑦ Falsification face: the true account of six popular claims

> Epistemic discipline: each item below gives a judgment and its basis. Falsifying a claim is not mockery — it is damage control.

1. **"Just endure a headache and it passes"** — **CONTESTED (false for cluster and for untreated migraine)**. Cluster pain ranks at the very top of human pain (the literature's common alias is "suicide headache" · three references verified); migraine is one of the main engines of the global neurological disease burden — WHO 2021 framing: migraine ranks **third** among neurological diseases in DALYs, behind only stroke and neonatal encephalopathy [FACT · WHO/GBD 2021]; among women 15–49, migraine alone accounts for 11.2% of all YLDs in that population [FACT · GBD 2016]. What "enduring" delays is exactly treatability.
2. **"Painkillers stop working = my disease is worsening"** — **CONTESTED (the common counter-example is medication-overuse headache)**. The likelier script is MOH: the diagnostic combination of headache ≥15 days/month plus regular analgesic overuse for >3 months (ICHD-3 8.2), with **most patients improving after discontinuation and responsiveness to prevention recovering** [FACT · ICHD-3 verbatim] — it is reversible; do not read it as a fatal prologue.
3. **"Migraine is a small ailment"** — **CONTESTED**. About 1.04 billion people affected worldwide (GBD 2016 verbatim), disability burden near the top of neurological diseases (WHO 2021: third · FACT); WHO also records that "globally only a minority of patients are correctly diagnosed and treated, and about half medicate themselves" [FACT · WHO verbatim]. The words "small ailment" are exactly what block the proper-care channel.
4. **"Neck massage cures headache"** — **CONTESTED (evidence downgraded)**. Manual-therapy evidence lies mainly in cervicogenic headache with no consensus on technique (PMID 35294051); for migraine/TTH there is no "cure"-grade evidence [MODEL]; **massage is forbidden when a red flag is present** — fever with neck stiffness, thunderclap headache, post-trauma, progressive worsening go to the emergency department first; massage may delay care and carries risk against unexamined structural causes [INFERENCE · extrapolated from red-flag logic].
5. **"Headache = high blood pressure"** — **CONTESTED (weak association)**. "Headache → check blood pressure → anxiety" is a common loop; hypertension is mostly asymptomatic, the direct causal link between headache and hypertension is weak in most situations, and both misreadings ("hypertension must cause headache; headache must mean hypertension") point the wrong way (severe hypertensive emergencies are a different matter — the red-flag channel) [MODEL · common clinical framing · no dedicated quantitative review verified this round; not promoted to FACT]. Do not manage blood-pressure anxiety with headache, and do not explain headache with a cuff — beyond the red flags, headache has its own channel.
6. **"Children don't get migraine"** — **CONTESTED (false)**. WHO verbatim: headache, "migraine in particular," can affect children and adolescents, and **childhood attacks are shorter, with abdominal symptoms often more prominent** [FACT · WHO]; ICHD-3 verbatim: childhood migraine is **more often bilateral**, occipital headache is rare and warrants diagnostic caution, onset can be as early as school age [FACT · ICHD-3 1.1]. A child's recurrent "stomach ache + headache" is not faking — it is a migraine-spectrum presentation worth a diary and medical evaluation (child red-flag channel: the child-health volume).
