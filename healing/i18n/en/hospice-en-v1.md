# End-of-Life and Palliative Care Canon v1 (2026-09-12)

Death is the doorway every person and every family eventually reaches. This volume answers only one question: **when a disease can no longer be cured, what can medicine still do, what should it not do, and how should the family choose**. It centers on non-cancer terminal illness (heart failure, COPD, kidney disease, dementia, and the like) — the WHO fact sheet is explicit: among adults needing palliative care, cardiovascular disease (38.5%) outnumbers cancer (34%), chronic respiratory diseases account for 10.3%, and diabetes 4.6% (FACT · WHO 2020 fact sheet, Chinese edition). "Palliative care is only for cancer patients" is a structural misunderstanding. Tumor-specific end-of-life faces interlink with the oncology volume (`tm:` anchors); the deeper faces of bereavement interlink with the cry:/grief: domain.

Epistemic labels: FACT = authoritative-source data verified by search this round; MODEL = model/framing estimate; INFERENCE = judgment derived from evidence; UNKNOWN = not yet verified, honestly not claimed. **This canon does not replace the doctor**: all medication and clinical decisions defer to the attending physician.

## Face 1 The concept-boundary face: palliative care ≠ giving up

### 1.1 What it is, and what it is not

The WHO definition: palliative care (called 安宁疗护, hospice/palliative care, in mainland China) is **an approach that improves the quality of life of patients and their families facing life-threatening illness**, through early identification, correct assessment and treatment of pain and other physical, psychosocial or spiritual problems, preventing and relieving suffering (FACT · WHO fact-sheet original text). Three key clarifications:

1. **It has active treatment content**: pain, breathlessness, delirium, constipation, insomnia all have mature management tools — the patient for whom "no drugs are left" is precisely the patient with a large armamentarium of symptom-directed treatment available.
2. **It can run alongside life-prolonging treatment**: the WHO states palliative care "should be provided early, together with life-prolonging therapy" — it is not something that waits until "all other treatment has stopped" (FACT · WHO fact sheet's orientation statement "should be offered from the time of diagnosis · not limited to end of life"). In practice it can be sequential or parallel; the rhythm is set by goals and benefit (MODEL · guideline-consensus direction).
3. **It brings the "family" into the circle of care**: including bereavement support — this is its institutional difference from ordinary hospital services (FACT · the WHO definition includes "provision of bereavement support").

### 1.2 Who it is for: recognizing non-cancer terminal illness

The end stages of organ failure and degenerative disease — heart failure, COPD, chronic kidney disease, cirrhosis, dementia — carry a symptom burden no lighter than advanced cancer. Generalized cues that "it is time to transition" (MODEL · an internationally used "terminal-phase identification" framework, for alertness, not a diagnostic standard):

- **Repeated unplanned hospitalizations**: repeated emergency visits/admissions for the same disease within a year, each recovery to baseline weaker than the last;
- **Stepwise functional decline**: walking → walking but breathless → chair-bound → bed-bound; in dementia, entry into FAST stage 7 (unable, even with help, to speak in full sentences, unable to walk unaided, incontinent) (FACT · FAST is the dementia functional staging tool published by Reisberg in 1988, PMID 3249767; its reliability/validity study is PMID 1504288);
- **The "half-life" indicator**: the list of procedures the physician would not want for themselves or would not perform on their own relative (the "surprise question" test);
- **Continued weight loss + clearly reduced food intake** (see Face 3, §3.5).

One point where chronic-disease end stage differs from cancer: **the course is unpredictable** — heart failure and COPD cycle through "deteriorate — rescue back — deteriorate again" over years, and the moment of death is harder to estimate. Hence palliative-care involvement for non-cancer patients often comes late (MODEL · the late-palliative-invocation phenomenon consistently reported in the literature). The countermeasure is not to predict death but to **talk, in advance and every year at the checkup, about "what I would want if I reached the terminal phase"** (Face 2).

## Face 2 The advance-care-planning face: leaving the decisions to yourself, in advance

### 2.1 Three concepts

- **Advance directive**: a document in which a person, while of clear mind, states in advance in writing "which medical interventions I do or do not want at the terminal stage of an incurable illness or at the end of life."
- **Living will (生前预嘱)**: the common colloquial expression in China and the wording adopted by the Shenzhen legislation; its meaning is the same as above (FACT · the legislative wording of the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone Medical Regulation — "a directive document stating which medical care one does or does not want at the terminal stage of an incurable illness or at the end of life," per the Shenzhen living-will promotion association's framing).
- **Advance care planning (ACP)**: the fuller process — not merely signing a document but the person + family + clinicians repeatedly discussing values and preferences; the document is only the sediment of that process (MODEL · international ACP consensus direction).

### 2.2 The Chinese context: Shenzhen first, other regions in progress

**Shenzhen legislated first**: the 2022 revision of the Shenzhen Special Economic Zone Medical Regulation wrote the living will into local legislation, effective 2023-01-01 — when a medical institution receives a patient's living will meeting the statutory conditions, at the terminal stage of an incurable illness or at the end of life it **must respect the patient's wishes** and not perform unnecessary traumatic resuscitation and the like (FACT · the regulation text on the Shenzhen municipal government portal and implementation reporting; Legal Daily 2022-07 coverage of the legislative process and expert discussion of procedural safeguards). This is the first local legislation in mainland China to give the living will legal rank.

**The situation elsewhere, stated honestly**: outside Shenzhen there is no unified national higher-level law defining the binding force of living wills; most regions practice through palliative-care pilot programs and hospital-internal "informed consent + wishes documentation," operating under the Civil Code's general provisions on patient autonomy and informed consent (MODEL · the latest local regulation and hospital process prevail — policy stays unfrozen).

### 2.3 The family's substitute-decision dilemma

The most common real-world scenario is not "is there a directive" but **"there was no time to leave one"**: the patient is comatose and the family must decide "intubate or not, compress or not." What the family carries at that moment is not the composure of legal authorization but the moral weight of "my choice = evidence of my guilt." Three pragmatic suggestions (INFERENCE · direction derived from the communication and family-conference literature):

1. **Talk as a whole family while talking is still possible**: begin with "If one day I can't speak for myself, I would want …" — not with a first discussion at the ICU door;
2. **Ask the patient, not yourself**: replace "Do you think Dad would want the tube?" with asking the lucid patient;
3. **Write it down and tell the attending physician**: even ordinary written words convert "the family's choice" into "the patient's wishes being carried out," and markedly reduce the family's later guilt (INFERENCE · the consistent direction of ACP and bereavement-psychology research).

## Face 3 The honest ledger of symptom control: terminal pain can be managed

### 3.1 Pain: oral morphine and the "addiction" fallacy

The WHO fact sheet: **80% of patients with late-stage HIV/AIDS or cancer and 67% of patients with cardiovascular disease or COPD experience moderate-to-severe pain at the end of life**, and opioid analgesics are indispensable for relieving it (FACT · WHO fact sheet, Chinese edition). The twin errors — "morphine = addiction" and "not taking it = enduring it" — are the number-one amplifier of artificially magnified end-of-life suffering.

**Falsifying the addiction fallacy**: pain itself is a protective factor against opioid addiction — under pain-relief use, addiction rates are not remotely the folk imagination's magnitude; systematic reviews assessing opioid use disorder in cancer-pain populations find it **low and manageable, requiring monitoring rather than fear** (FACT · 2025 multidisciplinary review of oncology opioids PMID 40806585, "opioid therapy for cancer pain is necessary but misuse risk is manageable, emphasizing screening and monitoring"; PMID 29465470, a review dedicated to opioid misuse in palliative/cancer-pain populations). **No verifiable authoritative value for a precise addiction rate was found this round — UNKNOWN; no number invented.** What is verifiable: standardized oral sustained-release morphine dosed to pain is the core instrument of the WHO three-step cancer-pain ladder, and medical-use addiction is rare enough that "analgesic inadequacy caused by misplaced fear" is the bigger problem (INFERENCE · reasoned from the WHO's "opioids are indispensable" plus the reviews' direction). The correct reading of "oral morphine keeps escalating until it poisons you": dose growth usually reflects disease progression and worsening pain, and under physician titration the safety margin is clear (MODEL · three-step guideline consensus direction).

### 3.2 Breathlessness: non-drug measures genuinely work

- **A fan blown at the face**: cold-air stimulation of the facial trigeminal distribution can lessen the sensation of breathlessness — a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials supports fan therapy for relieving breathlessness in adults (FACT · PMID 41761933, a 2026 meta-analysis of RCTs in respiratory care; PMID 42174753, a 2026 systematic review). A household floor fan aimed at the face, an open window, sitting upright leaning on a table — all zero-cost and doable immediately (MODEL · standard-practice direction).
- **Opioids work for breathlessness too**: low-dose opioids are the evidence-based instrument for refractory dyspnea, and mechanism research answers the common fear outright — **opioid relief of breathlessness does not hasten death** (FACT · PMID 22594628, the pathophysiology review "Why opioids relieve dyspnoea without accelerating death"; PMID 18692359, a study of end-of-life opioid use trends and death-hastening effects).
- Oxygen helps most only when blood oxygen is low; anxiety and breathlessness amplify each other, and sedating measures need physician assessment (MODEL · direction level).

### 3.3 Delirium: the symptom families fear most

Terminal delirium = an acute, fluctuating disturbance of consciousness and attention, presenting as drowsy stupor (hypoactive type) or agitation, grabbing, shouting, not recognizing family (hyperactive type). It is one of the commonest cognitive complications of the terminal phase and the most frightening image in families' memories (MODEL · consistently reported in the palliative literature). Management principles (direction level; all executed by clinical staff):

- **First find and correct reversible triggers**: infection, urinary retention, constipation, hypoxia, pain, drugs — remove the cause where it can be removed (FACT · direction of the palliative delirium expert consensus and reviews, PMID 24480529);
- **For the hyperactive type, pharmacological management centers on neuroleptics**: antipsychotics are the mainstream choice for terminal agitated delirium; in randomized controlled studies of agitated delirium in terminal cancer, **adding lorazepam on top of haloperidol brought no benefit and more adverse events** (FACT · JAMA 2017 randomized controlled study PMID 28975307; Lancet Oncology 2020 study of neuroleptic strategies for terminal agitation in acute palliative units PMID 32479786) — "add a sedative to quiet the patient" is not a direction the family should push; the regimen belongs to the doctor;
- **What the family can do**: keep lights on, place familiar objects nearby, softly say your own name, do not argue with the patient's misperceptions, keep a calendar and clock in view (MODEL · non-pharmacological management consensus direction).

### 3.4 The terminal death rattle

In the final hours to days, the patient loses the strength to clear airway secretions and breathing produces a gurgling sound. **It is distressing for the family to hear, but current evidence does not support "suction whenever there is secretion"**: the Cochrane systematic review's conclusion on interventions for the death rattle is that reliable evidence is lacking that drug interventions eliminate it (FACT · PMID 18254072, Cochrane 2008); clinical-practice Delphi consensus and recent reviews both prioritize **explanation and reassurance + positioning (side-lying) + reducing intravenous-fluid overload**, with **routine suction explicitly listed as an intervention with no benefit that only adds suffering** (FACT · PMID 38565275, a nursing-practice Delphi study; PMID 41024465, a 2025 review of the rattle's footprint; PMID 41376311, a management review). The key point: the rattle usually **does not signal that the patient is in distress** — it frightens the listeners; explaining this to the family present is itself an intervention (INFERENCE).

### 3.5 Refusing food and fluids: a natural process, not "starving"

A terminal patient's fading appetite and disappearing thirst are part of the disease process, not a failure of care. The classic prospective study observed 32 lucid terminally ill patients in a comfort-care unit continuously until death: **63% never felt hunger**; among those who did develop hunger or thirst, **small amounts of food, mouth care and lip balm sufficed for relief**, without forced feeding or intravenous fluids (FACT · JAMA 1994, PMID 7523740, the McCann comfort-care study). In advanced dementia, enteral feeding (nasogastric tube / gastrostomy) **has not been shown to prolong survival or reduce aspiration pneumonia**; hand feeding plus comfort care is the mainstream recommendation (FACT · BMJ 2025 clinical review of enteral feeding in advanced dementia PMID 40374293; comparison of tube-feeding and hand-feeding outcomes PMID 39518674; de-implementation decision support PMID 42302160). The reframe for families: **"I couldn't feed them" is not your failure — it is the body's natural deceleration**; a forced tube buys no time; what it buys is restraint, aspiration and discomfort (INFERENCE · derived from the evidence above).

## Face 4 The falsification face of "giving up treatment = unfilial / leaving them to die"

### 4.1 The bodily cost of futile resuscitation is a real number

"Try CPR, just in case" — the harm data for CPR in non-traumatic cardiac arrest: a systematic review and meta-analysis of 74 studies and 16,629 patients found: **overall CPR-related injury incidence 60% (95% CI 49–71), with rib fracture at a pooled prevalence of 55% (95% CI 48–62)**, and mechanical compression devices injuring more than manual compression (RR 1.36) (FACT · PMID 38206442, Eur J Trauma Emerg Surg 2024). For a frail elder at the end of life, rib fractures and sternal injury are not abstract risks; they are the reality of "even if the heartbeat comes back, the remaining days are spent in pain and pneumonia" (INFERENCE). This does not say CPR is useless — it saves lives when the cause is reversible and expected survival is reasonable; it says **on a terminal, frail body the benefit/harm ratio of CPR is entirely different**, and that is precisely the decision a living will and a family conference should settle in advance (the bridge from FACT to decision).

### 4.2 The ethical standing of "giving up invasive resuscitation out of love"

The equation "no resuscitation = leaving them to die" needs inverting: when cure is no longer possible, **continuing to escalate traumatic procedures is harm to the body and a violation of the person's wishes**; choosing comfort care switches medicine's goal from "more days" to "the quality of each day" — this does not conflict with love; it is love's adult form (INFERENCE · a value judgment supported by the WHO definition and the comfort-care evidence chain). The real question of filial devotion is not "are we willing to sign the abandonment" but "does the patient themself want these tubes." Shenzhen's legislative wording is worth remembering: medical institutions **must respect** the wishes laid down by patients themself at the terminal stage of an incurable illness (FACT · the regulation's framing).

### 4.3 The family conference, four steps

The operational skeleton of terminal-phase decision-making (MODEL · a family-conference adaptation built on the SPIKES six-step bad-news protocol; FACT · Baile et al., The Oncologist 2000, PMID 10964998):

1. **Preparation and opening**: gather the key family members, choose a quiet room; open by saying "today I'd like us to talk together about the goals of care from here";
2. **Listen first, speak second**: ask the family/patient first, "How much do you understand of the illness now? What worries you most?" — let the fear pour out first;
3. **Honest telling + options**: state the disease trajectory in plain terms, then offer options (continue symptom-directed + comfort care / the goals and costs of a specific treatment), each option with "what it can and cannot bring the patient";
4. **Close and record**: confirm understanding ("Could you tell me back what we just decided?"), write the consensus into orders and documents, and make explicit "this can be changed at any time."

Cultural note: the common Chinese-family pattern of "family-concealed telling" (asking the doctor to hide the diagnosis from the patient) must be viewed on two faces — the protective motive deserves respect, but **the patient has the right to know and to take part in deciding their own end-of-life arrangements**, and most patients' anxiety falls rather than rises after honest telling (MODEL · consistent direction of communication research); a workable compromise is "graded disclosure": first give the next-step plan and choices, and answer to whatever depth the patient asks (INFERENCE).

## Face 5 The terminal signs and companionship face: the last weeks, days, hours

### 5.1 The dying timeline (common order · wide individual variation)

- **Weeks before**: appetite fades, activity lessens, sleep lengthens, the social circle contracts to the few closest people;
- **Days before**: fluctuating consciousness (drowsiness alternating with lucidity), less urine, weaker swallowing, seeing/speaking of people or things that are not there (these can be near-death experiences without pathology; reassure, nothing more needed) (MODEL · textbook-consensus direction of terminal symptomatology);
- **Hours before**: breathing rhythm changes (deep and shallow alternating, pauses), the death rattle (see §3.4), limbs turning cool with mottling, consciousness deepening.
- Order and duration vary enormously between individuals; this timeline exists to **remove fear** ("he has stopped eating" ≠ "we neglected him"), not to count down.

### 5.2 Hearing fades last: the evidence file for whispers at the deathbed

"Hearing is the last sense to go" acquired direct electrophysiological evidence in 2020: EEG recorded from unresponsive dying patients in a hospice ward showed their brains still had measurable responses to sound stimuli, distinguishable from healthy controls yet still present — **the dying brain can still respond to sound in the final hours** (FACT · Blundon et al., Sci Rep 2020, PMID 32587364, UBC study). Operational meaning: **be careful what you say in the patient's presence (unable to speak does not mean unable to hear); bedside whispers, farewells, apologies, thanks and goodbyes all have an evidentiary basis** (INFERENCE · companionship practice derived from that evidence). Sight and touch are also worth using: a familiar voice and a held hand are the final channels of communication (MODEL · consensus direction of end-of-life companionship).

### 5.3 What to do at the final moment

**Presence itself is a medical act**: there is no "useful thing" you must do — talk (even if you do not know whether he hears), hold the hand, play music he loves, tell him who is present. Three things worth saying: thank you, I'm sorry, I love you; and the most important sentence of all — **"You can let go now; we will be all right."** (MODEL · consensus of end-of-life companionship and farewell practice.) If there is an unfinished religious/folk ritual need (returning home to die, inviting clergy to chant, etc.), that belongs to [folk·traditional] / [religious·traditional] practice: families may arrange it per their own faith, but should know **transport is a physical and temporal burden on both the dying person and the family**, and that an ambulance's jolting, death en route, and the downstream paperwork costs of death outside a medical facility should be asked of the institution in advance (FACT · transport procedures vary by jurisdiction — local regulation prevails; judged a values choice: described without denigration, without unilateral promotion).

### 5.4 The first hour after death

A death at home (the home-palliative-care scenario): do not move the body at once; first notify the contracted palliative-care/community-health team to issue the death certificate, then contact funeral services and the police-station household-registration window (FACT · procedures vary by jurisdiction — the latest local regulation prevails; UNKNOWN · local details not verified item by item); allow the family (children included) to be present, to touch, to say goodbye; **weeping and feeling relieved are both normal reactions** — a long-term carer's exhaustion and sense of release do not equal absence of love (INFERENCE; connects to Face 6).

## Face 6 The bereavement-and-grief face

### 6.1 Acute grief: the normal spectrum

The mourning that follows the loss of a loved one (longing, tidal waves of grief, insomnia, briefly "seeing" the deceased, life standing still) is **a normal reaction to loss**, not an illness. The deeper faces interlink with the grief domain: the normal-grief spectrum and supports are in the cry:/grief: maps (data/healing/grief-map-20260912.json, e.g., `cry:children_grief`).

### 6.2 Prolonged grief disorder (PGD): the dividing line is not "enough time has passed"

ICD-11 and DSM-5-TR have formally admitted **prolonged grief disorder** into the diagnostic systems: its core is **yearning for and/or preoccupying thoughts about the deceased** persisting, accompanied by emotional pain, identity disruption, loss of meaning and **functional impairment** (FACT · Lancet 2025 review PMID 40254022: typical onset **6–12 months after the loved one's death** · psychotherapy is the mainstay treatment). Two requisites separate it from normal grief, and both are required (FACT · same review + epidemiological-estimate study PMID 42689843): ① **time** — grief intensity shows no trend of settling after 6–12 months; ② **function** — work, relationships and self-care remain persistently impaired. **Falsifying "grief has a standard timetable": it is not "a year has passed so you should be fine," but "persisting beyond a year AND life function collapsing" that signals the disorder**; time threshold met with function intact = still within the normal spectrum. Conversely, before the time threshold but with function already severely disintegrated, help should be sought early (INFERENCE). Psychotherapy is PGD's mainstay; drugs are not first-line (FACT · Lancet 2025 review).

### 6.3 Children's age-graded understanding of death, and telling them

Children's understanding of death is built dimension by dimension with cognitive development: the literature recognizes three core dimensions — **biological cessation (a dead body no longer works), irreversibility (the dead do not come back), universality (everyone dies)** — with further dimensions such as personal attribution still under study (FACT · 2026 narrative review PMID 41778364; a study of child death-understanding instruments PMID 33572418). **Precise age cut-points vary widely between individuals; this volume gives no hard age table — UNKNOWN/MODEL**; the broad direction: around school age children move step by step from magical thinking ("death is reversible") to all three dimensions, children who directly experience a death and are honestly told understand more accurately, and unguided indirect learning raises the risk of forming mistaken or fearful understandings (FACT · conclusions of the PMID 41778364 review).

Principles for telling a child (MODEL/INFERENCE · direction derived from the developmental literature): **use the word "dead," not "went to sleep" or "went somewhere far away"** (to prevent magical-thinking misreading); let the child cry and also let the child play as usual (the two do not contradict); be specific and definite ("Grandma's body stopped working, it can't hurt anymore, and we miss her very much"); let the child take part in farewell rituals but **never force it**; deeper scripts in the cry domain at `cry:children_honesty`.

## Face 7 The falsification face: the six most widely circulated myths (each with its falsifier)

| # | Myth | Evidence and falsification | Falsifier (what would overturn this conclusion) |
|---|------|---------------------------|-------------------------------|
| 1 | "Morphine hastens death" | The breathlessness pathophysiology review is explicit: correctly titrated opioids relieve dyspnea **without hastening death** (FACT · PMID 22594628); the end-of-life opioid-trends study points the same way (PMID 18692359) | If high-quality RCT evidence appeared showing significantly shortened survival despite guideline-conform titration, this item would be voided |
| 2 | "You must not tell the patient the truth" | The family-concealment tradition deserves respect for its protective motive, but the patient's right to know and to take part in decisions stands independently; honest-communication protocols (SPIKES) are standard training content (FACT · PMID 10964998); the Shenzhen legislation anchors legal force in the patient's own wishes (FACT · regulation text) | If high-quality research showed disclosure generally worsens outcomes in the Chinese cultural context and patients mostly prefer not to know, graded disclosure would need re-evaluation |
| 3 | "If feeding fails, insert a tube" | Enteral feeding in advanced dementia has not been shown to prolong survival / reduce aspiration pneumonia (FACT · PMIDs 40374293, 39518674); 63% of terminal patients never feel hunger and small amounts of mouth care suffice (FACT · PMID 7523740) | If an RCT showed tube feeding improves survival and comfort in a specific terminal subgroup, this item would need rewriting by subgroup |
| 4 | "Palliative care = waiting for death" | The early-palliative RCT: in metastatic non-small-cell lung cancer, QoL scores 98.0 vs 91.5 (P=0.03), depressive symptoms 16% vs 38%, aggressive end-of-life care 33% vs 54%, **and median survival was in fact longer: 11.6 vs 8.9 months (P=0.02)** (FACT · Temel et al., NEJM 2010, PMID 20818875) | If subsequent large RCTs in other tumor types / non-cancer populations consistently showed early palliative care shortens survival, the conclusion would need restating |
| 5 | "The dying must be carried home" | Belongs to [folk·traditional] values practice; respected without denigration; but transport carries bodily and procedural burdens, and death en route brings the downstream paperwork costs of death outside a medical facility (FACT · jurisdictional rules vary; the latest local regulation prevails); bedside whispers and companionship have an EEG evidence file (FACT · PMID 32587364) and can be done at home or at the bedside | If jurisdictions built zero-burden green channels for end-of-life transport and research showed transport improves the dying experience, the burden argument would need updating |
| 6 | "Grief has a standard timetable" | The PGD dividing line = time (no settling after 6–12 months) **AND** functional impairment; both requisites required (FACT · PMIDs 40254022, 42689843); both "a year passed so it should be fine" and "the time hasn't come so it isn't an illness" misread the diagnosis | If later ICD-11/DSM revisions change the time threshold or the structure of requisites, update to the new edition |

Two supplementary falsifications: **"Suction clears the death rattle"** — Cochrane and consensus support explanation-and-reassurance first; routine suction brings no benefit (FACT · PMIDs 18254072, 38565275; falsifier: a new RCT showing selective and shallow suction improves comfort without harm); **"CPR, just in case"** — on the terminal frail body, CPR's 60% injury rate and 55% rib-fracture rate are real numbers that must be placed on the decision table (FACT · PMID 38206442; falsifier: if frail-subgroup data prove significantly lower than the pooled value, rewrite by subgroup).
