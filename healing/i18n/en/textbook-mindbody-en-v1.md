# The Mind-Body-Spirit Textbook v1 — Eighteen Factors, Every One with a Downside

> **Readers and how to use this book**: self-learners read systematically from Chapter 1; those who only want to act can stop after Chapter 0. The dividing line between this textbook and the mainstream "wellness checklists" is a single one: **every mind-body-spirit practice reports its side effects honestly** — meditation has a measured rate of unpleasant experiences, gratitude-style writing has an avoidance risk, and closeness to nature has a safety face. Every conclusion is tagged with its confidence level, and the section-end notes [L3:编号] lead back to the evidence layer for checking.

## Chapter 0. One-page action version (L1)

> The full one-pager lives at `docs/healing/textbook/mindbody-l1-80yi-20260912.md`; key points below.

**In one sentence**: get the body moving, give the environment both light and dark, make relationships actually exist — these are harder than any "soul fast food"; and every mind-body practice has a downside, so use it with the brakes on.

**Seven action cards**: ① move first, fine-tune later: 150–300 minutes a week of brisk-walk-level activity + 2 strength sessions (guideline-recommended), and 60–75 minutes of daily activity offsets the harm of prolonged sitting (confirmed) ② sleep is the foundation, and both ends of it are bad: 7–9 hours, anchored on a fixed wake-up time; chronically <6 h and >9 h both track with higher mortality risk (confirmed · association) ③ at least 120 minutes of nature a week (confirmed · association), with the benefit capped at 200–300 minutes (fairly well established) ④ relationships are a hard metric: people with stronger relationships have 50% higher survival odds (confirmed), and a bad relationship is worse than no relationship ⑤ meditation needs the brakes on: 83% of trainees report at least one unpleasant experience and 6–14% last longer (confirmed · a study built specifically to test side effects); start with guidance; those with a trauma history ask a professional first ⑥ the three-way stress sort + long exhales (confirmed · immediate effect) ⑦ help within your capacity: volunteering is associated with a 22% lower mortality risk (confirmed · association), causality unproven, and compelled, assigned help shows no benefit.

**Myth quick-check**: meditation suits everyone (83% report unpleasant experiences) ｜ positive energy cures disease (forced positivity = secondary suppression) ｜ gratitude lists work on the spot (small effect, varies person to person) ｜ making more friends cures loneliness (pressure = secondary harm) ｜ nature is all benefit and no cost (there is a ceiling) ｜ the more you help the better (caregiver burnout).

**Learning objectives**: hold the layering logic and the four-group usage.

## Chapter 2. The sensory environment: the body reads the world all the time (9 factors)

**Learning objectives**: tell apart which inputs have hard evidence and which are only associations; know the typical wrong usages.

### 2.1 Light, temperature, noise, nature — four environment dials

**Light** is the primary setter of the circadian rhythm: morning light advances the rhythm and lifts daytime alertness; light therapy brings small-to-moderate improvement in non-seasonal depression (confirmed by research). The flip side: blue light at night is the reverse operation (delays the rhythm, stirs up sleep); **in people with bipolar disorder (manic-depressive illness), light therapy can trigger mania** (fairly well established · clinical consensus); a "morning-light ritual" punched in as a checkbox, without looking at actual illuminance, is a behavioral pseudo-effect. [L3:B3]

**Temperature** (sauna / cold water) is a controllable "exercise-like stressor". A 20-year Finnish follow-up: those with 4–7 sauna sessions a week vs 1 a week had about 63% lower risk of sudden cardiac death (confirmed by research · association · causality unproven). The flip side: sauna dehydration and fainting, **alcohol plus sauna is a traditionally known dangerous combination**; the "cold shock response" at the moment of entering cold water (uncontrollable gasping inhalation + arrhythmia risk) can kill; a cold-water immersion immediately after exercise blunts the muscle-building signal — counterproductive for a muscle-building goal (fairly well established). [L3:B4]

**Noise**: nighttime noise activates the stress axis and fragments sleep **without waking consciousness** — the sleeping ear keeps the ledger too (confirmed by research · the effect exists). Move the bedroom toward ≤40 decibels; white noise masks unstable sound with stable sound (fairly well established). The flip side: **absolute silence ≠ better** — a low-level steady soundscape actually aids sleep, and over-silence makes tiny noises stand out more. [L3:M5]

**Nature**: people with ≥120 minutes of nature contact per week rate their health and sense of wellbeing significantly higher, with the benefit peaking at 200–300 minutes and no further gain beyond (confirmed by research · association). The flip side: the real risks of ultraviolet exposure / tick-borne disease / going out on poor-air-quality days; "treating nature as a substitute for care and delaying seeing a doctor" is listed by the canon as an applicability-boundary risk. [L3:M6]

### 2.2 Exercise and breath — two body outputs

**Exercise** is the widest-coverage modifiable factor, and its dose-response is settled (confirmed by research). The flip side: overtraining syndrome (training–recovery imbalance → falling performance + mood disturbance + immune suppression); a newcomer's sudden sprint can cause rhabdomyolysis (broken-down muscle cells injuring the kidneys); for people with unassessed cardiovascular disease, high intensity carries acute-event risk — **screening before sprinting**. [L3:B2]

**Breath** is the only autonomic channel you can steer at will (the nerves that manage the organs automatically, split into an accelerator and a brake): slow breathing immediately raises heart-rate variability (HRV · see the glossary; confirmed by research · immediate effect), while the evidence on long-term clinical outcomes is thinner (fairly well established). **The number-one technical error is hyperventilation** — turning "exhale longer than you inhale" into panting: carbon dioxide drops too low → dizziness and tingling hands, and the anxiety worsens instead. Breath-hold techniques call for caution in people with epilepsy / cardiovascular conditions / pregnancy; "deep breathing to flush out acids" wellness is pseudoscience packaging. [L3:B5]

### 2.3 Sleep, nutrition, microbiome — the metabolic chassis

For sleep and nutrition, see the Sleep and Eat volumes; only the handoff surfaces are listed here: sleep is the repair window, and the mortality curve is U-shaped (both ends are bad); nutrition defers to the nutrition canon (ultra-processed food, fiber 25–29 g/day, dinner 3 hours before bed). [L3:B1][L3:B6]

**The microbiome** is the "converter" between diet and the psyche (the microbiome-gut-brain axis): butyrate-producing bacteria correlate steadily and positively with quality of life, and certain bacterial genera are reduced in depression (confirmed by research · association); 10 weeks of fermented foods raised microbiome diversity and lowered 19 inflammation markers (confirmed by research · single trial). The flip side: **"universal strains with universal probiotic benefit" = still contested in the field**; a sudden jump in fiber brings bloating; consumer-grade "microbiome testing" involves overdiagnosis. [L3:B7]

## Chapter 3. Emotion: stress is not the enemy — losing control is (4 factors)

**Learning objectives**: master the three-way stress sort; tell "reappraisal" apart from "suppression"; know the meditation side-effect spectrum as it actually is.

### 3.1 Stress: two faces of one thing

The same stress mediators (cortisol and other stress-chemistry messengers) are an adaptation resource in the short term; chronic overload becomes "allostatic load" — the cumulative wear of repeatedly adapting to stress (the mechanistic framework is widely accepted). The practice — **the three-way stress sort**: controllable → break it down into action; uncontrollable → reappraise + accept; physiological → breathing, movement. Additions: schedule recovery periods of matching length, and reappraise the stress response as "the body mobilizing resources" (all fairly well established). [L3:M1]

The flip side: **the pursuit of "zero stress" itself becomes a stressor** — the belief that "stress is harmful" independently predicts worse outcomes (fairly well established); avoidant stress relief (alcohol / binge eating / screen scrolling) adds load in the reverse direction. [L3:M1]

### 3.2 Emotion regulation: reframing is cheap, hard suppression is expensive

**Cognitive reappraisal** (swapping in a more accurate reading of the same event) lowers emotional reactivity at a small physiological cost; **expressive suppression** (pressing it down and not showing it) looks calm on the surface while physiological load rises (fairly well established). Three tools: affect labeling (one sentence — "I am feeling X right now"); expressive writing 15–20 minutes × 3–4 days; **delaying the response** rather than reacting instantly or suppressing instantly. [L3:M2]

The flip side: **rumination disguised as regulation** (repeatedly "processing" = rehearsing the pain); **forced positivity = secondary suppression**; expressive writing **may be harmful in the acute trauma period** (still contested in the field · timing-sensitive); the expectation "I shouldn't have emotions" suppresses real signals → they convert into bodily symptoms (somatization).

> **On "gratitude lists"**: the evidence layer does not itemize gratitude practice separately; the closest evidence is the expressive-writing face — pooled effects are small and heterogeneous (fairly well established), not instantly effective; use with caution in the acute period.

### 3.3 Cognitive load: the prefrontal cortex is a finite resource

Sustained multitasking and decision fatigue drain executive function (model-level experimental evidence → the long-term health outcomes are inference). Practice: 90–120-minute single-task blocks + real rest (rest = not drifting off into the phone); write to-dos down to externalize them. The flip side: over-optimizing efficiency → leisure too becomes instrumentalized (rest becomes a KPI); overdoing the information diet → decoupling from the world. [L3:M4]

### 3.4 Meditation/mindfulness: the benefits are real and so are the side effects (this volume's soul section)

Benefits: mindfulness programs bring small-to-moderate improvement in anxiety/depression in non-clinical populations (confirmed by research · pooled level). **The side effects have measured rates** (the study built specifically to test side effects): **83% report at least one unpleasant experience; 58% negative-valence experiences; 37% impaired functioning; 6–14% lasting adverse effects** (confirmed by research), correlated with signs of hyperarousal / dissociation (the detached feeling of watching the world through glass). [L3:S4]

Practice: start at 10–20 minutes a day (breath anchoring / body scan); **guided beats self-taught** (adverse reactions are noticed earlier); long retreats are for those with a foundation plus support. Flip-side list: **for people with a trauma history, retreat-style long meditation can make things worse** (re-experiencing risk · clinical warning); "meditation sickness" was recorded long ago in the traditional Buddhist literature (traditional practice, not modern evidence + model); avoidant use (using meditation to dodge what needs facing); taking "no thoughts" as the success criterion → the effort-failure loop.

## Chapter 4. Social connection: relationships are the oldest medicine (3 factors)

**Learning objectives**: know the evidence strength for connection and its causal gap; recognize "bad relationships" and "compelled altruism".

### 4.1 Connection itself

A synthesis of 148 studies covering 309,000 people: people with stronger relationships have 50% higher survival odds (confirmed by research · association · consistent across populations); loneliness, isolation, and living alone each independently track with higher mortality risk. **The causal direction remains inference** (reverse causation partly controlled for, not eliminated). Practice: at least 1 face-to-face deep conversation per week; shared-activity socializing (exercise / volunteering / shared meals); maintain a strong-tie circle of 3–5 people. The flip side: **a bad relationship is worse than no relationship** (the health cost of high-conflict relationships exceeds that of isolation · inference); social exhaustion (introverts have a dose ceiling); **pressuring a lonely person to "go make more friends" = secondary harm**; online weak ties cannot replace face-to-face. [L3:M3]

### 4.2 Serving others: hard association, empty causality

Volunteers have about 22% lower mortality risk (confirmed by research · systematic-review pooled), **but randomized controlled trials have not confirmed causality** — the selection bias that "people who help were already healthier" has not been ruled out. The canon calls it **the case in this layer most in need of honest labeling**. The flip side: caregiver burnout / compassion fatigue are real occupational risks; excessive altruism = self-neglect; "moral licensing" (issuing yourself a permit for indulgence after helping); **compelled, assigned volunteering loses its benefit and can even reverse it**. [L3:S5]

### 4.3 Grief: the continuation face of love

Grief is a continuation of love, not an obstacle to it: **integrated grief** (maintaining the inner bond with the deceased rather than forcibly severing it) predicts adaptation; the "stage theory" has been replaced by the **wave-rhythm model** (grief arrives in waves and recedes · fairly well established). Practice: allow the waves; ritualize (funerals / memorials / death anniversaries — cross-cultural traditions); **make no major decisions in the acute period** (clinical consensus); continue the bond (letters / dialogic writing). The flip side: **prolonged grief disorder** (disabling grief exceeding cultural norms, now listed as a disease entity) needs professional intervention, not gritting your teeth; "let go / move on / look forward" scripts = forced severing = secondary harm; dodging mourning entirely with busyness → delayed grief. [L3:S2]

## Chapter 5. Meaning: why those with a reason to live are more likely to keep living (2 factors)

**Learning objectives**: understand that "meaning is woven, not found"; know the evidence state of awe as it actually is.

### 5.1 Sense of meaning

A sense of purpose predicts all-cause survival, with cohorts pointing the same direction (fairly well established · observational); the exact effect size has not been verified — **not known**. The mediation path "loneliness → meaning erosion → mortality risk" has fresh evidence (fairly well established · association). Practice: values-clarification writing; explicitly linking daily behavior to your meaning sentence; **revise the meaning sentence the moment it goes stale** — meaning is woven, not found. The flip side: **meaning that admits no revision = identity fragility** (pierced by reality with no update → a meaning crisis); force-feeding slogans into a vacuum deepens the void; "must find the one true calling" → choice paralysis; meanings imposed by others bite back. [L3:S1]

### 5.2 Awe: the thinnest evidence — prefer an empty cell to an invented one

The "self-dissolution" of awe experiences (feeling yourself shrink; rumination and anxiety temporarily dropping) points one way, but **no verifiable awe-health pooled study was found** — no effect size is given; **not known**. Practice: one awe micro-practice a week — starry skies / grand music / natural vistas / biographies. The flip side: **for people with low self-esteem, the small-self feeling can flip into self-abasement** (still contested in the field); awe weaponized into mobilizing obedience and worship (a history-grade lesson); over-pursuing transcendent experiences → risk-taking and detachment from reality. [L3:S3]

### 7.2 The three medical boundaries

1. **Mind-body-spirit practice does not replace treatment** — "using nature / meditation / gratitude instead of seeking care" is itself listed by the canon as a side effect.
2. **In an acute mental-health period, seek care first**: acute depression, bipolar disorder, psychotic symptoms (such as hallucinations); in bipolar disorder, light therapy can trigger mania and requires professional evaluation.
3. **With a trauma history, ask a professional before meditating**: retreat-style long meditation carries a re-experiencing risk; expressive writing may be harmful in the acute trauma period (still contested in the field).

### 7.3 The boundary sentences toward the wu-line and sound healing (how to treat tradition)

The placebo effect is a real pathway, but what it **proves is the pathway — it proves no symbolic system true**. The psychological mechanisms of ritual (sense of control / meaning reconstruction / collective presence) are real and usable; traditional practice is honestly labeled "traditional" (traditional practice, not modern evidence), never passed off as modern evidence. ## Chapter 8. How to practice today: decision tree, glossary, FAQ, self-test

### 8.1 Mind-body micro-practices for today (Figure 4 · decision tree)

```

├─ Heart pounding / body tight (acute stress)

│   → stop: dizziness and tingling hands = you turned it into hyperventilation; return to natural breathing

│   → stop: the more you write the more you circle = rumination; stop for today; within one month of the event (acute period), do not force the writing
├─ Restless / cannot settle
│   → breath-anchored meditation 10–20 minutes (guided audio is steadier)

│   → set up one face-to-face deep conversation; or 2 hours of skills-based volunteering
```

### 8.3 FAQ (ten questions, ten answers)

1. **Meditation isn't for everyone?** In the study built specifically to test side effects, 83% of trainees had unpleasant experiences and 6–14% lasted longer (confirmed by research); most are transient, but it is not "a zero-risk good thing".
2. **Can I meditate with a trauma history?** Short, guided forms that let you open your eyes at any time carry lower risk; retreat-style long meditation carries a re-experiencing risk (clinical warning). Ask a professional first.
3. **Do gratitude lists actually work?** The evidence layer does not itemize them; the closest evidence (expressive writing) shows small effects that vary person to person (fairly well established). Fine as a habit; not fine as "an instantly effective medicine".
4. **Isn't "positive energy" a good thing?** Swapping in a more accurate reading (reappraisal) works; forbidding negative emotions (forced positivity) = secondary suppression; the belief itself that "stress is harmful" predicts worse outcomes (fairly well established).
5. **Socially anxious and lonely — what do I do?** Don't force yourself to "make more friends" (pressure = secondary harm). Start with shared-activity forms: group exercise, volunteering, shared meals — there is something to do, and the chatting pressure is smaller.
6. **How long until grief is "abnormal"?** It follows the wave rhythm; there is no single clock. Beyond cultural norms and impairing daily functioning (prolonged grief disorder), it needs professional intervention, not gritting your teeth.
7. **Are saunas / cold baths worth taking up?** Sauna's observational data are fairly hard but causality unproven (fairly well established); the long-term benefit of cold water is not known. With cardiovascular disease, both are dangerous; no sauna after drinking; avoid post-exercise cold immersion during a muscle-building phase.
8. **Should I take probiotics?** "Universal strains with universal benefit" is still contested in the field; the more practical thing is fiber (25–29 g/day) + keeping fermented foods on hand + plant diversity.
9. **Lots of stray thoughts while meditating — is that failure?** No. Taking "no thoughts" as the success criterion is the failure loop; noticing the wandering and gently coming back — that very move is the training.
10. **When must I see a doctor instead of continuing to practice?** Persistent impairment (nearly every day for over two weeks), thoughts of self-harm, an acute mental-health period (such as hallucinations), bodily red flags like chest pain or fainting — see a doctor first, and practice steps back to an auxiliary role.

### 8.4 Self-test (10 questions with answers)

1. In the study built specifically to test side effects, what share of meditation trainees reported at least one unpleasant experience? **Answer: 83% (58% negative-valence, 37% impaired functioning, 6–14% lasting adverse effects).**
2. True or false: the longer you sleep, the healthier you are. **Answer: false — mortality vs duration is U-shaped, both ends rise (long sleep suspected of reverse causation; fairly well established).**
