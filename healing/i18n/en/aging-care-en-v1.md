# Aging Care Canon v1 (2026-09-12)

Aging is not a disease — yet the heaviest health burdens of an aging society — falls and fractures, dementia, disability, polypharmacy — are mostly not "things that simply happen when you're old," but processes with evidence for prevention and delay. This canon compresses aging health into seven faces: **muscle (the currency) → bone (the bank) → brain (the asset) → medications (liability management) → geriatric syndromes (the early-warning system) → caregivers (the system's power supply) → the falsification face (the anti-scam firewall)**. The two audiences: people whose family includes an older adult, and people aged 40–60 who still want to bank health capital for the future.

Epistemic labels: FACT = authoritative-source data verified by search this round; MODEL = model/framing estimate; INFERENCE = judgment derived from evidence; UNKNOWN = not yet verified, not claimed. **This canon does not replace the doctor**: all medication, screening and diagnostic decisions defer to the physician.

## Face 1. The sarcopenia face: muscle is the currency of aging health

### 1.1 Why muscle is "the currency"

Almost every health outcome of later life — whether one falls, whether one stands back up after a fracture, whether a hospital stay ends at home, whether one stays self-sufficient — is paid for in the currency called "muscle." Muscle naturally drains from age 30–40 onward, and in old age the drain is **accelerating**: the faster muscle goes, the faster the risks of falling, disability, hospitalization and death rise (FACT: the association of age-related muscle loss with adverse outcomes is a geriatric-medicine consensus). That is what "muscle = the currency of aging health" means: bank principal in midlife (train), spend carefully in old age (prevent loss) — whoever overdraws pays first.

### 1.2 Sarcopenia: a preventable, reversible window

**Sarcopenia = age-related loss of muscle mass + loss of muscle strength / physical performance** (FACT: the AWGS 2019 consensus definition, Chen et al., published in J Am Med Dir Assoc 2020, cited 8000+). The key cognition: it is not the fate of being "too old to do anything" — **muscle strength still responds to training at very advanced ages**, and protein + resistance exercise is the repeatedly validated intervention pair (FACT: the PROT-AGE/ESPEN guideline-consensus direction). The Asian standard (AWGS 2019) also sets a "possible sarcopenia" tier precisely so that communities and families can **detect early and intervene early**.

### 1.3 Grip strength: a simple self-test doable at home

**Grip strength is the most practical proxy for muscle strength** (FACT: the AWGS 2019 diagnostic cutoffs):

- Men **<28 kg**, women **<18 kg** = low grip strength (AWGS 2019 cutoffs, FACT);
- The companion simple physical measure: 6-meter usual gait speed **<1.0 m/s** (AWGS 2019, FACT).

Home operation: an electronic hand dynamometer (a few dozen yuan) + a fixed posture (seated, elbow at 90 degrees, two trials per hand, take the maximum). A low reading is not a diagnosis — it is **the signal to get assessed in geriatrics / rehabilitation**. Gait self-test: measure out 6 meters at home (a hallway works), walk it with a phone stopwatch; under 4.5 seconds is good (>1.33 m/s; past 6 seconds is the warning line).

### 1.4 Protein: what older adults most often lack is not "light eating" — it is protein

The mainstream guideline framing (FACT, verified this round):

- **Healthy older adults: at least 1.0–1.2 g protein/kg body weight/day** (PROT-AGE recommendation, Deutz 2014; the ESPEN guideline on clinical nutrition in older adults, Volkert 2022: at least 1.0 g/kg/d);
- **Older adults with acute/chronic disease, malnutrition, or recovering in hospital: 1.2–1.5 g/kg/d recommended** (PROT-AGE/ESPEN, same framing; kidney-function boundaries require physician assessment);
- Best paired with resistance training; with kidney disease, protein amounts must be set by a physician (boundary, stays unfrozen).

Translated into daily terms: a healthy 60 kg older adult needs about 60–72 g protein a day ≈ 20–25 g of quality protein per meal (one egg ≈ 6–7 g; one 300 ml glass of milk ≈ 9–10 g; a 100–150 g portion of fish / lean meat / tofu (source: "2–3 liang") ≈ 15–25 g). **Splitting it across three meals beats one concentrated dose for muscle synthesis** (FACT: the PROT-AGE direction-grade recommendation).

### 1.5 "Older people should eat lightly and vegetarian" = the most popular and most harmful misconception

In the Chinese context, "light eating" as porridge + pickles + vegetables often = **severe protein deficiency**, directly accelerating muscle loss, osteoporosis, and immune decline (CONTRADICTED: the equation "light eating = healthy" fails for the very old — see §1.4's protein magnitudes and the AWGS intervention framing; the counter-example mechanism = the sarcopenia–frailty–fall chain). What should be "light" is oil, salt and sugar — never protein. Fish, meat, eggs, milk, beans: an older adult can afford to miss none of them (kidney-function-limited people excepted, per physician).

### 1.6 Resistance training: the only intervention that at once "builds muscle + protects bone + prevents falls"

- WHO framing: adults — **150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity aerobic activity weekly + muscle strengthening on at least 2 days** (FACT, WHO as relayed by ADDF, verified this round);
- For older adults, resistance work (elastic bands, body-weight squats, heel raises, lifting water bottles all count) + balance training (single-leg stance, heel-to-toe walking, tai chi) are the core ingredients of fall-prevention exercise (FACT: interlinked with the home-health canon — `home:运动防跌` cites the ESSA 2025 position statement, same framing);
- The midlife window: the muscle and bone mass built at 40–60 is old age's "opening deposit" — it is never too early to bank.

## Face 2. The osteoporosis face: the silent disease and the "fall × brittle bone" death chain

### 2.1 The silent disease

Osteoporosis **does not hurt or itch before the fracture** — hence "the silent disease." For many people, the first they learn of their bone loss is an X-ray after a slip, or a hard sit on the floor. Geriatric medicine's stance: **don't let the fracture be the first symptom**.

Diagnostic framing (textbook consensus, FACT): dual-energy X-ray (DXA) bone density T-score ≤ -2.5 = osteoporosis; -1.0 to -2.5 = low bone mass (osteopenia). The T-score is how many standard deviations your bone density sits from the young-adult peak.

### 2.2 The basic three: weight-bearing exercise + calcium + vitamin D (and their boundaries)

- **Weight-bearing exercise**: bone banks calcium only under load — brisk walking, dancing, stairs, resistance all count; swimming, for all its virtues, is not weight-bearing (boundary).
- **Calcium: women 51+ and men 71+ = 1200 mg/day (including food); below those ages, 1000 mg** (FACT: the Bone Health & Osteoporosis Foundation (BHOF, formerly NOF) framing, verified this round). **Food first**: dairy, soy, dark leafy greens, small fish with bones.
- **Vitamin D: age 50+ = 800–1000 IU/day** (FACT: the NOF framing of 800–1000 IU D3; especially important for older adults who are housebound or mostly indoors).
- **Boundary one (the honest ledger)**: USPSTF 2018 explicitly does **not** recommend low-dose supplements of ≤400 IU vitamin D + ≤1000 mg calcium for primary prevention — the dose is too small to help; whether high doses help remains insufficiently evidenced (FACT: the USPSTF statement direction, verified this round). Supplements "fill a gap"; they are not "the more you eat, the better your bones."
- **Boundary two**: calcium + vitamin D alone cannot replace exercise and cannot treat diagnosed osteoporosis — they are the foundation, not the house.

### 2.3 Screening: when to get a DXA (stays unfrozen)

USPSTF 2025 (FACT, verified this round): **women 65 and older** are recommended DXA bone-density screening; **postmenopausal women under 65** whose fracture risk equals or exceeds that of a 65-year-old white woman (low body weight, parental hip fracture, smoking, long-term steroid use, etc.) are also recommended screening. Men have no equivalent screening recommendation, but men with a fragility fracture history / long-term steroid use / hypogonadism should be assessed by a physician. **The latest local guideline and your doctor prevail (stays unfrozen).**

### 2.4 The medication face: direction grade — no prescriptions, only when to ask the doctor

Once osteoporosis is confirmed, drug classes exist with RCT-verified ability to **significantly reduce fracture risk** — chiefly the "antiresorptives" (e.g., bisphosphonates; other-mechanism classes also exist); their use and selection are prescribing-level decisions, and this canon gives only **the triggers for asking the doctor** (FACT: class effectiveness is consensus; specific-drug details UNKNOWN, not claimed):

1. DXA shows T ≤ -2.5;
2. A fragility fracture has already occurred (especially hip/vertebral) — the highest priority;
3. A FRAX-type tool puts the 10-year fracture risk above threshold;
4. Long-term glucocorticoid therapy.

The three questions to ask the doctor: how big is my fracture risk? Should I take medication or start with the basics first? While on the medication, how much extra calcium and D? (Antiresorptives carry usage conditions and side-effect monitoring — self-purchasing and self-stopping both have real costs.)

### 2.5 Fall × osteoporosis = one death chain (interlinked with the home-health canon, not re-expanded)

**One fall × a set of brittle bones = a hip fracture; 1-year mortality after hip fracture is about 15–30%** (FACT: multi-source consistent; the home-health canon has verified and archived `home:髋部骨折预后`). Both ends of this chain can be acted on:

- **The fall end (the home-health canon's territory)**: exercise lowers the fall rate **23%** (Cochrane 2019, RaR 0.77, 95% CI 0.71–0.83, 12,981 participants in 59 studies, high-certainty evidence, FACT verified this round) + home modification (grab bars / anti-slip / lighting — see `home:浴室防滑卡` and `home:居家改造证据`). Not re-expanded here.
- **The bone end (this face's territory)**: turn bone from "shatters on one fall" into "takes a beating" — the basic three, plus medication when medication is due.
- **The combination is the answer**: guarding falls while ignoring bone, or supplementing calcium while ignoring falls — each is a one-legged strategy worth half a life. The sedative/hypnotic/antihypertensive/hypoglycemic × fall cross sits in `home:镇静药跌倒交叉` and this canon's Face 4.

## Face 3. The cognitive-decline prevention face: ≈45% of dementia is linked to modifiable factors

### 3.1 The Lancet Commission 2024: 14 modifiable risk factors (every number here search-verified this round)

The Lancet Commission 2024 report (Livingston et al., Lancet 2024, DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(24)01296-0), core conclusions (FACT, verified this round):

- **About 45% of dementia cases** are associated with 14 modifiable risk factors — addressing them across the life course could theoretically prevent or delay them;
- Two factors added in 2024: **uncorrected vision loss** and **high LDL cholesterol** (together associated with about 9% of cases);
- The largest single-factor population attributable fractions (PAF) (FACT): **hearing loss 7% · high LDL 7% · early-life low education 5% · late-life social isolation 5%**; the remaining factors (depression, traumatic brain injury, hypertension, obesity, smoking, excess alcohol, physical inactivity, diabetes, air pollution) complete the 45%;
- **Life-stage stratification** (FACT): early life (<18) = education; midlife (18–65) = hearing, LDL, depression, brain injury, hypertension, obesity, smoking, excess alcohol, inactivity, diabetes; later life (65+) = social isolation, air pollution, vision loss.

Three specific numbers people often ask about (FACT, verified this round): each 10 dB of hearing loss raises dementia risk 4–24%; the common threshold for excess alcohol is >21 units of alcohol per week (1 unit = 8 g pure alcohol); traumatic brain injury advances dementia by about 2–3 years.

### 3.2 Hearing: the most underrated value-for-money line on the list

Observational evidence: hearing loss is stably associated with dementia (7% PAF above, FACT); mechanism candidates include cognitive load and social isolation (INFERENCE). **Intervention evidence (FACT, verified this round)**: the ACHIEVE randomized trial (Lin et al., Lancet 2023, 977 adults 75+ with hearing loss, 3 years) — in the **overall population**, no significant benefit for cognitive decline; but in the **prespecified higher-risk subgroup** (ARIC cohort enrollees), the hearing intervention **slowed 3-year cognitive decline by about 48%**. The honest reading: this is evidence "for higher-risk older adults, in a 3-year window, it works" — not a universal slogan that "hearing aids prevent dementia"; yet combined with the observational evidence and the low risk, **getting hearing loss aided with hearing aids** stands on its own as a valid action (hearing and fitting: the senses canon — `sens:access-hearing`, the trial anchor `sens:achieve-2023`; not re-expanded here).

### 3.3 The "brain-boosting" drugs and supplements: no evidence face

A checkable fact (INFERENCE, derived from this round's verified sources): **the Lancet Commission 2024's list of 14 modifiable factors contains not a single supplement or "brain booster."** Vendor scripts (ginkgo, DHA, vitamin E, all manner of "brain-tonic oral liquids") have to this day never once entered an authoritative prevention recommendation. The right posture when an older relative is being pitched: swap the supplement money for two resistance-training days a week plus one old-friends gathering (the latter maps to social isolation's 5% PAF) — both are on the list; the supplements are not.

### 3.4 Subjective cognitive decline: when to get assessed

When you yourself, or those close to you, repeatedly notice "memory clearly getting worse" (asking the same question again and again, arithmetic getting harder, getting lost in familiar places) = subjective cognitive decline (SCD). The action framing (INFERENCE direction grade; the assessment pathway defers to physicians):

- **Worth assessing**: age 60+ with SCD steadily progressing, or with executive-function slips (money management, cooking, medication errors), or when the person's own anxiety impairs life — go to a memory clinic / neurology for a baseline assessment;
- **The point of assessment is not "diagnosing dementia"**: most SCD is not dementia; the value is ① screening **reversible causes** (B12 deficiency, thyroid dysfunction, depression, sleep apnea, drug side effects — see Face 4) ② establishing a baseline to compare against later;
- **Red flags**: changes observed by family are more reliable than self-report; personality change, sundowning-like behavior, clearly failing judgment — do not wait until it "gets serious" to check.

## Face 4. The polypharmacy face: "more and more pills" — losing control and taking it back

### 4.1 The five principles of older-adult medication (plain-words edition)

1. **Every drug, you can say its purpose** — a drug whose purpose you can't state is risk taken for nothing (ask at the next visit until you can);
2. **New symptom, think drug first** — for an older adult's "new problem" (dizziness, falls, constipation, confusion, poor appetite), the first suspect is often not a new disease but an old drug (INFERENCE direction grade; the geriatric-medicine consensus posture);
3. **Never add, cut or stop on your own** — especially sleep drugs, antihypertensives and anticoagulants: abrupt stops can be catastrophic; all adjustments go through the doctor;
4. **One master list** — bring **all** the pill boxes + supplements to every visit so one physician (family doctor / geriatrician) can see the whole picture; multiple specialists prescribing blindly of each other = the standard path by which polypharmacy happens;
5. **Subtract on a schedule** — at every follow-up, ask: "of these drugs, which can be stopped or reduced?"

(The five principles are the household translation of the geriatric prescribing consensus "start low, go slow" and the medication-review culture — INFERENCE / consensus grade.)

### 4.2 Deprescribing: a word that belongs in every family's dictionary

**Deprescribing = the planned, physician-guided tapering and stopping of drugs whose benefit has fallen below their risk** (FACT: the concept is an international consensus, with a systematic family of deprescribing guidelines). It is not "the doctor refuses to treat" — it is managing the drug list as a living list: an older adult's liver and kidney function change, so the drug list must not be frozen in place.

### 4.3 PPIs (the "-prazole" stomach drugs): the most common deprescribable example

The evidence face verified this round (FACT):

- At any moment about **7–15%** of adults take a PPI, and **among people 65+ the usage rate is about 40%**;
- About **22–63%** of long-term PPI use **exceeds the necessary duration** (continuation without a clear long-term indication);
- Long-term PPI exposure shows **association signals** with raised fracture risk and raised all-cause mortality in meta-analyses (observational evidence = signal grade, not a verdict);
- Clinical practice guidelines already support: after a mild indication has run its 4-week course, de-escalation/stop should be attempted.

The family action: open the older adult's medicine box — if a "-prazole" has been taken continuously for over half a year and the original indication was occasional heartburn — **write it down and ask at the next visit: "does this drug still need to be taken all the time?"** Stopping a PPI abruptly rebounds; tapering must be guided by a physician (boundary, stays unfrozen).

### 4.4 The family medication-review checklist for "more and more pills" (twice a year)

1. Lay out all pill boxes + supplements; photograph and build the list (drug name / dose / who prescribed / why);
2. Mark the drugs "whose purpose you can't state" → onto the questions list;
3. Mark recent new symptoms (dizziness / falls / constipation / drowsiness / poor appetite) → onto the "think drug first" list;
4. Find duplicated ingredients across clinics (two combination cold medicines = double the paracetamol; two departments each prescribing something sleep-inducing);
5. Take the list to one medication-review visit (family doctor / geriatrician / pharmacist); the goal = subtraction;
6. After the review, update the list + the home emergency card (see §6.3); archive the old list.

## Face 5. The geriatric-syndrome face: frailty is a reversible window; rapid weight loss is a red flag

### 5.1 Frailty: measurable in medicine, reversible inside the window

**The Fried frailty phenotype (2001, FACT)**: ≥3 of the following 5 = frail; 1–2 = pre-frail — ① unintentional weight loss in the past year (on the order of 10 lb / 4.5 kg, i.e., >5% of body weight) ② exhaustion (things feel effortful) ③ weak grip strength ④ slow gait speed ⑤ low physical activity. The evidence face: frailty is not a one-way street — **the phenotypic states convert in both directions** (including recovery toward non-frail, FACT: cohort transition studies, e.g., Romero-Ortuno 2021). In plain words: **the pre-frail window is reversible** — and the interventions are precisely Face 1 (protein + resistance) and this face's nutrition chain.

### 5.2 Rapid weight loss = the highest-priority red flag

**Unintentional weight loss >5% in 6 months (or 10% in 1 year) = a must-see-the-doctor red flag** (FACT: the standard geriatric framing; unintentional weight loss affects 15–20% of people 65+ in home/institution populations, and the first-tier differential includes malignancy, depression, hyperthyroidism, drugs, and swallowing/dental problems — the classic-review framing of Alibhai 2005, direction grade). The most practical home dashboard: **weigh once a week, same time, same scale, mark it on a calendar**. Older adults often say "thinner feels lighter" — unintentional thinness in old age is not lightness; it is an alarm.

### 5.3 Sleep change: the older adult's sleep is not just "sleeps less"

Age-related physiological sleep change (earlier sleep onset, fragmentation, early waking) is the norm — but **abrupt change and sleep trouble with impaired daytime function is not** (INFERENCE direction grade). The home workup order: night-urination frequency; snoring and apnea suspicion; nighttime leg movements; daytime sleepiness and its link to falls; newly changed drugs; hours spent lying in bed by day (the more bed time by day, the more shredded the night — sleep pressure is conserved; see the insomnia canon interlink `ins:elderly_normal_segmentation` · amended 2026-09-13). Sleep worsening persisting >1 month is handled at the same tier as rapid weight loss: see a doctor; do not self-purchase sleeping pills (sleep drugs × falls: `home:镇静药跌倒交叉`).

### 5.4 Constipation / swallowing / oral: the three gates of the nutrition chain (interlinked with the oral-health canon)

Older-adult malnutrition is often not "nothing to eat" but "can't get it down, can't swallow it well, can't chew it" — three gates on the nutrition chain (INFERENCE as an integrative framework):

1. **The oral gate**: missing teeth / ill-fitting dentures / toothache → can't chew meat and firm food → protein falls straight off the line. **"Losing teeth with age is inevitable" is a falsification-face rumor** (§7); teeth are organs, not consumables — see the oral canon (`oral:保牙拔牙决策树` is its decision anchor; the older-adult tooth-loss–nutrition topic has no corresponding node yet · the nearest face `oral:咀嚼×认知` exists and may serve · original anchor amended 2026-09-13 · original id in the receipt);
2. **The swallowing gate**: choking on water, meals getting slower, recurrent pneumonia — signals of dysphagia; a swallowing assessment is due (feeding adjustment is a technical skill, not the three words "cook it into mush");
3. **The constipation gate**: chronic constipation is both a result of poor nutrition and a cause of poor appetite, and is often a drug side effect (opioids, anticholinergics, calcium, iron) — review the drug list first (Face 4), then adjust fiber / water / exercise; the stubborn cases see a doctor.

Any one gate stuck for over 2–4 weeks + weight falling = the same care pathway (geriatrics / rehabilitation), because all three converge on one outcome: sarcopenia and frailty (Face 1 / this face, closed loop).

## Face 6. The caregiver face: the overlooked core variable

### 6.1 The caregiver's health is not a supporting role — it is the system's power supply

**Evidence anchor (FACT, verified this round)**: Schulz & Beach 1999 (JAMA) — **older spouse caregivers who reported caregiving strain (psychological and physical tension) had a 63% higher risk of death over 4 years of follow-up than non-caregivers**, and strained caregiving was an **independent** risk factor. Translation: the precondition for caring well for the older adult is that the caregiver is alive and healthy; the caregiver falling = the cared-for instantly losing the entire support system. So the caregiver's checkups, sleep, exercise and medical care all carry a priority equal to the cared-for's nursing level.

Self-monitoring of family caregiving load (INFERENCE): providing care ≥5 days a week continuously + self-rating "close to breaking" + somatic symptoms already present (insomnia / headaches / rising blood pressure) = an overload signal — trigger the actions below.

### 6.2 Respite care: not selfishness — maintenance

**Respite care = letting the caregiver take scheduled, guilt-free pauses** — day-care centers, short-term facility stays, hourly aide substitutions, relatives-and-friends rotation weeks (FACT: the concept is a standard component of international care systems; supply and reimbursement vary hugely by locale — stays unfrozen, the local civil-affairs/medical-insurance framing prevails). The psychological crux: many caregivers experience "being relieved" as "neglecting duty" — the canon's stance: respite = equipment maintenance; equipment left unmaintained burns down together with the person and the system (the 63% in §6.1 is the meter reading on that burning).

### 6.3 Long-distance caregiving: the remote checklist (the minimal viable plan for children living away)

1. **One emergency card** on the fridge: diagnoses, medication list, allergies, the treating physician's phone, neighbor/relative emergency contacts (maintained from the same source as the §4.4 list);
2. **One local person**: a neighbor / relative / community grid worker who can reach the door within 30 minutes, whose name and number the older adult's phone can actually hold;
3. **One remote observation set**: video calls at fixed times (look at complexion / how tidy the room is / whether the pill box moved — not just "are you fine"); a scale and blood-pressure cuff with Bluetooth upload, or a habit of photographing the readings;
4. **One care channel**: online appointment booking at the local hospital by proxy + cross-region medical-insurance filing (policy stays unfrozen; the local insurance framing prevails) + one accompanied visit to establish the doctor relationship;
5. **One alarm closed loop**: fall alarms / emergency-call devices — after the alarm, **who** picks up and **who** walks through the door must have been drilled once to count (interlinked with home safety);
6. **The financial and authorization face**: arrangements for healthcare authorization / banking matters (the two topics of long-term-care financing and caregiving's career impact: the corresponding graph has no same-referent node yet · pending placement · original anchor amended 2026-09-13 · original id in the receipt).

### 6.4 Advance care decisions and palliative care: not giving up treatment — changing the goal

Concept clarification (FACT: at the concept-definition level):

- **Advance care decisions / advance directives**: while still holding decision capacity, the person states in advance "if a certain situation arises in the future, I do / do not want which kinds of treatment" — this exercises the person's own autonomy; it is not the family "deciding to give up" on their behalf;
- **Palliative care**: active medical care aimed at relieving symptoms and improving quality of life — analgesia, antiemesis, breathlessness management, psychological and family support are all its treatment content; it **does not equal** hospice (hospice is its late-phase form), and even less **does it equal** "no longer treating";
- **The right phrase is "goal conversion"**: when the disease trajectory enters the stage where comfort and dignity are the optimal goals, the treatment goal turns from "extending survival time" to "making the time that remains as good as possible" — this is a legitimate position on the spectrum of medical decisions, not a failure, and not abandonment. The thing families should do earliest: open this conversation while the older adult still has decision capacity (the difficulty of talking now is far smaller than the difficulty of guessing on their behalf later). The domestic implementation forms (living-will legislation / palliative-care pilots) are evolving fast — **stays unfrozen; the latest local regulations and hospitals prevail**.

## Face 7. The falsification face: eight aging-health rumors, cleared one by one

| # | Claim | Verdict | Falsification points |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Brain-health supplements prevent dementia / boost the brain" | CONTRADICTED (no evidence face) | The Lancet 2024 list of 14 modifiable factors contains **no supplement of any kind** (FACT); a supplement's "health-food" status is a regulatory classification, not an efficacy promise; swap the money for resistance + socializing + hearing intervention — all on the list |
| 2 | "The thinner the older adult, the better" | CONTRADICTED | The reverse is truer: low body weight / rapid weight loss in old age marks rising risks of muscle loss, frailty and death (the §5.2 red flag); what old age pursues is **muscle mass + function**, not the scale number; "money can't buy a thin old age" should read "money can't buy a strong old age" |
| 3 | "IV infusions to 'clear the blood vessels' prevent stroke" | CONTRADICTED (evidence absent + guidelines silent) | No authoritative stroke/cardiovascular guideline recommends periodic infusions to "clear vessels" for stroke prevention (INFERENCE: evidence-of-absence-grade falsification; per-agent RCT blanks pending dedicated verification = UNKNOWN); the real "clearing of vessels" is blood pressure / lipids / glucose / smoking management — LDL is precisely a Lancet 2024 dementia factor (§3.1); the seasonal prevention infusion's position = zero |
| 4 | "Older people don't need that much protein" | CONTRADICTED | Precisely backwards: older adults need **more** protein than the middle-aged — declining synthesis efficiency plus insufficient intake squeezes from both sides (§1.4: healthy 1.0–1.2, ill/frail 1.2–1.5 g/kg/d, FACT); "light eating = porridge + pickles" is a muscle-loss accelerator |
| 5 | "Losing teeth with age is inevitable" | CONTRADICTED | The main drivers of tooth loss are caries and periodontal disease (preventable and treatable), not age itself; missing teeth cut the nutrition chain's oral gate (§5.4) → muscle loss; see the oral canon `oral:保牙拔牙决策树` — tooth-keeping has a decision tree, tooth loss has replacements; the fatalism fails |
| 6 | "Calcium supplements make bones brittle / cause stones" | CONTRADICTED (a tangled claim, untangled) | The fracture-risk association signal attaches to **long-term PPI stomach drugs** (§4.3), not to calcium; guideline-scale calcium + vitamin D (§2.2) is a baseline item in every country's guidelines; the "brittle bones" narrative mixes up two different ledgers; honest boundaries: excess supplementation has no benefit (USPSTF does not recommend low doses), and for anyone with a kidney-stone history the calcium plan is set by a physician |
| 7 | "Light vegetarian eating as longevity practice" — for older adults | CONTRADICTED | See §1.5 — protein deficiency = the sarcopenia–frailty–fall chain accelerated; what gets "light" is oil/salt/sugar, never protein |
| 8 | "Older adults naturally sleep less — ignore it" | Split verdict | Physiological shortening = the norm; **abrupt change + impaired daytime function + sleepiness-driven falls = not normal** (§5.3); self-medicating with sleeping pills imports fall risk (`home:镇静药跌倒交叉`) |
