# Care Access Canon v1 (2026-09-12)

> **⚠️ Header declaration**: this volume is **educational care-navigation information**, studying "how to seek care" as a skill in itself — which department to choose, how to describe symptoms, how to understand the doctor, how to use medical insurance, when to get a second opinion. **It is not medical advice**: diagnosis and treatment decisions always belong to the face-to-face clinical process between clinician and patient. Zero specific hospitals, zero doctors, zero commercial products (including insurance / crowdfunding platforms) recommended.
> **Topic source**: entry #17 of the human-needs full-spectrum mapping compass (`docs/healing/human-needs-map-v1-20260912.md` §3-17) — the L2 safety layer's "insurance and care access" blank slot. A dozen-odd canons in the library write "seek care" in their red lines, but **how to seek care itself is studied by no one in the library**: red flags tell you "seek care immediately"; this volume answers "once there, how do you make the visit actually useful".
> **One-line stance**: seeking care is a learnable skill, not a talent. How you describe symptoms, the quality of your questions, whether you dare ask again when you didn't understand, whether to ask one more doctor before a big decision — these "last mile" micro-differences are repeatedly proven by the evidence face to change diagnostic quality and treatment outcomes. This volume lays that last mile open.
> **For English-reading audiences**: the pathways below are named inside mainland China's institutional frame as the source wrote them — outside that frame, follow your local actual resources *(translator calibration)*.

## 1. The care-pathway face: how households choose

### 1.1 Triage logic: the real division of labor under primary care's absence (MODEL)

- **The textbook framework** (MODEL · policy face): mainland China's tiered healthcare delivery is designed as "primary-level first contact · two-way referral · acute/chronic separation · upper–lower coordination" — community health service centers / township health centers handle common illnesses, chronic-disease follow-up, prescriptions and referrals; Class-2 and Class-3 hospitals handle complex and acute-severe cases. **This framework describes institutional intent, not the current reality** (actual household care behavior commonly "skips the grassroots and heads straight for Class-3A" — widely recorded in the social-survey face; direction FACT, mechanism INFERENCE).
- **The real reasons for skipping the grassroots** (INFERENCE · educational summary): ① distrust of grassroots diagnostic ability (historically uneven, true) ② the one-stop habit of chronic-disease prescriptions at Class-3A hospitals ③ some tests/medicines incomplete at the grassroots ④ the scarcity feeling of hard-to-get appointments amplifying the "only a big hospital reassures" psychology. Of these, ④ is scarcity-mindset tunneling's care-seeking edition (aligned with `financial-health-canon-v1-20260912.md` §2.1).
- **The systemic cost of the mild-cases crowding Class-3A** (INFERENCE · multiple lines agree): per-visit time at Class-3A is compressed (2 hours waiting, a few minutes of consultation is the routine description) → communication quality squeezed by time (§2.6's evidence face: communication quality is exactly the outcomes lever) → queue cross-infection risk (respiratory season) → out-of-pocket share usually higher than at the grassroots (reimbursement ratios generally tilt toward the grassroots · specific ratios per local official sources) → genuinely acute-severe patients mixed-stream with mild ones. **The cost is not a moral accusation; it is a structural ledger**: everyone pays for "everyone crowding".
- **The pragmatic division-of-labor strategy** (INFERENCE · educational navigation, not mandatory):
  - **Community / grassroots fits**: routine follow-up and prescriptions for diagnosed chronic diseases, checkup-report interpretation, mild-case first screening (common colds / small wounds / minor skin problems), vaccines, family-doctor contracted services.
  - **Class-2/3 outpatient fits**: new persistent symptoms needing a clear diagnosis, post-grassroots-screening referrals, specialty follow-ups, needs for specific tests.
  - **Class-3A fits**: unresolved complex diagnoses, before big decisions (surgery / long-term medication plans), rare diseases, multi-morbidity needing multi-department coordination, grassroots repeatedly ineffective.
  - **This tree is "how to choose so you're more likely to be properly caught", not a moral judgment of "where you ought to go".**

### 1.2 The department navigation tree: symptom → likely department (educational navigation · INFERENCE)

> **Framing**: one symptom mapping to multiple departments is the norm; this tree gives only "the most common starting point + high-frequency mis-booking reminders". **When unsure, go straight to the hospital's triage desk / information desk and ask** — a triage organ the hospital provides for free, more authoritative than any navigation tree (INFERENCE · this tree can be overruled by the triage desk at any time).

| Symptom | Common starting department | High-frequency mis-booking and reminders |
|---|---|---|
| Chest pain (crushing / radiating to arm or jaw) | **red flags first**: crushing feeling / radiation / with cold sweat → **emergency department** (red flag #1); non-acute chest pain → cardiology | mis-booked to gastroenterology (assumed stomach), delaying the cardiac call; burning pain related to meals is indeed often reflux (gastroenterology), but **new chest pain in middle-aged/older adults rules cardiac out first** — the safe side |
| Dizziness | recurrent rotational vertigo (room-spinning, turn-of-the-head related) → ENT or neurology both viable starting points | the high-frequency mis-booking = booking only a "brain" department: peripheral causes (BPPV / vestibular) are actually more common than central ones (MODEL · epidemiological direction); **with slurred speech / weakness / double vision → emergency department** (red flag #2) |
| Palpitations | cardiology (bring an ECG); with hand tremor / weight loss / heat intolerance → endocrinology (thyroid) | the mis-booking = self-reassurance as "just an arrhythmia"; anxiety-related palpitations are a diagnosis of exclusion — **exclude organic first, then consider functional; the order never flips** |
| Headache | neurology; **the worst headache of one's life / thunderclap → emergency department** (red flag #4) | long-term fixed same-side + press-triggered ≠ brain-tumor logic; analgesics >10–15 days/month instead cause medication-overuse headache (symptom-textbook red line) |
| Abdominal pain | gastroenterology; persistent worsening / board-like rigidity → **emergency department** (red flag #6) | high-frequency blind spot: lower-abdominal pain in women ≠ all gastroenterology — gynecology / urology are legitimate starting points; fixed right-lower-quadrant pain (appendix direction): don't wait in a long outpatient queue |
| Joint pain | rheumatology & immunology (multi-joint / morning stiffness / swelling) vs orthopedics (single joint / trauma / activity pain) | the mis-booking = "just take calcium" across the board; morning stiffness >30 minutes + symmetric small-joint swelling is the rheumatology face's questioning focus (edu/arthritis popular-science volume, same framing) |
| Unexplained weight loss | endocrinology or gastroenterology starting point, oncology workup as needed | the misconception = "losing weight is good news" — unintentional loss >5% in magnitude within six months is the accepted workup line (MODEL · magnitude framing · clinician judgment prevails) |
| Persistent low mood / insomnia / somatic complaints | psychiatry / clinical psychology | **the highest-frequency mis-booking**: repeated laps through neurology / cardiology / gastroenterology hunting a "body disease", circling several times before reaching the right department (the typical structure of care delay · INFERENCE); in regions without psychiatry, some general hospitals' "psychosomatic medicine" departments serve the same function |
| Abnormal blood glucose / blood pressure | endocrinology / cardiovascular medicine; stable follow-up after initial diagnosis can return to the grassroots | the mis-booking = booking "general internal medicine" and switching doctors repeatedly, causing plan drift; fixed department and fixed doctor at follow-up better keeps the plan coherent (INFERENCE) |

### 1.3 Emergency vs outpatient vs evening clinic / internet hospital: usage criteria (INFERENCE · linked to the red-flag system)

> **The red-flag layer (cannot wait · minutes-scale)** directly cites PG `healing_red_flags_en_v1` (10 rows) and each canon's red-line column; this volume does not rebuild it, only hooks in (the §6 interlink table). Here are the **middle-layer criteria**:

| Layer | Criterion (direction-grade) | Time expectation |
|---|---|---|
| Emergency department / 120 | red-flag signals of unstable vital signs: the full red-flag #1–#10 set + accidental injury + altered consciousness | now |
| Emergency department (no 120) | clear emergency but stable vital signs: infants and young children with fever >39°C, severe vomiting with dehydration, acute trauma, foreign bodies | the next few hours |
| Evening clinic / extended-hours clinic | needs same-day handling but not an emergency: new symptoms needing tests ordered, cannot leave work by day | that evening |
| Internet hospital / online follow-up | **follow-up re-prescriptions for diagnosed chronic diseases, report interpretation** — not for initial visits / emergencies / anything needing physical examination (a screen cannot palpate an abdomen) | hours to days |
| Ordinary outpatient | any diagnosis/treatment need that can wait 1–2 weeks | the booking cycle |
| Checkup / screening | asymptomatic people — go through `healing_screening_schedule_v1` (66 rows) rather than crowding outpatient clinics | the booking cycle |

- **The one-sentence "can wait / cannot wait" criterion** (INFERENCE · educational): **does the symptom involve consciousness / breathing / circulation / severe pain / bleeding / sudden neurological signs** — any hit = cannot wait; purely "uncomfortable but functioning, eating and sleeping fine, stable vital signs, gradual symptoms" = mostly can wait (but **progressive worsening itself** is a reason to seek care; do not delay weeks on "it can wait" — care delay is the recurring negative line across the canons).
- **UNKNOWN**: mainland coverage and quality distribution of evening clinics and internet hospitals — not systematically surveyed in this volume; no claims.

## 2. The communication face: making a visit actually useful

### 2.1 The evidence face: clinician–patient communication quality × outcomes (MODEL)

- **Direction-grade conclusion** (MODEL · meta-grade evidence direction verified, precise effects anchor-pending): multiple metas support the robust association of communication quality with adherence and health outcomes — poor communication (patient didn't understand / wasn't heard / information asymmetry) → adherence drops, outcomes worsen, disputes rise. Zolnierek & DiMatteo 2009's adherence review is the commonly cited anchor (bibliographic level · precise OR anchor-pending).
- **Read in reverse** (INFERENCE): the patient doing three things well — "describe the symptoms clearly + ask the questions out + confirm understanding" — is itself an **operable intervention** on treatment outcomes; it costs nothing and is this volume's highest-value-per-cost face.
- **The Chinese-context structural constraint** (INFERENCE): short per-visit consultation time is a structural reality — communication strategy must be designed as "completable within a few minutes"; that is why the two cards below exist.

### 2.2 How to describe symptoms: the five-sentence method (a plain-language version of the OAPQR-class framework · INFERENCE)

> OAPQR/SOAPE are clinical-charting frameworks (Onset / Palliation-Provocation / Quality / Region / Severity / Timing); patients need not memorize acronyms — the plain-language five sentences:

1. **Where**: the specific site + where it radiates ("right lower abdomen", "the chest pain shoots into the inner left arm").
2. **What it feels like**: pick from quality words — stabbing / bloated / crushing / burning / colicky / dull aching / tingling numbness (quality words are high-value input for differential diagnosis: "crushing" and "needle-pricking" point in completely different directions).
3. **How long + how it changes**: when it started, continuous/intermittent, worsening or easing ("started three days ago, getting more frequent").
4. **What makes it worse/better**: after eating, on exertion, lying flat, on pressure (triggers and relievers = the fork points of differential diagnosis).
5. **History + accompaniments**: old illnesses, medicines currently taken (**bring the boxes or photos** — far more information than "I take a blood-pressure pill"), new accompaniments this time (fever / weight loss / pain waking you at night).

- **The visit carrying list** (INFERENCE · zero cost, high return): insurance card / ID, all medicine boxes or a list of everything currently taken, prior test reports and discharge summaries (time-ordered), a symptom timeline (written on paper beforehand — dictation in the consult room drops information; a written timeline is the cheapest anti-forgetting device).
- **The courteous early cut** (INFERENCE): a doctor interrupting your narration is a time-structure issue, not an attitude issue — the strategy is "hand over the written timeline first, then let the doctor ask"; paper resists interruption better than speech.

### 2.3 Four questions for the doctor (the four-question card · INFERENCE · educational framework)

> At each diagnosis or medication change, the four questions in order usually take under two minutes:

1. **"What is my condition? How was it determined?"** — get the diagnosis name, then the basis (which tests support it · what else could it be). "Could it be something else?" is a legitimate question, not an attack on the doctor.
2. **"Why this medicine / this test?"** — you want the basis and the purpose (treating the cause or the symptom? Will this test change the treatment decision?).
3. **"What are the alternatives?"** — including **not treating / delaying treatment** as an option (conservative observation is also a plan); the alternatives face turns the decision from "receiving instructions" into "informed choice".
4. **"What side effects? What situations mean come back immediately?"** — red flags up front: get "what signal = return / emergency" clarified and write it on the medicine box.

- **One more question at follow-up**: "I developed XX on the medicine — is it related to the drug?" — do not self-adjudge side effects online and stop the drug directly (§5-4 misconception).
- **Red line**: the four-question card is not for adversarial "doctor-examining"; the goal is information symmetry, not audit. When communication breaks down, changing doctors is a legitimate right (INFERENCE · patients have the right to choose · educational).

### 2.4 When you didn't understand: the repeat-back confirmation method (teach-back, patient side · MODEL)

- **The mechanism**: medical language's default output is jargon — "sinus arrhythmia", "benign lesion", "follow-up and observe" are precise concepts to the doctor and possibly catastrophic or falsely comforting misreads to the patient. **The repeat-back method**: say the doctor's orders back in your own words ("So I take one tablet every morning, come back in two weeks for a liver test, and come immediately if my eyes turn yellow — did I get that right?"), and let the doctor correct it.
- **The evidence face** (MODEL): teach-back as a patient-comprehension intervention has educational-research support (direction-grade · exact effect size anchor-pending); the plain-language-in-medicine movement's basic conclusion agrees: **"say it back to me" catches an order of magnitude more comprehension errors than "did you understand?"** (direction-grade INFERENCE).
- **In practice** (INFERENCE): the golden three questions before leaving the room — ① how to take this medicine (dose / timing / before or after meals) ② follow-up time and tests ③ what situations mean come back early. Any one unanswered = raise your hand and ask again. **No one is harmed by asking once more; only those who took the wrong medicine because they didn't understand are harmed.**

### 2.5 Medical translation: bringing family and the boundaries of recording (as-is · INFERENCE / legal-information face)

- **Bringing family** (INFERENCE · high value, low risk): in the consult room the patient is often in a stress state — reduced information intake is a physiological result of stress, not a psychological weakness. Bringing one family member as "a second pair of ears" (note-taking + post-visit repeat-back) is a normal component of patient care, accepted in the vast majority of consult rooms. **In elderly / serious-illness / big-decision scenarios, bringing family is nearly a necessity** (informed-consent signing usually involves it too).
- **Recording** (legal-information face · layered as-is):
  - A patient recording for the purpose of documenting their own diagnosis and treatment generally does not violate the doctor's privacy (the setting is an open clinical activity, the content concerns the patient) — **direction-grade INFERENCE; local cases and institutional management rules vary; this volume is not legal advice**.
  - As etiquette, **announce before recording** ("Doctor, may I record this to play for my family?") is the optimal solution in the vast majority of scenarios: the announcement costs almost nothing, and announced recordings carry higher credibility in later disputes.
  - A recording's evidentiary force is determined case by case by the court (authenticity / relevance / how obtained) — the UNKNOWN-to-INFERENCE boundary; consult a lawyer per case.
  - **Red line**: recording must not disrupt clinical order or leak others' (other patients') privacy; a "record it as a weapon" mindset poisons communication — the legitimate purpose of recording is "to play it back at home", not "to hold evidence" (isomorphic with §2.4).

### 2.6 A negative observation on the communication face (INFERENCE · educational)

"Pulling connections", "giving red envelopes", "ingratiating manner" are not the communication face; they are substitutes under absent trust (§5-5 falsification); **the real communication-face investment is the written timeline + the four questions + repeat-back confirmation** — cheap, legal, replicable, and landing directly on §2.1's outcomes evidence chain.

## 3. The decision face: ask one more doctor before big decisions

### 3.1 Second opinion: when to seek one (FACT/MODEL layered)

- **The external evidence trunk** (FACT · title/abstract-level verification 2026-09-12):
  - **Ruetters et al. 2016**, "Is there evidence for a better health care for cancer patients after a second opinion? A systematic review", *J Cancer Res Clin Oncol* 142(7):1521-1528 (PMID 26687791 · DOI 10.1007/s00432-015-2099-7 · Europe PMC verified) — after an oncology second opinion, **the proportion of cases in which the diagnosis / treatment recommendation / prognosis changed was 12–69% (varying by included study · original abstract sentence)**; the share of original recommendations confirmed varies widely between studies.
  - **Hillen et al. 2017**, "Patient-Driven Second Opinions in Oncology: A Critical Review", *The Oncologist* (PMC5634767 · search bibliographic-level verification) — reported seek-rate ranges **1%–88%** (extreme variation between studies); higher education level is the most consistently correlated variable with seeking a second opinion.
  - Institutional face (not peer-reviewed · framing marked): large cancer centers' self-published data claim about 1/3 of second-opinion patients had treatment adjusted (mostly toward de-escalation) — **institution self-reported data · cite with caution**.
- **The interpretive framework** (MODEL): the 12–69% "change rate" does not mean "the first doctor was wrong 12–69% of the time" — oncology has a genuine plan space (the same stage can have multiple legitimate plans); what a second opinion changes is often the **choice of plan**, not right vs wrong. Reading the "change rate" as an "error rate" is this topic's highest-frequency misreading (INFERENCE).
- **When it's worth getting** (INFERENCE · educational criteria, not directives):
  1. **Before big decisions**: major surgery, organ removal, long-term expensive medication, chemo/radiotherapy plans — the higher the cost of changing a decision, the higher a second opinion's expected value.
  2. **Doubtful diagnosis or rare disease**: treatment response mismatched to expectation, insufficient diagnostic basis, rare diseases (the rare-disease diagnostic pathway itself depends on multi-center opinions · UNKNOWN, details not claimed).
  3. **Feeling rushed**: when a "must decide tomorrow" urgency coexists with a big decision, that is a legitimate signal to request a second opinion (most oncology decisions' real time window allows days to weeks of confirmation · MODEL · the attending physician's assessment prevails).
  4. **Persistent unease of your own**: a patient's intuitive unease is itself a legitimate decision input (not hypochondria — kept strictly apart from §5-4's unbounded search anxiety).
- **Chinese-context practice** (INFERENCE): book the same specialty at another hospital and bring the complete pathology slides / original imaging (films or cloud imaging + reports); a second opinion usually **does not require repeating invasive tests** — bringing the complete set itself reduces duplicate testing and cost. Manners: no need to hide it from the first doctor (a legitimate right · educational); pathology is the gold standard, and **a pathology review (borrowed slides / consensus review) changes conclusions more than asking again in an outpatient room** (MODEL · pathology as the gold standard of tumor diagnosis is a textbook FACT).

### 3.2 What informed consent actually means (MODEL / educational)

- **A consent form ≠ a liability waiver** (MODEL · educational clarification): the legal and ethical substance of informed consent is the three-element process of **the doctor's adequate explanation + the patient's understanding + voluntary choice**, not a contractual exemption of "signed = the hospital bears no responsibility". Complications not explained before signing, or execution deviating from the instructions, are not automatically exempted by the signature (direction-grade · case-by-case variation honestly acknowledged · this volume is not legal advice).
- **The patient's four corresponding rights** (educational framework · INFERENCE): to know (diagnosis / plan / risks / costs), to choose (including conservative plans), to refuse (including partial refusal), to question to the end (the decision version of §2.3's four questions).
- **The continuing-process clause**: consent is a process, not an instant — when the intra-operative plan changes, when drugs are switched, when the treatment path changes, re-explanation and re-confirmation are the continuation of the same ethical requirement (MODEL · general principles of GCP and clinical norms · educational).

### 3.3 Clinical-trial enrollment: the information face (educational · latest official platforms prevail)

- **Official information entry points** (structural pointers · **latest official platforms prevail**): the Drug Clinical Trial Registration and Information Publicity Platform of the Center for Drug Evaluation (CDE, National Medical Products Administration) registers domestic drug clinical trials; the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR) registers multiple study types. **These platforms are the public face for "checking whether a trial exists"**; enrollment itself must pass the investigator's in-person evaluation.
- **The real logic of enrollment** (MODEL): trials have strict inclusion/exclusion criteria (stage / prior treatment / organ function); "rushing into a trial in desperation" is neither feasible nor safe; informed consent has dedicated clauses under the GCP framework (withdraw at any time without affecting ordinary treatment · educational).
- **Risk identification** (INFERENCE): any "paid enrollment" or "guaranteed efficacy" wording is a red flag — a properly run trial does not charge participants for trial drugs or trial-related tests (GCP general principles · educational · the protocol and consent form prevail).

## 4. The cost face: insurance logic and the money question

> **Master framing**: this section teaches **structure and logic only** (the geometry of reimbursement); all ratios, amounts and catalog versions — **latest official releases prevail** (the National Healthcare Security Administration site / the national medical insurance service app are the authoritative channels · no number frozen as of 2026-09-12).

### 4.1 Insurance reimbursement in plain language (MODEL · institutional structure)

- **Three layers decide what you pay** (structural FACT · numbers per official sources):
  1. **The catalog**: drugs split into Class A and Class B (Class A fully enters the reimbursement calculation · Class B requires a personal co-payment share first, then enters); procedure items and consumables have their own catalogs; outside the catalog = fully self-paid. **"Inside the catalog" ≠ "all reimbursed" — it only means "enters the reimbursement calculation"** (the high-frequency misunderstanding, §5 has no row but deserves its own listing).
  2. **The deductible threshold / the annual cap**: below the deductible you self-pay; above the cap, major-illness insurance and other layers take over (structure · local numbers per official sources).
  3. **The reimbursement-ratio gradient**: usually grassroots > Class-2 > Class-3 (the system-design lever, directly tied to §1.1's division-of-labor strategy — **the same illness, the same drug at the grassroots, usually costs you less out of pocket** · direction-grade INFERENCE · ratios per locality).
- **Outpatient and inpatient ledgers have different structures**: outpatient pooling / outpatient chronic & special-disease benefits (chronic-disease outpatient status requires local filing per local process) and inpatient reimbursement are two parameter sets — "getting chronic-disease outpatient status certified" is the single most worthwhile thing a chronic-disease patient can do (INFERENCE · process per the local insurance authority).

### 4.2 Cross-region care registration: process overview (educational · **latest official rules prevail**)

- **Why register**: cross-city / cross-province care without registration usually means a lower reimbursement ratio or no direct settlement (direction-grade MODEL · per the enrolling locality's policy).
- **The structured process** (educational overview · channels per latest official sources): ① confirm enrollment status → ② register online (national medical insurance service app / official mini-programs etc. · choose the care locality and registration type) → ③ present the code (the medical insurance electronic credential) at a networked contracted institution in the registered locality for direct settlement. Long-term relocation (settlement / work) and temporary travel (referral / emergency) are different registration types with different rules (structure · details per official sources).
- **The emergency special case** (direction-grade · per the enrolling locality): out-of-region emergencies usually have an after-the-fact supplementary registration / manual reimbursement channel — **failing to register before emergency care ≠ cannot be reimbursed** (the high-frequency panic point · process per latest official releases).

### 4.3 Huiminbao and million-yuan medical insurance: roles and boundaries (category education · zero product recommendations)

- **Huiminbao** (city-customized inclusive supplementary insurance · category description, not a recommendation): government-guided, low premium, usually no or loose health underwriting — role = a second layer of cost-sharing for the out-of-pocket band inside the major-illness catalog; boundary = higher deductibles (usually thousand-scale yuan amounts per terms) and varying pre-existing-condition rules. **It thickens the base of the pyramid; it does not replace basic medical insurance** (INFERENCE).
- **Million-yuan medical insurance** (commercial category · description, not a recommendation): high deductible (commonly around ten-thousand-yuan scale) + high ceiling; role = transferring large inpatient-cost risk; note the health-disclosure duty (non-disclosure is the number-one source of claims disputes · direction-grade INFERENCE) and renewal clauses. **This volume recommends no products; enrollment decisions and clause interpretation are beyond medicine's scope**.
- **The layering diagram** (INFERENCE · one sentence): basic insurance covers "most things inside the catalog" → huiminbao covers "the out-of-pocket band inside the catalog" → commercial insurance covers "the large tail risk" — three layers each covering a segment; no layer should pretend to be all of it.

### 4.4 Risk identification for medical crowdfunding (INFERENCE · educational)

- Crowdfunding is a mutual-aid form of personal appeals, **not insurance and not an entitlement** — the risk face before and after launching:
  1. **The truthfulness obligation**: appeals concealing assets or exaggerating illness have already drawn legal and public backlash precedents (direction-grade · no per-case claims);
  2. **Platform fees**: withdrawal fees / third-party payment-channel fees may exist (platform rules vary · read terms before launching · this volume evaluates no platform);
  3. **Privacy exposure**: medical records, identity and family finances go fully public — the internet has no delete key; patient privacy is permanently surrendered (aligned with the digital_health_world privacy face);
  4. **The alternative priority** (INFERENCE · structural judgment): for catastrophic-illness costs, exhaust **the three insurance layers + government medical assistance** (the civil-affairs medical assistance system · per locality) before considering crowdfunding — assistance is an entitlement, crowdfunding is an appeal for help; the order should not invert.

## 5. The falsification face: five care-seeking misconceptions (each with epistemic state and the breaking face)

1. **"Whatever the Class-3A doctor says is right" = overtreatment really exists** (falsified · MODEL): diagnosis and treatment have a genuine plan space plus interest/habit biases — over-testing and over-treatment are documented in both the literature and the regulatory face (direction FACT · Chinese regulators keep issuing rules against overtreatment · educational). The four-question card (§2.3) and the second opinion (§3.1) are the matched tools. **The reverse is equally falsified**: "Class-3A always overtreats" → under-diagnosis and delay also exist (UNKNOWN boundary · individuals cannot predict which; the tools are for use, not for prediction).
2. **"More tests = more responsible"** (falsified · MODEL): every test has a false-positive rate — unindicated comprehensive screening produces the "incidentaloma cascade" (a harmless small nodule found → more tests → anxiety and potentially invasive procedures · MODEL · direction-grade), and radiation accumulates likewise. A good test = a test that would change the decision ("would this test's result change the treatment plan?" is a legitimate variant of §2.3's four questions).
3. **"Imported drugs are always better than domestic ones"** (falsified · official framing prevails): generics must pass the **consistency evaluation** (quality-and-efficacy consistency · the drug regulator's framing · latest official rules prevail) to count as interchangeable with the originator — the old intuition "imported = better" is no longer a general rule under the consistency-evaluation framework (**judge product by product**: the evaluation's coverage and individual response variation are honestly acknowledged · UNKNOWN boundary · switching decisions belong to the doctor).
4. **"Search-your-diagnosis replaces seeking care"** (falsified · FACT-grade evidence face): the **cyberchondria** research face: McMullan et al. 2019, "The relationships between health anxiety, online health information seeking, and cyberchondria: Systematic review and meta-analysis", *J Affect Disord* 245:270-278 (PMID 30419526 · Europe PMC verified · meta-grade) — health anxiety is significantly associated with online health information seeking and cyberchondria; Schenkel et al. 2021 (*J Med Internet Res* · meta-grade · bibliographic-level verification) same direction: the cyberchondria association with health anxiety / OCD symptoms / intolerance of uncertainty holds; Doherty-Torstrick et al. 2016 (bibliographic level): the longer the online health searching, the higher the anxiety during and after testing, and the greater the functional impairment. **Search symptoms → worst case jumps out → anxiety → search again** is the unbounded loop; the correct posture = searching only to **prepare for the visit** (§2.2 five sentences / §2.3 four questions), never to replace the visit or self-diagnose. Red flags on board → care priority goes up, not "let me check more".
5. **"Connections can cure the disease"** (falsified · INFERENCE): connections' real function is **information and queueing** (an earlier appointment, a few more sentences), with no evidence of improving diagnostic correctness or treatment quality itself; their cost is systematically underestimated — favor debts, in-hospital compliance risk, and **connections substituting for the care-seeking skills you could have owned** (§2's three cards). The cheaper, replicable equivalent: written timeline + four questions + teach-back + choosing the right tier per §1.1's strategy. "Cure" belongs to medical evidence, not to the corridor.
