# Vertigo & Dizziness Canon v1 (English)

> **In one sentence**: "Dizziness" is a pocket word holding at least four signals with completely different disease routes — **vertigo (vestibular), presyncope (cardiovascular), disequilibrium (neurological/proprioceptive), lightheadedness (multi-cause)**; type first, localize second — that is this canon's entire skeleton. And inside the most common vertigo hides a small miracle of clinic and ER: BPPV (benign paroxysmal positional vertigo), where **a repositioning maneuver lasting under a minute relieves most people on the spot** — while also being the textbook counter-example of **overtreatment** (pills and IV drips cannot treat a misplaced ear crystal).
>
> **How to read this volume**: ① type it first (one four-type table) → ② BPPV (most common · most treatable) → ③ Ménière disease (most mislabeled) → ④ vestibular neuritis & labyrinthitis (the diseases that need "movement") → ⑤ presyncope & cardiovascular (the type least belongable to the ear) → ⑥ red-flag chapter → ⑦ eight falsifications.

## ① Typing master face: four dizzinesses, four completely different roads

**How big is the burden (FACT · verified numbers)**

- In large population studies, **more than 15%–20% of adults experience dizziness or vertigo each year** — one of the highest-frequency complaints in clinic and emergency departments, present at every age.
- Of these, **vestibular vertigo accounts for about a quarter**; of vertigo itself, **about 80% is peripheral** (inner ear / vestibular nerve) and **about 20% is central** (brainstem / cerebellum) (FACT·relayed) — peripheral far exceeds central: that is the epidemiological foundation for "check the ears first, don't start with a 'vascular-flushing' IV"; but that 20% is why this canon's red-flag chapter exists.

**The four-way dizziness table (FACT · Drachman & Hart 1972 · Neurology 22:323-334 · classic 125-case study)**

| Type | Subjective experience | System pointed to | Typical question | Representative diseases |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Vertigo** | Room spinning · objects whirling · self turning | Vestibular system (inner ear or its brain pathways) | "Is the room spinning, or is it a foggy heaviness?" | BPPV / Ménière / vestibular neuritis |
| **Presyncope** | Vision blacking out · about to fall · cold sweat · palpitations | Cardiovascular (blood pressure/volume, heart rhythm) | "Any feeling of blacking out or falling?" | Orthostatic hypotension / arrhythmia |
| **Disequilibrium** | Unsteady walking · walking on cotton · needing to hold on | Neurological / proprioceptive / multi-sensory decline | "Does it spin? Or mainly unsteady walking?" | Peripheral neuropathy / multi-factor elderly imbalance |
| **Lightheadedness** | Floating feeling · foggy head · non-spinning | Non-specific · multi-cause | "Is it a dull, floaty feeling?" | Medications / anxiety / anemia / sleep / PPPD |

**Why "type first, localize second" is the skeleton (MODEL)**: the four types differ completely in workups, danger level, and first-visit department — vertigo type → ears; presyncope type → heart and blood pressure; disequilibrium type → nerves and gait; lightheaded type → drugs, mood, the whole body. Untyped "dizziness treatment" (a grab-bag of anti-vertigo drugs, a grab-bag of "vascular-flushing" IVs) is exactly the source of the misdiagnosis chain.

**The abuse of "dizziness = insufficient brain blood supply" and the real share (FACT·relayed + CONTRADICTED)**

- The framing of China's expert consensus on posterior-circulation ischemia is explicit: **"posterior-circulation ischemia" refers only to posterior-circulation TIA and cerebral infarction**; dizziness/vertigo can be its manifestation, but **its share among all dizziness/vertigo patients is quite low**, it almost always comes with other neurological signs, and **its main causes are the same as anterior-circulation stroke (vascular risk factors) — cervical spondylosis is not a main cause**.
- The neurological consensus movement is to **retire the old diagnostic term "vertebrobasilar insufficiency (VBI)"** — it once stuffed countless unexplained dizziness into the "insufficient blood supply" basket.
- Look at the real share in reverse: treating "insufficient blood supply" as the default explanation of dizziness means **covering the highest-share peripheral vestibular disease and cardiovascular positional problems with the lowest-share cause that most requires sign evidence**. The premise "dizziness = insufficient brain blood supply" = **CONTRADICTED**; the derived practice of "IV drips to flush the blood vessels for dizziness" = no supporting evidence (evidence gap · UNKNOWN), and its premise is already falsified — see ⑦ falsification item 1.

**A modern annotation on the lightheaded type (FACT·relayed)**: chronic lightheadedness is not a diagnostic dumping ground; it has its own entities — **vestibular migraine** is now regarded as **the most common cause of spontaneous episodic vertigo** (headache may be absent); **persistent postural-perceptual dizziness (PPPD)** is the functional entity of chronic dizziness: worse upright, with active/passive motion and visual stimuli, often following an acute vertigo event, amplified by anxiety. Both point toward typed diagnosis + vestibular rehabilitation / behavioral intervention — neither "endure it" nor "get an IV."

## ② BPPV face: most common · most treatable · least in need of pills

**Classic picture (FACT)**: getting up, lying down, turning over in bed, bowing to tie shoes, reaching overhead — **the head position changes and a whirl hits, usually self-resolving within tens of seconds (<1 minute)**. Nausea can accompany attacks; between attacks the person is as usual. This triad — positional + short-lived + provokable — is BPPV's identity card.

**Mechanism in plain words (FACT·relayed)**: inside the utricle of the inner ear sits a layer of calcium carbonate micro-crystals (otoconia, "ear crystals") that help us sense gravity and linear acceleration. Sometimes they shed and fall into a neighboring semicircular canal (most often the posterior canal). When the head turns, the crystals slide along the canal and, like ball bearings, push the endolymph, misreporting "not moving" as "spinning madly." **The entire principle of repositioning maneuvers is to walk the misplaced crystal home through a fixed sequence of head positions, letting it roll back along the canal to its origin (the utricle)** — hence Epley maneuver's other name, **canalith repositioning**.

**Epidemiology (FACT · verified numbers)**: BPPV is **the most common cause of vertigo**; lifetime prevalence about **2.4%**, one-year prevalence about **1.6%**; point prevalence about **3.4%** over age 60, and **cumulative incidence approaches 10% by age 80** (Agrawal 2013 et al.). More women than men, rising with age — and it is a treatable sensory factor in elderly falls (interlink with agc:/home: fall faces).

**Diagnosis (FACT)**: the **Dix-Hallpike positional test** is the diagnostic gold standard for posterior-canal BPPV — rapidly lowering the person from sitting to head-hanging, side-lying position, watching for a provoked "torsional, upbeating nystagmus." Diagnostic performance framing: **sensitivity about 82%, specificity about 71%** (Lopez-Escamez framing, cited in the AAO-HNS 2017 guideline).

**Treatment (FACT · this canon's brightest evidence point)**: **one Epley maneuver relieves most people on the spot** — verification anchors: 24-hour success rates after a single (modified) Epley reported as high as **83.6%**; a CMAJ 2003 review framing: "**the vast majority (>90%) of patients can be successfully treated with simple office repositioning**." The honest combined framing: **a single maneuver relieves roughly 80% (reported range 80%–90%)** (FACT).

**"BPPV needs blood-circulation pills / stone-dissolving pills" = CONTRADICTED (FACT · guideline-text level)**: the AAO-HNS 2017 BPPV guideline's key action statements, in substance — **vestibular suppressants (antihistamines / benzodiazepines) should not be used routinely to treat BPPV**, only short-term for symptom control of nausea and vomiting. An ear crystal is a calcium carbonate crystal, not a thrombus and not "stasis" — **no pill can return a crystal to its original room; hands can.**

**Self-repositioning boundary (FACT·relayed)**: online "home self-repositioning" has a precondition — **the first diagnosis must be clinician-confirmed** (characteristic nystagmus seen on positional testing + central positional nystagmus excluded) before learning the maneuver. Failed repositioning, atypical nystagmus, or new neurological symptoms during repositioning all mean back to hospital, not more practice.

**Recurrence face (MODEL)**: BPPV has a high lifetime recurrence rate (order of about half · framings vary across studies), but **recurrences can be re-positioned, same maneuver, same effect**; home Brandt-Daroff habituation exercises can serve as an adjunct. Fall risk remains elevated in the days around repositioning — home fall prevention (home:) and the repositioning face must be read together.

## ③ Ménière face: the triad · the truth about low salt · the most misapplied label

**Diagnostic criteria (FACT · AAO-HNS 2020 guideline executive summary / Barany 2015 dual framing)**:

- **At least 2 spontaneous vertigo episodes**, each lasting **20 minutes to 12 hours** (Barany 2015 framing; the AAO-HNS 2020 executive summary widens to 20 minutes–24 hours);
- **Fluctuating cochlear (hearing) symptoms of the affected ear**: hearing loss, tinnitus, aural fullness (at least one, recurrent);
- **Audiometrically documented** sensorineural hearing loss in the affected ear (typically low-to-mid frequency).
- Conclusion: a Ménière diagnosis must be completed by a clinician (history + pure-tone audiometry) — **"been dizzy once" is not Ménière**.

**The triad in plain words (FACT·relayed)**: endolymph (inner-ear fluid) hydrops swells and presses — during attacks the room spins (minutes to hours); afterwards the ear raises the alarm first: tinnitus, fullness, muffled hearing, with hearing fluctuating through attacks. Peak onset 40–60 years; prevalence about **0.2%–0.5%**.

**"All dizziness is Ménière" mislabeling = CONTRADICTED (INFERENCE · transparently derived from verified numbers)**: annual dizziness prevalence 15%–20%+; Ménière prevalence 0.2%–0.5% — **a two-order-of-magnitude gap**. Diagnosing ordinary dizziness, BPPV, or anxiety dizziness as Ménière costs the patient a "incurable disease" psychological burden plus missed treatment of the true cause.

**The evidence tier of low-salt diet (MODEL · honest grading)**: low salt targets the endolymphatic-hydrops hypothesis — mechanistically reasonable, cheap, low-risk; most guidelines list it among first-line conservative measures — but **high-quality RCT evidence is lacking**; it belongs to "mechanism-plausible + weak observational evidence" reasonable attempts. Honest positioning: **worth doing, but it is not a strong-evidence-grade prescription, still less the whole of Ménière management.**

**Long-term hearing-loss face (FACT·relayed)**: repeated attacks can cause **permanent sensorineural hearing loss** in the affected ear (starting low-mid frequency, gradually spreading). So one main line of Ménière management is **regular pure-tone audiology follow-up**; if **sudden significant hearing loss** appears, switch immediately to the sudden-hearing-loss 72-hour line (see ⑥ red-flag chapter · interlink with sens:ssnhl-72h).

## ④ Vestibular neuritis & labyrinthitis face: dizzy for days after a cold · the less you move, the longer you stay dizzy

**Mechanism and picture (FACT·relayed · textbook-level consensus framing)**: a viral infection (often after a cold) involving the vestibular nerve / inner ear — **one side's vestibule suddenly stops working**, the two sides' signals severely mismatch, producing **severe vertigo persisting for days** (unlike BPPV's tens of seconds), with marked nausea and vomiting. The dividing line is one sentence: **in vestibular neuritis hearing is normal; in labyrinthitis the inflammation reaches the cochlea, with hearing loss** — the latter needs prompt medical evaluation.

**One-line differentiation from BPPV (FACT·relayed)**: **BPPV = dizzy only on moving, seconds, hearing fine; vestibular neuritis = continuous dizziness, days, hearing fine; labyrinthitis = continuous dizziness, days, hearing damaged**. Duration (seconds–minutes–hours–days) is itself the first cut of typing.

**Vestibular rehabilitation = the undervalued mainstay (FACT · Cochrane evidence)**: the Cochrane review (McDonnell & Hillier 2015 · CD005397) conclusion, in substance: **"moderate- to strong-level evidence: vestibular rehabilitation is a safe, effective management for unilateral peripheral vestibular dysfunction"** — improving dizziness, balance, and daily function.

**The vestibular-compensation logic of "the less you move, the longer you stay dizzy" (MODEL)**: after one side's vestibule is damaged, the brain must **recalibrate** the mismatch between the two sides — the raw material of calibration is **vision, proprioception, and repeated head/position movements**. Prolonged bed rest cuts off the supply of calibration material, dragging the acute phase's "ought to be dizzy" into the chronic phase's "dizzy all the time." **Brief rest during the first 1–3 acute days is understandable; after that the direction is "resume activity as early as safely possible + vestibular rehabilitation"** — the opposite of the folk instruction "vertigo means absolute bed rest" (see ⑦ item 4).

**Differentiation from stroke (FACT)**: in acute sustained vertigo (acute vestibular syndrome · AVS), the most dangerous cause is posterior-circulation stroke. The **HINTS bedside three-step** (head-impulse test — nystagmus direction — test of skew; a professional exam, not home use) identifies stroke with **sensitivity better than early MRI** (Kattah 2009 · Stroke · 1600+ citations) — that is the evidentiary foundation of red-flag chapter ⑥: "vertigo + new neurological signs = emergency."

## ⑤ Presyncope & cardiovascular face: this type is not an ear problem, it is an internal-medicine urgency

**Why a separate face (FACT)**: the presyncope type of the four-way split runs on the **blood pressure / volume / heart rhythm** road — blacking out, about to fall, cold sweat, palpitations. It is not ENT territory, and **some of its causes are lethal** (malignant arrhythmia). "Repeated blackouts" deserves cardiac-cause exclusion even more than "repeated spinning" does.

**Home self-testing for orthostatic hypotension (FACT · international consensus framing)**:

- **Definition**: within **3 minutes** of standing (or bed head-up tilt), systolic blood pressure falling **≥20 mmHg** and/or diastolic falling **≥10 mmHg**.
- **Home method**: measure blood pressure and heart rate lying down → measure again at 1 minute and 3 minutes standing, compare the values and note any blackout/dizziness symptoms. A home electronic cuff is sufficient.
- **If positive**: bring the four sets of values to the visit — far more informative than "I'm always dizzy."
- Boundary (INFERENCE): one positive home test is not a diagnosis; repeated symptoms plus repeat-positive support a deeper workup; in older adults, even values below threshold, a "sustained standing drop in systolic pressure" has clinical meaning — leave the judgment to the clinician.

**Multi-drug attribution in the elderly (FACT·relayed · interlink with agc:polypharmacy_harm)**: a large share of older people's "dizziness" is **on the medication list** — antihypertensives (excess / stacking), prostate alpha-blockers (the classic first-dose orthostatic hypotension drugs), antidepressants, sedatives and sleeping pills, diuretics. **Every "elder is dizzy" visit should bring the complete medication list** for the clinician's attribution review — this is often the only "test" needed, and the only treatment with 100% reversibility. Sedating drugs both dizzy and cause falls (interlink with home: sedative-fall crossing).

**This type's correct action sequence (MODEL)**: type it (presyncope clues) → home lying/standing blood pressure → medication-list review → repeated blackouts / syncope, or with chest pain and palpitations → cardiology (ECG is the basic triage document).

## ⑥ Red-flag chapter: hit any one line → emergency department, no observation period

> These are the admission tickets of the 20% central type and the cardiac type. Red-flag logic: **vertigo alone is mostly benign; vertigo plus "something extra" is what is dangerous.**

| Red flag | Why | Action |
|---|---|---|
| **Vertigo + new neurological signs**: double vision, slurred speech, choking on water, limb weakness or numbness, severe headache, veering to one side when walking (ataxia) | Posterior-circulation (brainstem/cerebellar) stroke line. Posterior-circulation ischemic strokes are about 20%–25% of ischemic strokes (FACT·relayed), heavy disability and death burden, and thrombolysis/thrombectomy have a time window | **Emergency department now** (evidence: the HINTS bedside exam identifies stroke better than early MRI · Kattah 2009) |
| **Sudden hearing loss (especially one ear) with vertigo** | Sudden sensorineural hearing loss treatment window is **72 hours**; delay = permanent loss (AAO-HNS 2019 · PMID 31369359 · interlink with sens:ssnhl-72h) | **Treat as emergency**, straight to ENT |
| **Vertigo/presyncope + chest pain, palpitations, or syncope during exertion** | Cardiac line: arrhythmia / structural heart disease. Exertional syncope is a classic danger signal | **Emergency / cardiology now** |
| **Vertigo after head trauma** | Labyrinthine concussion, temporal-bone fracture, perilymph fistula, post-concussion balance disorder | **Medical evaluation**; emergency if progressively worsening |
| **Persistent vomiting, unable to keep food or fluids down** | Dehydration/electrolyte risk of severe vestibular illness; central causes also need exclusion | **Medical care** (fluids + antiemetics + cause evaluation) |
| **Older adult with repeated "blackouts" or falls** | Presyncope type / rhythm / drug stacking — not "normal aging" | Internal-medicine visit; bring lying/standing BP records + medication list |

## ⑦ Falsification face: the eight most widely circulated errors (counter-example history never deleted)

1. **"Dizziness = insufficient brain blood supply; get an IV to flush the vessels" = CONTRADICTED (premise) + unsupported (practice)**: the premise is rejected by the framing of China's posterior-circulation ischemia expert consensus — posterior-circulation ischemia means only TIA/cerebral infarction, holds a quite low share of all dizziness, and almost always carries neurological signs. The practice (routine IV "vessel-flushing" drugs for untyped dizziness) has no high-quality evidence and masks typed diagnosis. "The dizziness stopped after a few days of IV" is an entry in the self-limitation-and-comfort column, not evidence that any vessel was flushed.
2. **"The dizziness comes from cervical spondylosis" = weak evidence (entity) · CONTRADICTED (default attribution)**: "cervicogenic dizziness" as an independent entity is contested — the diagnosis requires **first excluding BPPV, vestibular migraine, and posterior-circulation ischemia**, and manual-therapy evidence is limited (systematic-review framing: limited evidence); China's posterior-circulation consensus is explicit that **cervical spondylosis is not a main cause of posterior-circulation ischemia**. Blaming an older person's dizziness on the neck first is the commonest misattribution and can miss the two highly treatable causes: BPPV (repositionable) and orthostatic hypotension (adjustable drugs).
3. **"BPPV can be dissolved with pills / blood-circulation medicine suffices" = CONTRADICTED**: AAO-HNS 2017 clearly advises against routine vestibular suppressants for BPPV. Ear crystals are calcium carbonate; "circulation pills" and "stone-dissolving pills" have no mechanism to return a crystal to its place; **repositioning is the only cause-directed treatment**, drugs only for short-term nausea control.
4. **"Vertigo means absolute bed rest, no moving" = CONTRADICTED (direction reversed)**: vestibular compensation needs movement input; prolonged immobilization delays compensation and drags an acute illness into chronic disability; Cochrane's moderate-to-strong evidence supports vestibular rehabilitation. The right approach: brief rest in the acute severe phase, then resume activity as early as safely possible + standard rehabilitation.
5. **"Dizziness is a small thing; endure it" = CONTRADICTED (for the presyncope / red-flag types)**: the presyncope type can point to arrhythmia, and every red-flag chapter line is an emergency indication. The cost function of "enduring" in a few subtypes is death.
6. **"Young people's vertigo needs no workup" = CONTRADICTED**: vestibular migraine (the most common cause of spontaneous episodic vertigo), vestibular neuritis, PPPD, and anxiety-related dizziness are all common in the young; persistent or recurrent vertigo in the young equally requires proper typing. Youth changes the risk spectrum, not the need for evaluation.
7. **"All dizziness is Ménière" = CONTRADICTED**: the order-of-magnitude gap between prevalence 0.2%–0.5% and dizziness 15%–20%+; the mislabel simultaneously creates "incurable disease" panic and missed treatment of the real cause (BPPV / orthostatic hypotension / PPPD).
8. **"One repositioning and it never comes back" = CONTRADICTED (over-optimistic)**: BPPV's lifetime recurrence is high (order of about half · MODEL), but recurrences can be re-positioned, same maneuver, same effect — the right expectation is "may recur, and can be treated again": neither panic nor false luck.

- **Ménière low-salt diet**: labeled MODEL as "mechanism-plausible + weak observational evidence"; no single-source direct verification performed.

- **Epley "about 80%"**: an honest synthesis of single-session success 83.6% (reported) and cumulative >90% (CMAJ review), not a single guideline sentence.
