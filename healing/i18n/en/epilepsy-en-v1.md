# Epilepsy Canon v1 — the brain's electrical storm: not possession, not a death sentence, and mostly well controllable

> **Written for**: all 8 billion people. No medical background assumed.
> **In one sentence**: one seizure is not epilepsy; what the bystander does (recovery position, timing, nothing in the mouth) matters more than any "folk trick"; about seven in ten treated patients can be seizure-free; stigma harms earlier than the disease itself.
> **Epistemic statement**: every key number and judgment in this volume carries an epistemic label. Literature anchors are PMIDs obtained via Europe PMC bibliographic search this round (item-by-item in the receipt's verification table); whatever could not be verified is labeled UNKNOWN — no invented numbers. This volume is not individual medical advice; all medication, tapering and pregnancy decisions must be made by a neurologist in person.

---

## ① The dividing line: one seizure ≠ epilepsy

1. **A seizure is an event; epilepsy is a disease tendency (FACT)**: a seizure is a transient functional disturbance caused by abnormally synchronized neuronal discharge; fever, hypoglycemia, alcohol withdrawal and acute brain injury can trigger one seizure in **anyone**. The ILAE 2014 operational definition confines epilepsy to: **≥2 unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart**; or 1 unprovoked seizure with a recurrence risk ≥60%; or meeting the diagnosis of an epilepsy syndrome (anchor: Fisher et al., Epilepsia 2014, PMID:24730690 — bibliographic record directly verified this round, a formal ILAE report with citedByCount>3700).
2. **Acute symptomatic seizures: seizures with a clear acute trigger; most are not diagnosed as epilepsy (FACT · concept)**. Common triggers include —
   - **Febrile seizures**: fever-related seizures in infants and toddlers, mostly benign in course, with a low long-term risk of developing epilepsy; guidelines advise against long-term antiseizure medication for simple febrile seizures (anchor: AAP guideline, Pediatrics, PMID:18519501).
   - **Metabolic/systemic triggers**: severe hypoglycemia, hyponatremia, uremia and the like (anchor: acute symptomatic seizure review direction, PMID:42513263; the hypoglycemia mechanism direction is physiology common knowledge, labeled MODEL).
   - **Alcohol withdrawal**: abrupt cessation after long heavy drinking can trigger seizures — this is a "withdrawal reaction," not epilepsy the disease (MODEL · multi-source consistent direction).
   - **Acute central nervous system injury**: acute stroke, acute traumatic brain injury, central nervous system infection, etc. (PMID:42513263 review direction).
   - **Reflex seizures**: reliably provoked by specific stimuli (flashing lights, specific thought/movement patterns) — a different concept from spontaneous seizures (anchor: photosensitivity screening protocol study, PMID:24346821; concept level MODEL).
   > **What the boundary means**: equating "one seizure" directly with "epilepsy" errs in both directions at once — labeling a reversible provoked seizure as a lifelong disease (over-labeling), or missing true epilepsy (underestimating recurrence risk).
3. **Prevalence, real numbers (FACT · WHO page directly verified)**: about **50 million people** worldwide have epilepsy; point prevalence of "active epilepsy" (still seizing or on treatment) in the general population is about **4–10 per 1,000**; in low-income countries about **three quarters** of patients receive no treatment (the "treatment gap"). WHO and ILAE have long used the framing "about 70% of patients can be seizure-free with proper treatment" — the WHO page directly verified this round did not hit that sentence verbatim, so the framing is downgraded to **MODEL·relayed** (recorded honestly in the receipt).
4. **"Benign childhood" as it is (FACT · bibliographic)**: some childhood epilepsy syndromes are self-limited/benign, e.g., benign epilepsy with centrotemporal spikes (BECTS) and childhood absence epilepsy, most remitting around puberty (anchor: World J Pediatr 2017 review, PMID:28101769). "Self-limited" does not mean "no need to see a doctor" — the diagnosis and follow-up judgments must be made by a pediatric neurologist.

## ② Recognizing seizure types: the bystander's eyes are the first "EEG"

1. **Generalized tonic-clonic seizure (the grand mal) (MODEL · textbook-consistent framing)**: sudden loss of consciousness → tonic phase (limbs extended, possible fall, possible cry) → clonic phase (rhythmic jerking of all four limbs) → post-ictal confusion/drowsiness. What the witness should note: **start time and end time**, whether jerking is bilaterally symmetric, tongue biting/incontinence (supporting clues, not necessary conditions).
2. **Absence seizures: the epilepsy missed as "daydreaming" (FACT · bibliographic direction)**: mostly in children — abrupt interruption of activity, staring, lasting seconds, possibly many times a day, with immediate recovery afterward; the EEG shows characteristic spike-and-wave discharges, and hyperventilation can provoke them (anchor: childhood absence epilepsy review PMID:28101769; review of hyperventilation activation's role in diagnosis PMID:39175400). **The danger is in the label**: a child repeatedly criticized for "spacing out in class, daydreaming, poor attention" may need an EEG rather than a scolding (INFERENCE · extrapolated from the miss-direction above).
3. **Focal seizures: awareness preserved or not is the watershed (MODEL · ILAE classification framing)**: focal seizures start in a local region of one hemisphere and may be motor (jerking of one limb/the mouth corner), sensory (odd smells / an epigastric rising sensation / déjà vu), autonomic, or with psychic symptoms; **awareness preserved** (can respond) = "focal aware"; **awareness impaired** (blank stare, automatisms such as lip-smacking/fumbling/wandering aimlessly) = "focal impaired awareness," and either can evolve to bilateral tonic-clonic activity.
4. **The value of family phone videos for diagnosis (MODEL · honestly downgraded)**: the core of diagnosing paroxysmal events is the ictal presentation, which doctors almost never witness — a family video brings the event into the clinic, directionally on the same route as video-EEG (ictal video + synchronized EEG, the diagnostic gold standard, anchor: best-practice proposal PMID:30214985) and wearable/smartphone-connected seizure-detection applications (anchor: EBioMedicine 2025 phase-3 validation study, PMID:41027311): "filling the clinic's blind spot with real-event recordings." **Exactly how much a video raises diagnostic yield — no direct bibliographic record found this round; labeled UNKNOWN**; but "advise the family to film the whole event (whole body in frame, from onset to full recovery)" is a low-cost, low-risk, consistent recommendation (MODEL).

## ③ First-aid face: do three things right, do not do three things

> **Interlink with the Family First-Aid Canon** (docs/healing/first-aid-canon-v1-20260912.md): the first-aid volume sets the master line (scene safety → call for help → only do what is certainly safe); this volume expands the seizure scenario.
1. **Do three things right (MODEL · first-aid organizations' consistent framing; no single-intervention first-aid RCT bibliographic record found this round — honestly downgraded)**:
   - **Recovery position (on the side)**: lets oral secretions drain, keeps the airway clear, prevents aspiration;
   - **Time it**: note the seizure's start time on a watch — the sole basis for the "5-minute line" below;
   - **Move hazards away** (table corners, hot water, sharp objects), pad something soft under the head, loosen the collar.
2. **Do not do three things (MODEL · supported by mechanism INFERENCE)**:
   - **Put nothing into the mouth** — biting force during a seizure is enormous; inserted objects can break teeth, injure the mouth, and cause aspiration suffocation; the "tongue-biting prevention" benefit is far smaller than the suffocation risk; tongue biting itself is rarely fatal (mechanism-level INFERENCE · extrapolated from airway-obstruction physiology and first-aid guidelines' consistent framing; no single-point RCT bibliographic record found this round, honestly recorded in the receipt);
   - **No philtrum pressure, no hoku-point pinching, no restraining the limbs** — pressure cannot stop the electrical discharge, and holding limbs down causes fractures/dislocations; pinching only adds injury;
   - **No water, no medication by mouth** — with consciousness impaired, anything taken by mouth can be aspirated.
3. **When to call an ambulance (MODEL · multi-source consistent; call-for-help master line interlink fa:call_120_only)**: ① a seizure **>5 minutes** without stopping (a generalized convulsive seizure running long is handled per the status-epilepticus red line); ② a **first-ever** seizure (any seizure of unknown cause deserves medical evaluation); ③ injury after the seizure, drowning, pregnancy/postpartum, diabetes; ④ a seizure **in water** (aspiration risk); ⑤ **clustered seizures**, or another one arriving before awakening from the first; ⑥ breathing difficulty after the seizure that does not recover.
4. **Post-ictal confusion care (MODEL)**: after the seizure stops there is often a period of confusion, resistance and drowsiness (minutes to tens of minutes); lay the person on their side to rest, and say quietly: "you just had a seizure, you are safe now, I am here"; **do not leave them alone, do not interrogate, do not pour water into them until fully awake**; once fully awake, accompany them to medical care or send them in per the red lines above.
5. **SUDEP as it is: fear must be layered against real risk (FACT · bibliographic existence; point estimates labeled MODEL)**: SUDEP (sudden unexpected death in epilepsy) is the sudden, non-traumatic, non-drowning death of a person with epilepsy that remains unexplained even in part at autopsy (definition and epidemiology review anchor: PMID:35623204; NEJM review anchor: PMID:22070477). Review framing: on the order of **about 1 per 1,000 person-years** in the general epilepsy population, significantly higher where generalized tonic-clonic seizures are uncontrolled; **the strongest modifiable protective factor is "reducing generalized tonic-clonic seizure counts"** — regular medication, no self-directed tapering, improved nighttime supervision all push in that direction. Risk communication should be layered and honest: neither terrifying (the actual annual risk magnitude for well-controlled people is small) nor concealing (it is a major cause of epilepsy-related early death) — informed is for taking medication better, not for fear.

## ④ The treatment honest ledger

1. **Drugs first-line: start with one (FACT · bibliographic framing)**: anti-seizure medications (ASMs) are the first step for nearly all diagnosed epilepsy; the principles are **choose by seizure type, start monotherapy, titrate up slowly**. Cohort evidence: about **half of patients achieve long-term seizure freedom on their first monotherapy** (Kwan & Brodie cohort, Epilepsia 2001, PMID:11737159 — bibliographic record directly verified; "about half" is this record's direction; the exact percentage defers to the original text); add WHO's "about 70% seizure-free on proper treatment" framing (MODEL·relayed, see ①3). **The other side of "70% controllable"**: about 30% are drug-resistant — they are not "beyond help"; they need the next paragraph's options.
2. **Tapering conditions: not "I'm better" but "the doctor says so" (MODEL · guidelines-consistent framing)**: usually requires **2–4 years seizure-free** plus EEG and syndrome-type assessment, after which the doctor designs a slow taper. **Recurrence as it is**: recurrence after tapering is a common event — a meta-analysis exists and the direction is clear (Berg & Shinnar, Neurology 1994, PMID:8164811, bibliographic record directly verified); overall recurrence is on the order of tens of percentage points (**MODEL**: exact point estimates vary by syndrome/course/EEG; the original numbers were not verified verbatim this round, so no hard citation). **The cost of stopping on your own**: withdrawal-triggered seizure flare-ups, accidental injury, driving and occupational-qualification risks (INFERENCE).
3. **Where surgery and neuromodulation sit (MODEL)**: drug-resistant epilepsy (still seizing despite correct use of 2 or more suitable medications) should be evaluated early for **epileptogenic-zone resection** — for suitable focal cases the only potentially "curative" option; where resection is impossible, neuromodulation (vagus nerve stimulation, VNS; deep brain stimulation, DBS; etc.) can reduce seizures. No single-intervention surgery RCT bibliographic record was found for this volume; positioned at MODEL, recorded honestly in the receipt.
4. **The ketogenic diet (FACT · RCT bibliographic)**: the high-fat, low-carbohydrate ketogenic diet, used for (mainly pediatric) drug-resistant epilepsy, has randomized controlled trial support that it outperforms regular-diet controls (Neal et al., Lancet Neurol 2008, PMID:18456557, bibliographic record directly verified; the classic report's direction is "about 40% of children reduced seizures ≥50% vs about 6% of controls" — **number level MODEL · original not verified verbatim**). Position: one of the legitimate treatment options for resistant cases, **must be conducted under the supervision of a dietitian and a doctor** — not an influencer weight-loss fad.
5. **The women's face: two sides contrasted, no one-sided scare (FACT+MODEL)**:
   - **The teratogenic face (FACT · bibliographic directly verified)**: valproate has **dose-dependent teratogenicity**; use in pregnancy is associated with raised fetal malformation risk, higher with higher dose (EURAP observational study, Tomson et al., Neurology, PMID:26085607); people of childbearing potential should in principle discuss alternatives with their doctor — but **the switch decision belongs in preconception planning, not in stopping medication on your own after discovering a pregnancy** (uncontrolled seizures from self-directed stopping are just as dangerous for parent and fetus, INFERENCE).
   - **The missed-dose/seizure face (MODEL)**: seizures in pregnancy (especially generalized tonic-clonic) carry risks of falls, hypoxia and SUDEP — "under-taking out of teratogenicity fear" and "taking medication regardless of seizures" are both wrong; the correct posture is **preconception counseling, monotherapy at the lowest effective dose, drug-level follow-up**.
   - **Folic acid (FACT · bibliographic)**: preconception/prenatal folic acid supplementation in women with epilepsy is associated with improved offspring outcomes (observational anchors: Epilepsia 2026, PMID:42096275; Chinese pregnancy population study anchor: PMID:41532884); exact dosing follows joint obstetrics and neurology advice.

## ⑤ The living face: boundary management, not imprisonment

1. **Driving-law differences as they are (FACT · bibliographic direction)**: countries' rules on driving with epilepsy (required seizure-free intervals, medical assessment, exemption situations) **differ widely** and keep updating (anchor: Seizure 2026 review of driving restrictions in photosensitive epilepsy, PMID:42526149); this volume gives no "globally unified number" — defer to the current law of your jurisdiction and your treating doctor (UNKNOWN · per-jurisdiction intervals not verified one by one).
2. **High-risk scenarios (MODEL · multi-source consistent framing)**: **swimming** (must be accompanied; tell the lifeguard), **work at heights/climbing**, **machine operation**, **hot baths** (no solo bathing), **sleep deprivation** (a documented seizure trigger; interlink ins:sleep_hygiene_necessary_insufficient — the sleep-hygiene route), **alcohol** (lowers the seizure threshold + interferes with drug metabolism + disrupts sleep architecture — three layers stacked, INFERENCE). These are a "risk-management checklist," not a "lifetime house arrest" — the goal is that activities continue under controlled conditions.
3. **Photosensitivity: real but a minority only (MODEL · exact prevalence UNKNOWN)**: TV/screen flicker and strobe lights can provoke photosensitive seizures (anchor: photosensitivity screening study PMID:24346821; driving-restriction discussion PMID:42526149); but photosensitivity affects only a **minority** of people with epilepsy, and **ordinary everyday lighting (daylight, steady indoor lighting, normal-brightness screens) does not provoke seizures** — "people with epilepsy can't watch TV or use phones" is over-generalization; practical advice for watching events/gaming: keep the room bright, increase the distance, avoid long gazing at rapidly strobing images (MODEL · practical level).

## ⑥ Stigma and society: taking the "disease" off the "personality"

1. **Falsification: "epilepsy = madness / possession / soul loss" (FACT · conceptual falsification)**: epilepsy is a **network disease with clear electrophysiological and neurobiological underpinnings**, having nothing to do with schizophrenia, personality, morality, or ghosts; "possession" explanations phenomenologically piggyback on the altered consciousness and motor display of seizures and have been mechanistically replaced by abnormal-discharge evidence (a historical course of medicine · FACT). Stigma itself is a real dimension of health harm: in epilepsy research, stigma correlates with psychosocial comorbidity and has been described as a "social comorbidity" (anchor: Epilepsy Behav 2026, PMID:41927457) — **the disease yields to medication; stigma yields only to education and to seeing real patients**.
2. **[TCM·Traditional] The xian syndrome (independently annotated · traditional-knowledge layer)**: traditional Chinese medicine literature classifies paroxysmal loss of consciousness with convulsions as "xian syndrome/epilepsy," treated by pattern types of phlegm, fire, stasis, deficiency, etc. — a **traditional-medicine conceptual system** (INFERENCE · traditional knowledge layer, not a modern mechanistic explanation); this volume annotates its independent origin and position as it is: it exists as cultural-medical history and in the TCM clinical context, and **is not interchangeable with modern antiseizure treatment** — any "replace your ASMs" claim is a dangerous proposition (FACT · safety boundary).
3. **Employment and education rights (MODEL · international-framework framing)**: under most jurisdictions and the WHO/UN disability framework, people with epilepsy enjoy equal rights to employment and education on the basis of reasonable accommodation (e.g., space for seizure management at work; no veto on seizure history alone); the main real-world obstacle is usually not the seizures but employer/school misunderstanding — exactly the social application of this volume's ⑥1 falsification face (INFERENCE). Specific statutes per jurisdiction: UNKNOWN (not verified one by one).

## ⑦ Falsification face (each item with its falsifier — "what would prove me wrong")

1. **"Put something in the mouth to prevent tongue biting" — false.** Basis: inserted objects can break teeth, injure mouth and airway, cause aspiration suffocation; the first-aid consensus framing is nothing in the mouth (MODEL · mechanism INFERENCE). **Falsifier**: a high-quality controlled study showing the object-in-mouth group with lower aspiration/injury rates than the nothing-in-mouth group would overturn this item.
2. **"Philtrum pressure / hoku-point pinching stops the convulsion" — false.** Basis: seizures self-terminate or are terminated by drugs; painful stimulation does not alter discharge; pressing limbs instead causes orthopedic injury (INFERENCE · mechanism level). **Falsifier**: a blinded controlled study showing the painful-stimulation group's seizure duration significantly shorter than controls' with no injury would overturn this item.
3. **"The seizure is possession/soul loss — exorcise, don't seek care" — false.** Basis: the ILAE definition and the EEG/imaging evidence chain (PMID:24730690 et al.); the cost of delayed care = missed treatable disease + uncontrolled seizures + SUDEP risk (FACT/INFERENCE chain). **Falsifier**: a reproducible outcomes study showing "exorcism beats ASMs" independent of discharge evidence would overturn this item.
4. **"Antiseizure drugs make children dull" — half false (both sides honestly).** Basis: most modern ASMs at effective control doses have limited, individualized negative developmental effects; **whereas uncontrolled frequent seizures and epileptic encephalopathy themselves can impair development** — "not taking drugs is smarter" trades the real risk (uncontrolled seizures) for phantom safety (MODEL · multi-source consistent direction; no monotherapy-cognition bibliographic record found this round, honestly recorded in the receipt). **Falsifier**: a long-term cohort showing the properly medicated group's developmental outcomes systematically worse than the unmedicated uncontrolled-seizure group would overturn this item.
5. **"A normal EEG means it's not epilepsy" — false.** Basis: interictal EEG can be normal; diagnosis rests mainly on history + seizure description (with video-EEG capture when needed, PMID:30214985 direction); a normal EEG cannot exclude epilepsy (MODEL · textbook-consistent framing). **Falsifier**: interictal EEG excluding epilepsy with near-100% sensitivity would overturn this item.
6. **"Off drugs = cured; stop whenever I like" — false.** Basis: recurrence after tapering is common (PMID:8164811 meta-analysis exists · direction FACT); self-directed stopping can reignite seizures and cause accidental injury (INFERENCE). **Falsifier**: a meta-analysis update showing near-zero recurrence after tapering in properly managed populations would overturn this item.
7. **"Epilepsy is always hereditary" — half false.** Basis: most epilepsy is sporadic/acquired (structural, metabolic, unknown cause), and only some genetic syndromes have clear, itself-diverse inheritance patterns (MODEL · textbook-consistent framing; bibliographic level UNKNOWN · no dedicated hereditary-proportion paper found this round). **Falsifier**: population research showing first-degree relatives' overall recurrence risk well over twice baseline across the entire spectrum would force "mostly sporadic" to be rewritten.
