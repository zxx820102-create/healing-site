# The Drugs-and-Food Textbook v1 (2026-09-12)

> **Readers**: self-learners and health workers. **How to use it**: every chapter has learning objectives and a summary; [L3:id] leads back to the evidence-layer source text (assertion + epistemic state + falsifier), and [N2], [SH], [PG] are sister-layer anchors. Epistemic states are aligned verbatim with L3, zero promotion: FACT is never written as "absolute," MODEL is never written as "proven," and [TCM·Traditional] never passes itself off as modern evidence. **One through-line: this book teaches how to eat so that your meals do not fight with your medicines — it does not teach how to stop medicines.**

## Chapter 0. The one-page version (L1 · readable with zero background)

> Abridged companion text: this chapter is a condensed transcription of `docs/healing/textbook/foodrug-l1-80yi-20260912.md`, not word-identical; the companion is the fuller, supplemented version and can be distributed on its own.

**The doctrine: keep eating as usual, keep four rules — alcohol, herbal products, and habits should not swing wildly, and when you see a doctor, report what you are taking first.** Hold the four confidence tiers in mind: confirmed by research → fairly well established → still being studied → not known.

**Seven action cards (all confirmed by research)**: ① take medicines with plain water: levothyroxine meets coffee and absorption falls sharply, tetracyclines/quinolones meet milk, calcium, or iron and absorption drops to nearly zero, and the osteoporosis medicines require staying upright for half an hour after taking them ② cephalosporins and metronidazole — avoid alcohol for three days after stopping them too; metformin and antidepressants stack with alcohol and harm the body ③ St. John's wort disables birth-control pills, leaves transplant medicines unguarded, and de-fangs anticoagulants — contraindicated for anyone on long-term medications ④ on the anticoagulant warfarin, keep eating leafy greens with a constant daily amount; danggui, danshen, and ginkgo may enlarge bleeding, while ginseng may instead weaken the drug's effect ⑤ on the "-pril"/"-sartan"/spironolactone blood-pressure medicines, potassium supplements are contraindicated — low-sodium salt is a hidden potassium supplement ⑥ supplements are "medicines" too: biotin scrambles lab tests, excess zinc injures nerves, and iron should be tested for before it is supplemented ⑦ if you are on nitrates, ED drugs and "poppers" are contraindicated — combined use can crash blood pressure fatally.

**The six myths are all false** (herbal medicines are natural and never clash / supplements do not count as medicines / spacing two hours apart solves everything / "tea neutralizes medicine" is pure superstition / taking extra supplements is harmless / every medicine should be taken on an empty stomach). **Three things not known** (traditional incompatibilities such as ginseng × radish; the vast majority of Chinese-herb × Western-medicine combinations; whether the Eighteen Antagonisms and Nineteen Fears hold as a whole). **Red line: this book is not a medication-stopping guide — before adding or stopping anything, ask your doctor or pharmacist first.**

## Chapter 1. Concepts and terms

**Learning objectives**: be able to summarize the mechanisms as "the assembly line, the porters, the glue, the accelerator-and-brake"; be able to tell the four confidence tiers apart; be able to name the three outcomes.

### 1.1 The three outcomes of an interaction

Only three outcomes exist: **the drug gets stronger** (blood levels rise → toxicity), **the drug gets weaker** (the effect is discounted → the disease treatment fails), and **the lab report turns fake** (the drug harms no one, the reading lies → misdiagnosis). The third is the least intuitive [L3:C6].

### 1.2 The mechanism trio (plain-language version · expanded in Chapter 4)

Picture the body as a port: the **digestion assembly line** (the CYP enzymes) breaks medicines down and ships them out; the **porters at the door** (the transporters) decide how much of a medicine goes in and comes out; the **glue** (chelation) sticks a medicine and a food component together into an insoluble lump; and the **accelerator and the brake** means that things acting on the same system stack when taken together and cancel when opposed. All 45 entries fit inside — this book teaches you to reason, not to memorize.

### 1.3 The four confidence tiers side by side (Table 1-1)

| L3 epistemic state | Plain-language tier | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| FACT | confirmed by research | package-insert / guideline / systematic-review grade |
| MODEL | fairly well established | mechanism clear, clinical quantification not yet settled |
| Consistent (dual framework) | confirmed / fairly well established (marked item by item) | traditional observation and modern pharmacology cross-confirm each other |
| [TCM·Traditional] | still being studied / not known (marked item by item) | the traditional framework presented as it is · the modern face unstudied |

### Glossary (in order of appearance)

1. **Interaction**: two or more things entering the body (medicines/foods/supplements) change each other's effects.
2. **CYP enzymes (the liver's drug-processing enzymes)**: the "medicine digestion assembly line" in the liver and gut wall; it breaks medicines down for excretion; CYP3A4 is the busiest line.
3. **Enzyme inhibition**: a foot on the assembly line's brake (grapefruit juice, for example) → more medicine in the blood.
4. **Enzyme induction**: a foot on the assembly line's accelerator (St. John's wort, for example) → the therapeutic effect is discounted.
5. **Transporters (P-glycoprotein and others)**: the "porters" at the cell door, pumping medicines in and out; once sped up, they pump more of the medicine back out for disposal.
6. **Chelation**: metal ions (calcium, iron, magnesium, zinc, aluminum) act like glue binding a drug into an insoluble lump → absorption drops to nearly zero.
7. **Therapeutic window**: the blood-concentration corridor between effective and toxic; narrow-window medicines (warfarin, lithium, digoxin, immunosuppressants) cannot tolerate swings.
8. **Warfarin**: the veteran anticoagulant, dosed by blood tests, exceptionally easy for foods and Chinese herbs to perturb.
9. **DOAC**: the newer oral anticoagulants; few food interactions, no need for vitamin K stability.
10. **INR**: the clotting indicator checked regularly by people on warfarin — too high means bleeding, too low means clots.
11. **Nitrates**: vessel-widening medicines (nitroglycerin and others, including the sublingual emergency medicines); the term also covers the inhaled "poppers."
12. **PDE5 inhibitors**: the sildenafil (Viagra) class of prescription medicines; they run through the same vessel-widening pathway as nitrates.
13. **Disulfiram-like reaction**: certain cephalosporins/metronidazole block the second step of alcohol breakdown; acetaldehyde piles up → flushing, palpitations, vomiting, a blood-pressure crash, and in severe cases shock.
14. **SSRIs**: the modern mainstream antidepressants (sertraline and others).
15. **MAOIs**: the older antidepressants; meeting tyramine-rich foods (aged cheeses, cured products) or ephedra-type substances can trigger a blood-pressure crisis [N2:特殊人群节].
16. **Levothyroxine**: the lifelong replacement medicine for an underactive thyroid; extremely picky about absorption.
17. **Bisphosphonates**: the oral osteoporosis medicines (alendronate and others); less than 1% reaches the blood — the least food-tolerant of all.
18. **The [TCM·Traditional] marker**: the honest label for traditional-Chinese-medicine content — presenting the traditional framework as it is, without impersonating modern evidence and without promotion.

**Summary**: three outcomes × the mechanism trio = this book's whole analytical frame; narrow-window medicines fear swings the most.

## Chapter 2. The red line: this book is not a medication-stopping guide

**Learning objectives**: be able to restate the three boundaries.

1. **This book does not touch prescriptions.** Every "what to do" here sits on the "how to eat, how to offset timing, how to report" side — no medicine is reduced, stopped, or switched here; if you suspect food has interfered with a medicine, tell the prescribing physician, who verifies and adjusts.
2. **Intervals and prohibitions defer to the package insert.** The same medicine in different formulations can carry opposite conclusions: tablet levothyroxine is disturbed by coffee, the liquid oral solution is not [L3:A2-3]; doxycycline is far less affected by milk than tetracycline [L3:A2-1]. This book's intervals are general rules; for an individual case the package insert and the doctor prevail.
3. **Report first, adjust later.** At the visit, report the "three-part list": prescription medicines + OTC medicines + Chinese herbal products. Most of the 45 entries are "manageable" rather than "absolutely forbidden" — on the condition that the doctor knows; without the list, the doctor is prescribing blind.

**Summary**: knowledge is only safe on the premise that the doctor knows.

## Chapter 3. Life-saving interactions (each in three parts: why → what to do → care-seeking signals)

**Learning objectives**: for each of the four life-saving interactions, state the mechanism in one sentence, the action in one sentence, and one set of care-seeking signals. The four: St. John's wort (broad-spectrum failure · FACT); nitrates × ED drugs/poppers (blood-pressure crash · FACT · quoted from [SH]); warfarin's two-way trap (bleeding or failure · FACT+MODEL); lithium × sudden sodium swings (lithium toxicity · FACT).

### 3.1 St. John's wort (Hypericum perforatum) × broad-spectrum medications [L3:C1]

- **Why**: high-hyperforin content **induces** CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein in a dose-dependent way — pressing the assembly line's accelerator and speeding up the porters at the same time, thinning out a whole class of medicines systemically: cyclosporine/tacrolimus (transplant → rejection), oral contraceptives (reports of contraceptive failure), warfarin, DOACs, some antiretrovirals and anticancer medicines (the imatinib class), digoxin. Honesty boundary: low-hyperforin preparations induce weakly and preparations are not equivalent — conservative clinical practice is to **treat all of them as interacting**.
- **What to do**: contraindicated for anyone taking any of the medicine classes above; before buying a "calming / mood-lifting" herbal product, check the ingredient list; at the visit, put St. John's wort on the medication list.
- **Care-seeking signals**: a transplant recipient's urine output falling sharply; abnormal bleeding while on contraceptives; INR or drug effect fluctuating for no explainable reason.

### 3.2 Nitrates × PDE5 inhibitors (including poppers) [SH:§3.1·L3 未单列，证据源为性健康正典]

- **Why**: the two run through the same NO-cGMP vessel-widening pathway; stacked → blood pressure can crash, fatally (a consistent framing across cardiovascular-safety reviews · FACT). Inhaled alkyl nitrites ("poppers") fall under the same contraindication (consensus grade). **Hidden nitrates** are often overlooked: the sublingual nitroglycerin kept for emergencies is one too.
- **What to do**: for anyone on nitrates, ED drugs and poppers are contraindicated; the question "how long after stopping a PDE5 inhibitor may a nitrate be used" is managed by emergency physicians.
- **Care-seeking signals**: chest pain that does not ease after taking the sublingual emergency medicine, or dizziness and inability to stay standing after combining them — call for help immediately and say what was taken. Note: sildenafil is metabolized by CYP3A4; while taking a PDE5 inhibitor, avoid large amounts of grapefruit juice (direction FACT · quantification MODEL) [SH].

### 3.3 Warfarin's two-way trap [L3:C4/B4·N2:特殊人群节]

- **Why (both directions are dangerous)**: warfarin dosing is balanced against vitamin K intake, and its therapeutic window is narrow. **Forward direction (bleeding)**: danggui (Angelica sinensis; contains coumarin-type constituents), danshen (Salvia miltiorrhiza; antiplatelet + metabolic interference), and ginkgo (antiplatelet stack-on) combined with warfarin → INR rises and bleeding follows (a cluster of case reports + mechanism · FACT). **Reverse direction (failure)**: ginseng has case reports of INR falling (MODEL · an unsettled direction that calls for even more monitoring).
- **What to do**: ① keep vitamin K **stable rather than quit it** — keep eating leafy greens at a constant daily amount, and do not take a vitamin-K-containing multivitamin on and off (contrast: DOACs need none of this [PG:drug#35]) ② any start or stop of a Chinese herbal product goes through the anticoagulation clinic first ③ check INR on schedule.
- **Care-seeking signals**: bleeding gums that will not stop, unusual bruising, black tarry stools, blood in the urine, a sudden surge in menstrual flow = alarms on the bleeding side; an INR plunging or signs of a clot = the failure side — either way, contact the anticoagulation clinic immediately.

### 3.4 Lithium × sodium/caffeine [PG:drug#21·N2:特殊人群节]

- **Why**: the kidneys handle sodium and lithium through the same reabsorption channel — when **sodium intake drops suddenly** (dieting, extreme salt restriction, heavy sweating, diarrhea with dehydration), the body pulls sodium back avidly, and the channel "hitchhikes" more lithium back too → blood lithium rises → lithium toxicity (FACT). The caffeine wing: L3 and PG both do not list it standalone (labeled honestly); the standard pharmacology framing places it at the fairly-well-established tier — caffeine's diuretic dehydration can indirectly affect blood lithium, and the risk again lies in the "sudden swing," not in the drinking.
- **What to do**: salt intake, water intake, and coffee habits all **stay stable**; tell the doctor proactively when you sweat heavily, vomit or have diarrhea, or cannot eat during an illness.
- **Care-seeking signals**: worsening hand tremor, nausea and vomiting, slurred speech, drowsiness or confusion = signs of lithium toxicity — seek care immediately and have blood lithium checked.

**Summary**: what the four share — the thing that goes wrong is never "took one bite of something"; it is long-term exposure or a sudden swing; and the shared exit: let the prescribing physician know.

## Chapter 4. The mechanism trio: teaching you to extrapolate from one case to the rest

**Learning objectives**: without memorizing the 45 entries, use the mechanisms to infer the risk direction of a new combination; be able to sort any entry into its mechanism.

### 4.1 The digestion assembly line (the CYP enzymes)

When the drug-breaking enzymes get a foot on the brake (inhibition) → levels rise → side effects; a foot on the accelerator (induction) → levels fall → treatment failure. Grapefruit juice is the classic brake: it inhibits intestinal CYP3A4, and the casualty list includes felodipine, nifedipine, cyclosporine, and some statins [L3:A1·N2]; St. John's wort is the classic accelerator (§3.1). **Inference rule**: for an herb advertised as "liver-protecting" or "metabolism-adjusting," first ask whether your medicine runs through this assembly line — if it does, both brake and accelerator spell trouble.

### 4.2 The porters at the door (the transporters)

St. John's wort speeds up P-glycoprotein → more of the medicine is pumped back into the gut lumen; levodopa has its "porters snatched" by the amino acids of a high-protein meal (they share the large neutral amino acid transporter) → less reaches the brain, and protein redistribution must be prescribed by a doctor [L3:A2-10]. **Inference rule**: when a package insert says "absorption changes when taken with high-protein/high-fat food," you are dealing with the porters or the solvent environment.

### 4.3 The glue (chelation) and the accelerator-and-brake (stack-on/offset)

**Glue**: calcium, iron, magnesium, zinc, and aluminum bind tetracyclines, quinolones, and levothyroxine into insoluble lumps [L3:A2-1/2/4]; the glue also hides inside "health foods" — mineral-containing multivitamins are the largest hidden chelation source [L3:C2]. **Stack-on** (all feet on the brake): the anticoagulant family stacked to bleeding (danggui/danshen/ginkgo/high-dose fish oil/vitamin E >400 IU/melatonin · see Table 4-1); certain cephalosporins (with an MTT side chain) block the second step of alcohol breakdown → the disulfiram-like reaction [L3:A2-8]; ephedra × MAOI stacks blood pressure upward [L3:B4]. **Offset** (accelerator against brake): ginseng offsets warfarin; vitamin B6 accelerates the peripheral breakdown of levodopa [L3:B4/C7].

### 4.5 The three extrapolation questions

Meeting a new combination (a new supplement × an old medicine): ① **Does the package insert say "absorption falls/rises when taken with such-and-such"?** — glue or environment mechanism; follow the interval as written. ② **Is my medicine a narrow therapeutic window drug?** — a narrow window cannot tolerate swings; route everything through the "tell the doctor" exit. ③ **Does the new thing push the same direction as the medicine or the opposite?** — same direction stacks, opposite offsets, unknown is handled as unknown (do not gamble).

**Summary**: the mechanism trio is not a knowledge point but a tool — the 45 entries fit inside it, and entry 46 will too.

## Chapter 5. The TCM face: presenting the traditional framework as it is

**Learning objectives**: be able to tell "what the tradition says" apart from "what modern evidence says," without either crossing the line.

### 5.1 Labeling discipline

Traditional content is always marked [TCM·Traditional] — presented as it is, never impersonating modern evidence, neither promoted nor disparaged; where it cross-confirms with modern pharmacology, it is marked [Consistent] and given an evidence grade [L3:B 节总纪律].

### 5.2 General dietary restrictions while on medicines (Table 5-1) [TCM·Traditional]

| Restriction | Traditional rationale | Modern-evidence face |
|---|---|---|
| Raw and cold foods | injures the yang qi of the spleen and stomach; the cold herb force stacks | not known (GI irritation has a physiological counterpart) |
| Pungent and spicy | pungency disperses and assists heat, at odds with heat-clearing and yin-nourishing formulas | not known (mucosal irritation has a counterpart) |
| Fishy and muttony foods | clash with the aromatic damp-resolving herbs; assist the pathogen in sores and asthma | not known (foreign-protein sensitization has a common-sense counterpart) |
| Strong tea | "tea neutralizes medicine" · tannic acid hinders absorption | [Consistent · fairly well established] tannins chelate iron and alkaloids; "neutralizes every medicine" is an overgeneralization |

### 5.3 Traditional herb × food entries [TCM·Traditional · each as it stands]

Ginseng/dangshen × radish (radish "breaks qi" and weakens the qi tonic) — no modern controlled study has been done (not known); even inside the tradition there is no single line: folk practice also holds that people who feel bloated on ginseng feel relieved by radish. Honey × scallion ("honey clashes with raw scallion") — no harmful reports in modern toxicology. Tannin-containing herbs/strong tea × alkaloid-containing herbs (Coptis/Phellodendron) — [Consistent · fairly well established] precipitative binding has pharmacological corroboration (not clinical-verification grade). The traditional medicine-timing rules (tonics before meals / dewormers on an empty stomach / calming formulas at bedtime) — the modern face is not known; parts coincide with pharmacokinetics by accident, and this is not grounds for promotion [L3:B2].

### 5.4 The Eighteen Antagonisms and the Nineteen Fears: the framework and the honesty boundaries [TCM·Traditional]

A traditional summary of compatibility prohibitions, carried in mnemonic verses (see *Rumen Shiqin* and other classics). The core of the Eighteen Antagonisms = three groups: the aconite group, the licorice group (antagonizing gansui 甘遂 / daji 大戟 / haizao 海藻 / yuanhua 芫花), and the veratrum group (antagonizing ginseng, shashen 沙参, danshen, and others); the core of the Nineteen Fears = sulfur fears puxiao 朴硝 (mirabilite), clove fears yujin 郁金, ginseng fears wulingzhi 五灵脂, and others (the full verses are in L3:B3). Honesty boundaries: ① in the original meaning of the seven classical compatibility relations, "fear" (wei) meant "its toxicity is suppressed by another herb" (a beneficial face); the verses' use of it as a prohibition carries a **historical superimposition at the concept level** and must not be read literally as the modern "interaction"; ② some entries (licorice × haizao, clove × yujin) already have opposing experiences of combined use inside traditional clinical practice, and modern research conclusions are not uniform; ③ **the framework as a whole is neither promoted into modern evidence nor negated** [L3:B3].

### 5.5 The Chinese-Western consistent face [Consistent]

The dual-framework cross-confirmed entries already collected in Table 4-1: warfarin × danggui/danshen/ginkgo/ginseng, ephedra × MAOI, bitter apricot kernels/peach kernels × centrally acting cough medicines, cinnabar (mercury-containing preparations) × bromides/iodides (contraindicated), licorice × diuretics/digitalis (potassium wasting amplifies digitalis toxicity) [L3:B4·FACT 为主].

### 5.6 Honesty boundary: the evidence for herb × Western-drug interactions is thin

Apart from the few entries above, **the vast majority of Chinese-herb × Western-medicine combinations have no systematic study** (the not-known tier) — which is exactly the reason "always report your herbal list at the visit": unknown does not equal safe; only with knowledge can there be monitoring.

**Summary**: the traditional face presented as it is; the modern face given an evidence grade item by item; the blank face stated plainly as not known.

## Chapter 6. The medication-taker's diet self-check tree and timing quick-reference

**Learning objectives**: be able to use the self-check tree to locate "which class my medicine belongs to → which diet questions to ask."

### Figure 6-1 The medication-taker's diet self-check tree

```
Start: list everything I am currently taking (prescription medicines + OTC medicines + Chinese herbal products)
├─ First question: do I take any of the "four life-saving" medicines? [L3:C1/B4·SH·PG#21]
│   ├─ Warfarin → leafy greens constant + report any herbal start/stop to the anticoagulation clinic + check INR (§3.3)
│   ├─ Nitrates (including the sublingual emergency medicine) → ED drugs and poppers contraindicated (§3.2)
│   ├─ Lithium → salt, water, and coffee habits stay stable (§3.4)
│   └─ St. John's wort → hands off, ask the doctor (§3.1)
```

## Chapter 7. FAQ (10 questions)

1. **If the package insert lists no food prohibitions, is it safe?** Not necessarily. The minerals inside multivitamins and herbs of the St. John's wort kind are both "entry points the package insert does not mention" — run the three questions of Chapter 4 yourself [L3:C1/C2].
2. **What if someone who drinks milk for calcium needs antibiotics?** Tetracyclines: 1-2 h before the milk or 4-6 h after; quinolones: 2 h before and after; calcium supplements get the same timing offset [L3:A2-1/2].
3. **How long should the coffee wait, exactly?** For levothyroxine tablets: morning, empty stomach, plain water, with coffee postponed at least 30-60 minutes (1 h to be conservative). Liquid formulations are unaffected — the formulation is the key variable [L3:A2-3].
4. **Does warfarin mean no greens ever again?** The opposite — keep eating leafy greens, at a constant daily amount; what is dangerous is the swinging. A DOAC does not even need the stability [L3:C4].
5. **Which medicines clash with grapefruit juice?** Felodipine, nifedipine, cyclosporine, some statins (the levels-rise face), sildenafil (same direction). If unsure, ask the pharmacist [L3:A1·N2·SH].
6. **Herbs and Western medicines two hours apart, and it's all fine?** Only partly true for the "glue" class; the "accelerator-and-brake" class (enzyme induction, effect stacking) has nothing to do with intervals — St. John's wort, and warfarin × danggui/danshen, act just the same hours apart [L3:C1/B4].
7. **Should I heed "tea neutralizes medicine"?** Half of it: tannins chelating iron and alkaloids has pharmacological grounding (Consistent · fairly well established), so avoiding strong tea while taking alkaloid-herb formulas and iron supplements is reasonable; "neutralizes every medicine" is an overgeneralization [L3:B1/B2-3].
8. **What should I stop before a checkup blood draw?** High-dose biotin ≥2-3 days beforehand (convention), and declare it; the most practical move is handing your supplement list to the doctor who orders the tests [L3:C6].
9. **Is "cephalosporins with alcohol — gone before you know it" real?** Half real: cephalosporins with an MTT side chain and metronidazole can indeed cause the disulfiram-like reaction; avoid alcohol during treatment + ≥3 days after stopping (clinical convention in China, not RCT — labeled as such); cephalosporins without that side chain are lower-risk, but the general rule is to avoid alcohol [L3:A2-8].
10. **I take five medicines — how do I self-check in one pass?** Walk the four questions of Figure 6-1, then take the list to a doctor or pharmacist for the final review — the self-check tree filters the priorities; professionals decide the plan (the red line of Chapter 2).

## Chapter 8. Self-test (10 questions with answers)

1. Why is St. John's wort dangerous? — Its high-hyperforin content **induces CYP3A4 and P-glycoprotein**, so substrate medicines' blood levels fall systemically; casualties include: immunosuppressants, oral contraceptives, anticoagulants. [L3:C1]
2. Which "hidden nitrates" are inside the nitrate red line? — The sublingual nitroglycerin kept for emergencies and the inhaled poppers fall under the same contraindication; they run through the same NO-cGMP pathway as PDE5 inhibitors, and stacking can be fatal. [SH:§3.1]
3. What are the directions of warfarin × vitamin K and × ginseng? — Vitamin K: **stable, not quit** (leafy greens constant); ginseng: the reverse, **weakening warfarin** (INR falls · MODEL · even more monitoring needed). [L3:C4/B4]
4. The mechanism of lithium × sodium, and two everyday scenarios? — Sodium and lithium compete for reabsorption through the same channel; low sodium → lithium retained → toxicity (FACT); scenarios: dieting/extreme salt restriction, heavy sweating, or diarrhea with dehydration. [PG#21]
5. Why does tetracycline fail with milk, and which drug is the exception? — Calcium ions **chelate** it into an insoluble lump, absorption near zero; doxycycline is affected less (still avoid milk; the package insert prevails). [L3:A2-1]
6. The complete way to take a bisphosphonate? — Morning plain water 200 ml → stay upright 30-60 min → only then eat or take other medicines (less than 1% of the oral dose reaches the blood). [L3:A2-5]
7. Is grapefruit juice an inhibitor or an inducer? — An **inhibitor** of intestinal CYP3A4 → levels **rise** → side-effect risk; casualties: felodipine/nifedipine/cyclosporine/some statins. [L3:A1]
8. Give one example of each piece of the mechanism trio. — Assembly line (St. John's wort induction), porters (levodopa × high protein competing for the transporter), glue and accelerator-and-brake (tetracycline × calcium, cephalosporins × alcohol). [L3:A/C 节]
9. How should "ginseng clashes with radish" be labeled epistemically? — [TCM·Traditional], recorded as it stands; the modern-evidence face is UNKNOWN = "not known"; even inside the tradition there are opposing experiences. [L3:B2-1]
10. This book's red line in one sentence? — This book is not a medication-stopping guide; before adding or stopping anything, ask your doctor or pharmacist first, in the order "report first, adjust later." [第 2 章]
