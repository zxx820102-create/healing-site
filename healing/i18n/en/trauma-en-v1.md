# Trauma and PTSD Canon v1 (2026-09-12)

> This volume is the healing bookshelf's **trauma and PTSD** volume. It answers the three questions asked most often: Is what I'm experiencing "normal"? Will I never get better? What can the people around me do?
> **Discipline of honesty**: every hard number verified verbatim to the PMID title or abstract level (channel = TinyFish search/fetch + Europe PMC REST; the built-in WebSearch/WebFetch were not used; details in the receipt); epistemic states never promoted unilaterally; UNKNOWN a first-class citizen. This volume is an **evidence map + action cards**, not a diagnostic tool — diagnosis and treatment decisions always belong to clinicians.

---

## 0. One-line collapse

**Trauma exposure is common, PTSD is the minority (lifetime prevalence ≈5.6% among the exposed — most people are resilient; that is a real number, not a comfort); PTSD is a measurable change in the threat-detection circuitry, not weakness; untreated, it chronicles into chronicity; first-line therapy is trauma-focused psychotherapy (PE/CPT/EMDR side by side), medication is adjunctive, benzodiazepines are not recommended, and cannabis/MDMA remain at the evidence frontier; the people around you help most by stabilizing and accompanying, and harm most by pushing "snap out of it."**

## 1. The trauma-response spectrum: the timeline dividing normal stress from PTSD

### 1.1 The traumatic event itself (definitional cell · FACT)

In the clinical context, a "traumatic event" is not "something very distressing"; it means **exposure to actual or threatened death, serious injury, or sexual violence** (DSM-5 Criterion A's four pathways: direct experience / witnessing / learning about it / repeated exposure). DSM-5 additionally requires the symptom cluster to persist ≥1 month and cause clinically significant distress or functional impairment. Source: DSM-5 (2013) definitional text, epistemic state FACT (definitional fact).

### 1.2 Four timeline tiers (dividing-line cell · FACT/MODEL)

| Time tier | Name | Status | Key points |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hours–days after the event | **Normal stress reaction** | Most symptoms in most people | Insomnia, flashbacks, numbness, hypervigilance, self-blame are all normal features of a high-arousal state; fluctuating, soothable, not disabling across the board. At this stage **no diagnosis is needed and no forced recall** |
| 3 days–1 month | **ASD acute stress disorder** (DSM-5) | A minority | ≥9 symptoms across the five classes (intrusion / negative mood / dissociation / avoidance / arousal), with functional impairment; it is the severe form of the stress reaction, not PTSD's mandatory way-station |
| 1–3 months | **PTSD window** (acute phase) | Diagnosable once criteria are met | Symptoms ≥1 month satisfies PTSD's time criterion; intervention works well in this window |
| >1 month (some meet full criteria only at ≥6 months) | **PTSD (chronic / delayed-onset)** | Needs systematic treatment | "Delayed expression" is a formal DSM-5 specifier (full criteria ≥6 months from the event); it is real (see §3.4) |

Epistemic note: the dividing times are diagnostic definitions (FACT); "most people with ASD do not develop PTSD, and ASD has limits as a predictor of PTSD" is clinical follow-up literature consensus (MODEL — no dedicated search anchoring was done this round; labeled as such).

### 1.3 The resilience real number: most trauma-exposed people do not develop PTSD (FACT · honest hope)

The WHO World Mental Health surveys (WMH · 26 countries · 71,083 people · PMID 28385165, abstract verbatim): **lifetime PTSD prevalence is 3.9% in the full sample and 5.6% among those exposed to trauma**. Direct reading: **of people exposed to trauma, about 94.4% never meet PTSD criteria in their lifetime**. This is not whitewashing; it is the measured structure across 26 countries. Resilience is not a slogan; it is the majority in the data.

At the same time, honestly: once PTSD forms, **half report persistent symptoms** (same anchor: "Half of respondents with PTSD reported persistent symptoms"), and the US NCS cohort showed more than a third of patients **fail to recover even after many years** (PMID 7492257, abstract verbatim: "more than one third of people with an index episode of PTSD fail to recover even after many years"). So the correct posture is: **no panic, and no delay** — most people recover spontaneously or with brief intervention; if the full cluster persists past 1–3 months, treat it as an illness that needs treatment, not as something to "wait out."

## 2. Epidemiology: real numbers and framings (FACT)

### 2.1 The US framing (Kessler family) and the global framing (WHO WMH), **labeled separately**

| Framing | Number | Source |
|---|---|---|
| US lifetime prevalence | **6.8%** | NCS-R (2001-2003, n=9,282) diagnostic-interview data; NIMH official statistics page verbatim (fetched and verified this round); underlying paper PMID 15939837 |
| US past-year prevalence | **3.6%** (women 5.2% / men 1.8%) | Same (NIMH verbatim) |
| US earlier framing (DSM-III-R) | Lifetime **7.8%** | NCS 1995 (n=5,877) · PMID 7492257 abstract verbatim |
| Global cross-national lifetime prevalence | **3.9%** (full sample) / **5.6%** (exposed) | WHO WMH 26-country survey · PMID 28385165 abstract verbatim |

Read side by side: the US numbers run above the cross-national mean, partly reflecting differences in instruments and the exposure spectrum — **any single prevalence figure must be cited with its framing and year**; that is this volume's hard rule.

### 2.2 High-risk exposure groups (FACT)

- **First responders**: 28 studies, 40 samples, 20,424 rescue workers, pooled **current prevalence 10%** (PMID 21681455, abstract verbatim) — about three times the general population's past-year rate; wide heterogeneity between police / fire / EMS / nursing (PMID 33542696 cross-sectional supplement: different roles carry different risk for the same event).
- **Trauma-type distribution** (Kessler 1995 · PMID 7492257, abstract verbatim): the exposures most associated with PTSD in men are **combat exposure and witnessing**; in women, **rape and sexual harassment** — civilian trauma (sexual violence, accidents, witnessing violence) is PTSD's main stage; war is only one branch.
- Social disadvantage compounds risk: younger age, female sex, being unmarried, lower education, lower income, and unemployment are associated with elevated lifetime PTSD risk in exposed populations (PMID 28385165, abstract verbatim).

### 2.3 Comorbidity (FACT · interlinks with the mood: volume)

PTSD is **strongly comorbid** with other lifetime mental disorders (PMID 7492257, abstract verbatim, "strongly comorbid"); the two heaviest channels are **depression** (→ the `mood:` volume: when comorbid, both ends need treatment; antidepressants alone often disappoint for the depression of untreated PTSD) and **substance use** (→ §4.5, the "drinking to settle the nerves" channel). Sleep collapse and nightmares in trauma populations: see the `slp:` volume (CBT-I and imagery rehearsal can be combined).

### 2.4 The treatment gap (FACT · interlinks with the care: volume)

WMH: among those with PTSD reporting any treatment, **53.5%** in high-income countries, **22.8%** in low/lower-middle-income countries, **28.7%** in upper-middle-income; only about half of severe cases receive any treatment, and specialist mental-health services less still (PMID 28385165, abstract verbatim). → the `care:` volume: access is the structural bottleneck, not a matter of personal will.

## 3. The four core symptom clusters (DSM-5 · FACT), and "PTSD ≠ weakness"

### 3.1 The four clusters (diagnostic definitions · FACT)

1. **Intrusion**: flashbacks, nightmares, intense and prolonged psychological distress and physiological reactivity to internal/external cues.
2. **Avoidance**: avoiding trauma-related memories/thoughts (internal) and people/places/conversations/situations (external).
3. **Negative alterations in cognition and mood**: persistent negative beliefs ("I am ruined," "no one can be trusted"), distorted self-blame, persistent negative emotional state, diminished interest, feelings of detachment, persistent inability to experience positive emotions.
4. **Marked alterations in arousal and reactivity**: exaggerated startle, sleep disturbance, irritability/outbursts, difficulty concentrating, reckless or self-destructive behavior.

Diagnosis requires: exposure + multiple items per cluster + persistence >1 month + functional impairment (the full DSM-5 criteria are the clinician's work; this volume offers no self-diagnosis checklist, only a "when to seek care" line — §6).

### 3.2 "PTSD ≠ weakness": the neurobiological anchor (FACT)

A meta-level neuroimaging review (PMID 19625997, abstract verbatim): in PTSD, **amygdala activation to trauma-related stimuli is relatively elevated** (the threat-detection system amplified), and, unlike other anxiety disorders, PTSD comes with **reduced reactivity of the rostral ACC and adjacent ventromedial prefrontal cortex** (the "brake" system on threat responses turned down). In one line: **PTSD is a measurable change in the threat-detection circuitry** — exposure-driven circuit remodeling, not a character flaw. The rescue workers' 10% current prevalence (exposure-dependent) and the sex difference also show this is the result of "exposure × individual differences," not a willpower scoreboard.

### 3.3 "PTSD ≠ weakness" does not mean "everyone's vulnerability factors are the same" (MODEL · honest boundaries)

Same exposure, different outcomes, tells us individual differences (genetics / prior history / social support) are real; this volume does no individual risk scoring — any self-rating quiz of the "trauma-prone personality test" type sits outside the evidence.

### 3.4 Delayed onset: stated honestly (FACT)

"I was fine at the time and collapsed months/years later" is a real phenomenon, with a formal DSM-5 specifier (full criteria at ≥6 months). Objective archival evidence (PMID 22348623, abstract verbatim): veterans who **fell ill only after discharge** were **indistinguishable** from non-PTSD controls on in-service behavioral measures, but their records held **significantly more misconduct entries before the exposure** (going AWOL, insubordination, and other externalizing markers) — that is, delayed onset often has traceable precursor signals, and is **not "faking" or "retrospective invention."** The most damaging narrative aimed at delayed onset is "it's been so long and you're still bringing it up" — time is not the enemy of testimony.

## 4. The honest ledger of interventions

### 4.1 First line: trauma-focused psychotherapy (FACT · no "miracle therapy" narrative)

The AHRQ-commissioned systematic review + meta (64 trials · PMID 26574151, abstract verbatim):
- **Exposure therapy (including manualized PE, prolonged exposure) has high strength of evidence (high SOE)**; cognitive therapy (CT), **CPT cognitive processing therapy**, and CBT-mixed are **moderate (moderate SOE)**; **EMDR and narrative exposure are low-moderate SOE**.
- Effect sizes for reducing PTSD symptoms are large (**Cohen's d of roughly −1.0 or larger** vs comparators); **NNT<4** for diagnostic remission (true of exposure/CPT/CT/CBT-mixed/EMDR alike).
- **Head-to-head comparisons are insufficient to rank them**; **adverse-event data are missing in most studies** (honesty: large effect ≠ zero dropout and zero discomfort; systematic measurement of dropout and harm is a known gap in this field).

Position statement: **PE/CPT/EMDR sit together on the first-line menu** — which one matters less than whether to do one at all; the shared active ingredient lives in the family of "structured processing of trauma memories + systematic de-sensitization of avoidance behavior" (the attribution of the active ingredient is MODEL). EMDR's mechanism (whether the eye movements are necessary) is an **unsettled dispute (UNKNOWN · §4.4)**, yet **the efficacy evidence places it on the first-line menu** — an unexplained mechanism and an established effect can coexist.

### 4.2 The honest face of early intervention: single-session mandated "debriefing" is not supported (FACT)

A Cochrane review (11 RCTs · PMID 12076399, abstract verbatim): for recent exposees, **single-session individual debriefing neither reduces psychological distress nor prevents PTSD** (3–5 month PTSD risk OR 1.22, 95% CI 0.60–2.46); **one trial reported significantly higher PTSD risk at 1 year among recipients (OR 2.88, 95% CI 1.11–7.53)**; no benefit for depression/anxiety either. → Inference for organizations and loved ones (INFERENCE): **what survivors and front-line staff need after an incident is safety, information, food, sleep and reconnection (psychological stabilization), not "retelling the event face-to-face, mandatorily."** The evidence runs against the intuition "talking it out heals" — one of this field's most important counter-intuitive findings.

### 4.3 SSRI/SNRI: effective but small effects, positioned as adjunct (FACT)

Systematic review + meta of 51 RCTs (PMID 25644881, abstract verbatim): SSRIs beat placebo overall but with **small effect sizes (SMD −0.23, 95% CI −0.33 to −0.12)**; drug by drug, **fluoxetine, paroxetine and venlafaxine** have significant small-effect evidence from two or more trials. Positioning (INFERENCE): **medication is not PTSD's first choice**; it serves comorbid depression (the `mood:` volume), symptom generalization, and unavailability of psychotherapy; the expectation that "a drug cures PTSD" does not hold. (That sertraline/paroxetine carry a US FDA indication for PTSD is a regulatory-label fact — the label was not re-verified this round; labeled MODEL with an honesty note.) Medication and imagery-rehearsal therapy for PTSD-related nightmares: see the `slp:` volume.

### 4.4 Benzodiazepines: no role / not recommended (FACT title-level + small-sample empirics; mechanism is MODEL)

- Title-level anchor (PMID 28747110, title verbatim): **"No role for benzodiazepines in posttraumatic stress disorder until supported by evidence"** — until such evidence appears, benzodiazepines have no place in PTSD.
- Prospective empirics (small sample · stated as such · PMID 9746445): early after trauma (2–18 days), clonazepam/alprazolam in 13 treated vs 13 matched controls: no difference on scales, but at 6 months **9/13 of the benzodiazepine group vs 3/13 of controls met PTSD criteria**. n=13; a single study cannot settle it; the direction agrees with the reviews: no early benefit, possibly a reverse signal.
- The review face of addiction/tolerance/dependence and withdrawal risk (PMID 29385731): a systematic evidence face on the cost side of benzodiazepines' short-term fast relief (adverse-effect spectrum) and on first-line alternatives (psychotherapy, serotonergic agents, etc.).
- **Mechanism hypothesis (MODEL)**: fear extinction learning depends on new learning about the threat memory; benzodiazepines' sedative/amnesic properties may **interfere with the encoding of extinction learning**, while relieving anxiety itself weakens natural exposure to trauma cues — this mechanism is **not directly confirmed at the RCT level in human PTSD**, and this volume honestly stops at MODEL.

### 4.5 Cannabis and MDMA: the evidence frontier, labeled honestly (FACT+UNKNOWN)

- **Cannabis**: an observational longitudinal study (2,276 veterans · PMID 26455669, abstract verbatim): those who **initiated cannabis after treatment worsened in PTSD symptoms (effect +0.34), with more violent behavior and more alcohol/drug use**; those who stopped had the lowest symptom levels. RCT-grade efficacy evidence: **two searches this round found no record of the 2023 multi-arm cannabis RCT (drop · see receipt); it stays UNKNOWN**. Conclusion (INFERENCE): "cannabis to settle the nerves" is treating the substance-comorbidity channel (§7, item five) as if it were treatment; the medical-cannabis policy dispute is a policy issue, not efficacy evidence.
- **MDMA-assisted therapy (MDMA-AT)**: a phase-3 double-blind RCT (n=90 · PMID 33972795, abstract verbatim): CAPS-5 reduction MDMA −24.4 vs placebo −13.9 (P<0.0001, **d=0.91**), SDS disability d=0.43. **But on 2024-08-09 the FDA issued a complete response letter declining approval**, asking for further safety/efficacy research (including another phase-3 trial; source NPR/Science reporting 2024-08-09, fetched and verified this round). → **MDMA-AT is a promising research pipeline, not an approved routine treatment**; any "psychedelics cure trauma" narrative does not match the regulatory reality. The inherent difficulties of sample size and blinding (psychedelics are hard to blind truly) noted as such (MODEL).

### 4.6 Intervention menu summary (INFERENCE)

| Need | First choice | Strength of support |
|---|---|---|
| Confirmed PTSD · function impaired | Trauma-focused psychotherapy (any of PE/CPT/EMDR) | High (NNT<4) |
| Comorbid depression / declines therapy / no access | SSRI/SNRI | Small effects |
| Nightmare-dominant | The `slp:` volume (CBT-I / imagery rehearsal) + a clinic visit | — |
| Acute phase (<1 month) | Stabilization + social support + sleep protection; **no benzodiazepines, no mandated debriefing** | FACT/MODEL |
| Currently using alcohol/cannabis to settle the nerves | The substance channel handled first (the `med:` / mood comorbidity pattern) | FACT (observational) |

## 5. The companion's guide (INFERENCE · action cards extrapolated from the evidence)

### 5.1 Why "move on / snap out of it / it's all behind you" harms

Three mechanisms (MODEL, extrapolated from the avoidance-extinction model): ① **invalidating the experience** — the very confirmation the person most needs ("what happened is real, and the reactions are normal") is cancelled by one sentence; ② **pressuring toward avoidance** — urging "don't think about it" trains avoidance behavior, and avoidance is precisely the core mechanism maintaining PTSD (§3.1, cluster two); ③ **closing the help-seeking channel** — "you need to pull yourself together" turns an illness into a moral assignment. Deleting these three sentences is the single biggest thing a companion can do.

### 5.2 What to do: stabilize, be present, follow the rhythm

- **Say**: "What happened was not your fault." "Whatever you're feeling is normal." "I'm here — whenever you want to talk, I'm here." **Ask** open-ended, without chasing detail: "Talk about the parts you want to; skip the rest."
- **Do**: help restore **rhythm** — meals, sleep, walks, a regular schedule (stabilization first is the shared ground rule of the acute phase and the treatment phase; interlinks with the `slp:` / `st:` volumes); accompanying the first step of getting care (booking / going along) beats a hundred exhortations.
- **Don't**: interrogate for detail; decide for the person whether they "should talk"; shake or slap during a flashback — instead lower your voice, give your name and the place to help reorientation; never promise confidentiality you cannot keep (if there is self-harm risk, §6 takes precedence over confidentiality).

### 5.3 When to encourage seeking care (linked to the §6 red flags)

Symptoms persisting past **1–3 months** with the full cluster intact; or any red flag of §6 appearing — the companion's task is not diagnosis but turning "see a trauma-specialized psychiatry / clinical psychology service" into one completable concrete step (choose a provider → book → accompany). The `care:` volume: access and cost are real concerns; choosing a path by accessibility is nothing to be ashamed of.

### 5.4 The companion's own burnout (interlinks with the agc: volume)

Family members who long accompany trauma survivors develop secondary traumatic stress (damaged sleep, irritability, numbness). The rule: **the companion's own sleep and respite are not luxuries; they are part of the care plan** — use respite services (the `agc:` volume), rotate care, permit yourself not to be on call 24 hours a day. A burned-out companion cannot help anyone.

## 6. Red flags for seeking care (any one = seek care promptly; with immediate action items)

1. **Self-harm/suicide signals** (interlinks with the `tm:` volume · the `mood:` comorbidity pattern): saying "I don't want to live / people would be better off without me," putting affairs in order, giving away treasured possessions, self-harm behavior, sudden calm despair. Meta-analytic evidence: PTSD is significantly associated with prior suicide attempts and current suicidal ideation; the association weakens but does not vanish when depression is controlled; **evidence for increased completed suicide is insufficient** (PMID 20112140, abstract verbatim) — clinically what must be prevented is the ideation→action transition, with risk higher when depression is comorbid. A red flag appearing = **immediately** the crisis hotline / the emergency department, accompanied (action cards in the `tm:` volume).
2. **Dissociative episodes**: losing time during flashbacks, depersonalization/derealization, brief memory gaps (especially the dissociative subtype in the ASD window) — this needs professional assessment, not folk handling of the philtrum-pressure kind.
3. **Escalating substance misuse**: climbing alcohol/cannabis/drug doses, morning use, failed quitting (the "drinking to settle the nerves" channel · §4.5/§7) — substances and PTSD feed each other; both must be addressed.
4. **Functional collapse**: unable to work/attend school, avoidance to the point of not leaving home, eating and sleep disordered across the board, violence at home or being victimized — reaching the "clinically significant impairment" threshold is itself the care-seeking line; do not wait for "worse."

## 7. The falsification face (each with its falsifier · this volume's falsifiability statement)

| # | Claim / proposition | Verdict | Evidence | Falsifier (what finding would shatter this verdict) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Time heals everything" | **CONTRADICTED** (for untreated PTSD) | >1/3 of patients fail to recover for years (PMID 7492257); half of cases worldwide report persistent symptoms (PMID 28385165) | If untreated natural-course cohorts showed >50% self-remitting within 1 year with chronicization <20%, "treat when indicated" would downgrade to optional |
| 2 | "PTSD = weakness / oversensitivity" | **CONTRADICTED** | Measurable change in threat-detection circuitry: amygdala elevated + rostral ACC/vmPFC reduced (PMID 19625997); rescue workers' 10% current prevalence distributed with exposure (PMID 21681455) | If circuit differences were shown to stem mainly from pre-exposure personality traits and to be independent of exposure dose, the "circuit remodeling" wording would need rewriting |
| 3 | "You must recall/recount the trauma in detail to heal" | **CONTRADICTED** (for mandated single-session debriefing) | Single-session debriefing does not prevent PTSD; one RCT showed 1-year risk rising OR 2.88 (PMID 12076399); stabilization-first is the acute-phase principle | If multi-country RCTs showed mandated single-session debriefing significantly prevents PTSD with no harm signal, the acute-phase "no forced recall" rule would retire |
| 4 | "EMDR / energy therapy: a mystical mechanism cures all" | **MODEL boundary + UNKNOWN** | EMDR's efficacy evidence is low-moderate SOE, NNT<4 (PMID 26574151); the eye-movement-specific mechanism is unproven | If dismantling trials consistently showed the eye-movement component unnecessary and equivalence with any exposure-based therapy, the "specific mechanism" would be falsified (efficacy retained, mechanism reduced to exposure); if evidence emerged beating PE against comparators, it would upgrade |
| 5 | "Drinking / cannabis settles the nerves" | **CONTRADICTED** | Cannabis initiation → symptom worsening +0.34, more violent behavior (PMID 26455669); strong PTSD-substance comorbidity (PMID 7492257); SSRIs' small effects (PMID 25644881) underline that misusing drugs with no efficacy is even less defensible | If prospective RCTs showed cannabis/alcohol improving long-term PTSD outcomes without a dependence channel, this verdict would be overturned (status quo: RCT evidence UNKNOWN · see the receipt's drop record) |
| 6 | "PTSD only happens in war" | **CONTRADICTED** | Trauma exposure is common worldwide; civilian PTSD lifetime 3.9%; the sex split: women's main channel is sexual violence (PMID 28385165/7492257) | If general-population surveys showed PTSD almost confined to combat exposure, the civilian narrative would be void (contradicting all epidemiology) |
| 7 | "Psychotherapy = repeatedly tearing the wound open, the more you treat the worse it gets" | **CONTRADICTED** (for first-line therapies) | Trauma-focused therapy's effects are large (d~−1.0), NNT<4 (PMID 26574151); what harms is **unstructured mandated debriefing**, not structured treatment | If registry studies showed trauma-focused therapy's dropout/harm rates significantly above controls with lasting worsening, the "safety" wording would downgrade (status quo: systematic absence of adverse-event reporting is a known gap, stated as such) |
| 8 | "One pill cures PTSD" | **CONTRADICTED** | SSRI SMD −0.23, a small effect (PMID 25644881); benzodiazepines have no place (PMID 28747110/9746445) | If a new drug's phase 3 showed medium-to-large effects with successful replication, the medication positioning would upgrade |

## 8. UNKNOWN and honest gaps (first-class citizens)

1. **EMDR's specific mechanism** (whether the eye movements are necessary): two searches found no mechanism meta (drop record in the receipt) — UNKNOWN.
2. **RCT-grade efficacy of cannabis for PTSD**: no bibliographic record of that trial obtained this round — UNKNOWN; the observational negative signal is stated above.
3. **Systematic measurement of adverse events / dropout in trauma-focused therapy**: the AHRQ review itself states most studies lack it — a known evidence gap.
4. **Representative PTSD prevalence and delayed-onset distribution in China (Chinese-language contexts)**: not searched this round — UNKNOWN, left to a later lane.
5. **The precise ASD→PTSD conversion rate**: no dedicated search anchoring — handled this round as "model consensus" (MODEL).
