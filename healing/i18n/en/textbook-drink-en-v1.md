# The Drink Textbook v1 (2026-09-12)

**Readers**: self-learners and health workers. **How to use**: every chapter has learning objectives and a summary; the section-end anchor ([L3:id]) leads back to the evidence layer (assertion + sources + falsifier). This textbook never states observational evidence as causation, and never states classification, guideline, or mechanistic evidence as hard outcomes — epistemic states are aligned verbatim with L3, zero promotion.

## Chapter 0. The one-page version (L1 · zero-background readable)

> Abridged companion text: this chapter is a condensed transcription of `docs/healing/textbook/drink-l1-80yi-20260912.md`, not word-identical; the companion is the fuller, supplemented version and can be distributed on its own.

**In one sentence: water is the default-optimal drink — drink when thirsty, watch your urine color, no need to count cups. Coffee and tea can stay in moderation but not scalding hot; alcohol has no safe level; sugary drinks are the only "drink it and it is pure loss" beverage; sugar substitutes are a transition tool for quitting sugar, not a health food.**

Hold every claim to the four tiers: confirmed by research → fairly well established → still being studied → not known.

**Seven action cards**: ① drink when thirsty, and pale-yellow urine means you have had enough (thirst = confirmed by research; urine color = practical inference) ② don't chug water — the kidneys process roughly 0.7-1 L per hour; in endurance exercise drink to thirst (confirmed by research) ③ let hot drinks cool 3-5 minutes — above 65°C counts as "probably carcinogenic" (confirmed by research) ④ if you flush from drinking, don't take one extra drop — esophageal cancer risk multiplies, and antihistamines that mask the flush do not count as a solution (confirmed by research) ⑤ coffee's benefit evidence is strongest at 3-4 cups/day (still being studied · observational); choose paper-filtered drip (confirmed by research) ⑥ no strong tea with meals — leave 1 hour after eating (confirmed by research) ⑦ swap sugary drinks for water (still being studied · multiple consistent observations); sugar-free beats sugared, but the World Health Organization does not recommend sugar substitutes for weight control.

**Myth quick-check**: 8 cups of water (no basis) ｜ drinking more water to "detoxify" (no evidence) ｜ tea prevents cancer (not found) ｜ coffee de-calcifies the bones (exaggerated) ｜ red wine protects the heart / alcohol aids sleep (zero safe level; the sleep aid is an illusion) ｜ diet cola = healthy (water is the default-optimal). Not known: hard water × cardiovascular disease, long-term hard outcomes of sugar substitutes, independent evidence for each tea type. Special populations (pregnancy/breastfeeding caffeine ≤200 mg/day · liver disease · on medication) ask a doctor first.

## Chapter 1. Concepts and terminology

**Learning objectives**: be able to define total water intake, water intoxication, half-life, IARC groups, and the ADI, and to read evidence-grading language.

### 1.1 Total water intake and water balance

**Total water intake** = drinking water + other beverages + water in food; only the three summed is the authoritative framing of "how much you drink". **Osmolality** is the concentration of substances dissolved in the body's water; the body relies on it to decide whether to feel thirsty and whether to shed or hold water. In the hypothalamus (the brain region that governs water balance), osmoreceptors + volume receptors form a dual channel: one path generates the thirst sensation, the other directs **antidiuretic hormone (AVP)** — a hormone that makes the kidneys excrete less water — and together they hold body fluid steady. [L3:A1 FACT]

### 1.2 Water intoxication and hyponatremia

Drinking plain water faster than the kidneys can shed it dilutes blood sodium: **dilutional hyponatremia** — when blood sodium falls too low, water enters brain cells and causes cerebral edema, which can be life-threatening; when this happens in an exercise setting it is called **exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH)**, and endurance events such as marathons are the high-risk setting. [L3:A2 FACT]

### 1.3 Caffeine kinetics

**Half-life** is the time it takes for a substance's blood concentration to fall by half — caffeine's half-life varies widely between individuals, and it decides "how much of that afternoon cup is still with you at night". How fast it is cleared is decided by the **CYP1A2** gene (the main enzyme that metabolizes caffeine in the liver): people come in "fast-metabolizer" and "slow-metabolizer" types. [L3:B2 FACT]

### 1.4 Hazard-classification language

**IARC groups** are the carcinogen classification of an agency under the World Health Organization: Group 1 = carcinogenic to humans (e.g., alcohol's acetaldehyde); 2A = probably carcinogenic (e.g., very hot beverages >65°C); 2B = possibly carcinogenic, limited evidence (e.g., aspartame); 3 = not classifiable with current evidence (coffee itself). The group speaks of **evidence strength**, not **the size of the danger**. The **ADI (acceptable daily intake)** is a lifetime daily intake ceiling set by regulators, with a large safety margin built in.

### 1.5 Lipids and brewing

**LDL** (low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, the "bad cholesterol") is raised by the **diterpenes** in coffee (cafestol and other fat-soluble substances) — diterpenes survive only in brewing methods that skip the paper filter.

### 1.6 Alcohol metabolism and the East Asian specificity

Alcohol is first converted into **acetaldehyde** (a Group 1 carcinogen), then detoxified by the **ALDH2** enzyme. East Asian populations carry inactive ALDH2 variants at high frequency: acetaldehyde piles up → capillaries dilate → **alcohol flushing** — a genetic signal you can self-check.

### 1.7 The world of sugar and sweetness

**SSB (sugar-sweetened beverages)** = soft drinks, juice drinks, milk tea, and the like with sugar added. **Non-sugar sweeteners (NSS, commonly called sugar substitutes)** = high-intensity sweeteners such as aspartame and sucralose, plus sugar alcohols such as erythritol. **Reverse causation** is a common trap in observational research — "people who drink sugar-free drinks are fatter" may mean fatter people choose sugar-free more often, not that sugar-free makes people fat.

### 1.8 Evidence grading and epistemic states

This textbook carries over L3's five epistemic states, mapped onto the plain-language tiers (Table 1-1).

Table 1-1 Epistemic states vs the plain-language tiers

| L3 epistemic state | Plain-language tier | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| FACT | confirmed by research | verified research conclusions, regulatory consensus, or textbook consensus |
| MODEL | fairly well established | holds at the level of a clear mechanism / a single trial / surrogate markers |
| INFERENCE | still being studied | observational association or extrapolation; causation unproven |
| CONTESTED | still contested in the field | high-quality evidence in mutual conflict |
| UNKNOWN | not known | honestly not known — don't trust any assertion |

### Glossary (in order of appearance)

1. **Total water intake**: drinking water + beverages + food water, the three summed — the authoritative framing of "how much you drink".
2. **Antidiuretic hormone (AVP)**: the hormone that makes the kidneys excrete less water; works with thirst to steady water balance.
3. **Half-life**: the time for a substance's blood concentration to fall by half; decides how long the effect lingers.
4. **CYP1A2**: the main enzyme metabolizing caffeine in the liver; genes split people into fast and slow types.
5. **Diterpenes (cafestol)**: fat-soluble substances in coffee; raise LDL; trapped by the paper filter.
6. **LDL**: low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, the "bad cholesterol", the raw material of atherosclerotic plaque.
7. **IARC groups**: the WHO's evidence-strength classification of carcinogens (1/2A/2B/3).
8. **ADI**: acceptable daily intake; a lifetime daily ceiling with a safety margin.
9. **Acetaldehyde**: alcohol's first metabolite, a Group 1 carcinogen; causes the flush.
10. **ALDH2**: the enzyme that detoxifies acetaldehyde; East Asians carrying the high-frequency variant always flush.
11. **SSB**: sugar-sweetened beverages — sugared soft drinks / juice drinks / milk tea.
12. **Non-sugar sweeteners (NSS)**: sugar substitutes — aspartame, sucralose, sugar alcohols, and so on.
13. **Reverse causation**: the observational trap where the "effect" is really the "cause" (fatter people choose sugar-free more often).
14. **Sick-quitter confounding**: former drinkers who quit over illness get counted into the "non-drinking" group, faking the illusion that moderate drinking is healthier.
15. **Mendelian randomization**: a study design that uses inborn genetic differences to mimic random allocation and test causation.
16. **Hard outcomes**: health results that really matter, such as death and diagnosed disease (as opposed to intermediate markers like blood lipids).

**Summary**: read intake through total water; water intoxication is dilution damaging the brain; caffeine is read through half-life and genes; IARC groups speak of evidence strength, not danger size; flushing = the ALDH2 genetic signal; before believing any conclusion, check which evidence tier it stands on.

## Chapter 2. Water: the default optimum, with boundaries

**Learning objectives**: be able to answer how much to actually drink per day, when drinking water becomes dangerous, and whether tap water needs filtering.

### 2.1 The verdict on "8 cups"

"8 cups (about 1.9 L) of plain water a day" has no physiological basis. The authoritative framing is total water intake: the US NAM 2004 set adequate intake at 3.7 L/day for men and 2.7 L/day for women (official framing); food itself contributes about 20% — it does not all come from drinking. [L3:A1] The healthy person's thirst mechanism (the hypothalamic dual channel) is the reliable primary regulator (confirmed by research); "drink more water to detox / beautify / prevent disease" has no evidence in people who drink normally (confirmed by research · negative evidence). [L3:A1] The practical gauge: drink when thirsty, and pale-yellow urine means enough — dark yellow = short of water, nearly clear = trending toward excess (practical inference). [L3:A1] Exceptions: people exercising or in hot environments, and older adults with blunted thirst sensation, need to drink **proactively**. [L3:A1 FACT]

Figure 2-1 Drinking self-check decision tree

```
Thirsty right now?
├─ Thirsty → drink water, until you are no longer thirsty
└─ Not thirsty → check urine color
    ├─ Pale yellow → sufficient; keep drinking to thirst
    ├─ Dark yellow → top up a cup of water
    └─ Nearly clear → you are overdrinking; slow down
Exceptions (drink proactively, don't wait for thirst):
· Exercise/heat → drink to thirst, but no scheduled chugging (see 2.2)
· Older adults → thirst sensation blunts; family offers water on timed reminders
```

### 2.2 Water's dangerous boundary: water intoxication

Excess plain water → dilutional hyponatremia → cerebral edema. The main cause of exercise-associated hyponatremia (EAH) is **excess hypotonic fluid (plain boiled water is one) + abnormal AVP suppression of excretion**, not "losing salt in sweat" (confirmed by research). [L3:A2] The boundary: a healthy kidney's maximal water excretion is about 0.7-1.0 L per hour (fairly well established · mechanistic model); chugging upwards of 4-5 L over a few hours enters the danger zone. [L3:A2] Endurance events such as marathons are the high-risk setting for EAH — drink to thirst, not to the aid-station schedule (confirmed by research · extrapolated). [L3:A2]

### 2.3 Water quality: boiling, lead, and water filters

- **Boiling**: Chinese urban tap water meets residual-chlorine disinfection standards; boiling = a microbiologically safe and cheap drinking plan (official framing); new evidence shows boiling also markedly cuts microplastic intake (still being studied · a single new study) — boiling has been upgraded from "habit" to "an evidence-backed measure". [L3:A3]
- **Lead**: lead has no safe threshold; the lead-plated/lead-soldered pipes and brass faucets of older buildings are a real exposure face (confirmed by research · the hazard face); there are no data on the current state of Chinese urban pipe networks (not known). Running the water 30 seconds to 2 minutes before filling, and drinking from the cold tap, is the official low-cost countermeasure. [L3:A3]
- **Hard water**: whether limescale-heavy water is linked to cardiovascular disease is still contested in the field — new cohorts exist but direction and magnitude are unsettled; not a basis for switching your water (the "not known" tier). [L3:A3]
- **Water filters**: activated carbon works on chlorine taste, some organic substances, and some heavy metals (official framing); for gains in health hard outcomes there is no randomized-trial evidence (not known); **an unchanged filter cartridge = a biofilm source, worse than not filtering** (confirmed by research). [L3:A3]

**Summary**: drink to thirst + watch urine color and that is enough — 8 cups is dogma; water intoxication's real threshold is "pouring in more than 1 liter per hour"; urban tap water only needs boiling, run the water first in older buildings, change filter cartridges often.

## Chapter 3. Coffee: it can stay, with conditions

**Learning objectives**: be able to state coffee's benefit range and evidence tier, how brewing methods affect blood lipids, the pregnancy and sleep boundaries, and how to handle cessation.

### 3.1 Dose and all-cause outcomes

An umbrella review (a pooling of many reviews): coffee consumption shows beneficial or neutral associations with most health outcomes, with peak benefit at about **3-4 cups/day**; beyond 6 cups benefit tapers and adverse signals rise (still being studied · observational synthesis, residual confounding unavoidable — **no causal claim made**). [L3:B1]

### 3.2 Genes: why coffee makes some people jittery

The CYP1A2 gene decides how fast caffeine is cleared — in fast metabolizers moderate coffee does not add heart-attack risk, while in slow metabolizers intake is associated with raised heart-attack risk (confirmed by research · the gene interaction exists; clinical extrapolation still being studied; no routine genetic testing recommended). [L3:B2] For sleep, see the sleep canon (400 mg of caffeine disrupts sleep when taken 0/3/6 hours before bed; the practical cutoff = at least 8 hours before bed). [睡正典C5·mindbody B1]

### 3.3 Blood pressure and pregnancy

A single caffeine dose raises blood pressure briefly (confirmed by research); habitual moderate consumption has a weak/neutral association with developing hypertension (still being studied); people with uncontrolled hypertension are sensitive to single doses and should individualize (still being studied). [L3:B3] **Caffeine ≤200 mg/day in pregnancy** is a regulatory consensus (confirmed by research · official framing); the more aggressive "no safe level" position exists but has not been adopted into guidelines. [L3:B3]

### 3.4 The verdict on "coffee de-calcifying"

The claim "coffee causes osteoporosis" does not hold, or is greatly exaggerated: recent nutrition surveys + Mendelian randomization + longitudinal analyses show coffee is neutral-or-no-decline for femoral-neck bone density (still being studied · multiple lines agree, the dispute is converging); with adequate calcium intake, 3-4 cups a day do not constitute a bone-risk signal. [L3:B4]

### 3.5 Brewing method: filtration is the key

Coffee diterpenes raise LDL — French press / Turkish / Scandinavian boiled styles have the most, espresso is in the middle, **paper-filtered drip / pour-over the least** (diterpenes are trapped by the paper) (confirmed by research); filtered coffee shows benign associations with cardiovascular outcomes (still being studied · observational + mechanism); heavy consumers of unfiltered coffee should get blood lipids checked (still being studied). [L3:B5]

### 3.6 The side-effect face

Caffeine-withdrawal headache is a formal diagnosis recognized by the International Classification of Headache Disorders, third edition (ICHD-3): it appears 12-24 hours after the last intake, peaks in 1-2 days, and for most resolves within 2-9 days (confirmed by research · diagnostic-criteria grade); to stop, taper down stepwise (practical inference). [L3:B6] Anxiety, palpitations, and worsened gastroesophageal reflux are all dose-dependent (confirmed by research); above 400 mg/day restlessness and insomnia appear; above 1 g, acute poisoning is rare but real (official framing). [L3:B6]

**Summary**: keep coffee inside three lines — 3-4 cups, paper-filtered, cutoff 8 hours before bed; ≤200 mg in pregnancy; the osteoporosis claim has been overturned; taper off stepwise when quitting.

## Chapter 4. Tea: not scalding, not strong, and not with meals

**Learning objectives**: be able to tell tea's cardiovascular evidence apart from its cancer evidence, and state the 65°C warning and the iron-absorption countermeasure.

### 4.1 Cardiovascular and cancer: graded verdicts

Umbrella reviews: tea intake leans protective in its association with cardiovascular disease — green and black tea point the same way but the effect size is small (still being studied · observational); tea shows **no significant overall association with cancer** — "tea prevents cancer" does not hold (confirmed by research · negative evidence). [L3:C1] The between-category differences of green/black/oolong are smaller than the within-category differences of dose and temperature; "anti-cancer / weight-loss" claims extrapolated from catechins lack sufficient evidence. [L3:C1]

### 4.2 Temperature: the 65°C Group 2A warning (the life-saving line)

IARC 2016: **very hot beverages >65°C = Group 2A (probably carcinogenic)**, target organ the esophagus; coffee itself was downgraded from 2B to Group 3 (confirmed by research · hazard classification). Mendelian randomization supports the causal direction "temperature preference → esophageal cancer" (still being studied · a reinforcing line). [L3:C2] In practice: **let it cool 3-5 minutes before drinking**; "drink it while hot" is an esophagus-endangering habit (practical advice). [L3:C2]

### 4.3 Strong tea's two pits: fluoride and iron

- **Fluoride**: brick-tea fluorosis (in populations drinking high-fluoride brick tea long-term) is a real fluoride disease that harms bone (confirmed by research); ordinary clear tea in moderation does not constitute a fluoride risk (still being studied · inference). [L3:C3]
- **Iron**: tea polyphenols inhibit the absorption of plant-source iron; **strong tea with meals is an iron-deficiency risk factor** (confirmed by research); **leaving 1 hour after the meal** markedly weakens the inhibition (confirmed by research); green tea inhibits less than black tea (confirmed by research). For iron-deficient and pregnant people: no strong tea at mealtimes (confirmed by research · practical). [L3:C3]

**Summary**: tea itself neither prevents nor causes cancer — the real risks are **temperature** and **strong tea with meals**; cool it 3-5 minutes and leave 1 hour after the meal, and those two lines dodge most of the pits.

## Chapter 5. Alcohol: zero safe level and the flush signal

**Learning objectives**: be able to explain why "moderation helps" collapsed, flushers' risk multiples, and the alcohol-related cancer spectrum.

### 5.1 The paradigm collapse of "moderation helps"

The Global Burden of Disease study (attribution across 195 countries): **the safest level of drinking = zero** — the benefits are canceled out by cancer and injury risk (confirmed by research · that study's conclusion). [L3:D1] The main artifact of the old "J curve" (the curve on which moderate drinkers appear healthier than non-drinkers) is **sick-quitter confounding** (former drinkers who quit over illness get counted into the "non-drinking" group) plus self-report bias (fairly well established · methodological); a Chinese gene-randomization study at the hundred-thousand-person scale shows the cardiovascular-protection effect does not hold in a causal framework (still being studied · that paper not verified word-for-word, labeled as-is). [L3:D1] Verdict: "a little alcohol protects the heart" as health advice **has been overturned**; the J curve exists as an epidemiological phenomenon (confirmed by research · the phenomenon face). [L3:D1] The sleep face: the faster sleep onset that alcohol buys is paid for with a shattered second half of the night and reduced REM (the stage of sleep where dreaming happens). [睡正典C2]

### 5.2 Asian flush: ALDH2 (East Asian specific · the life-saving card)

ALDH2 enzyme deficiency (a high-frequency East Asian variant) → acetaldehyde accumulates → flushing. **Flushers who keep drinking multiply their esophageal cancer risk** (confirmed by research); acetaldehyde itself is a Group 1 carcinogen (official framing). [L3:D2] Taking antihistamines to mask the flush **does not remove the acetaldehyde exposure** (still being studied · the mechanism face), and it is forbidden as an "I can drink" solution. [L3:D2] In practice: **flushing is one of the strongest self-checkable "don't drink" signals** (confirmed by research · warning grade). [L3:D2]

### 5.3 Dose and the cancer spectrum

The alcohol-attributed cancer spectrum: oropharynx, larynx, esophagus, liver, colorectum, breast (female) (official framing); under the linear no-threshold assumption every dose sits on the curve (still being studied · an assumption); the higher the dose and the earlier the start, the higher the risk (confirmed by research · consistent observation); quitting lowers risk, but risks such as esophageal cancer recede slowly (still being studied). [L3:D3]

**Summary**: alcohol has no "beneficial dose"; for flushers the risk multiplies and masking the flush does not work; "just a little is fine" does not hold on the cancer spectrum.

## Chapter 6. Sugary drinks and sugar substitutes: the only pure loss, and the transition tool

**Learning objectives**: be able to state the SSB evidence face, WHO's advice on sugar substitutes, and the three sentences on diet cola.

### 6.1 Sugary drinks

Systematic reviews: the highest vs lowest SSB consumption categories are associated with higher incidence of type 2 diabetes (still being studied · prospective observational synthesis; the mechanism is plausible: sugar load → glycemic load → body weight); the positive association with all-cause death is consistent across multiple cohorts (still being studied). [L3:E1] The benefit of substitution: replacing SSB with tea/coffee is associated with lower type 2 diabetes incidence (still being studied · actionable). [L3:E1] **Before reading any SSB study, check who paid for it**: in this field, systematic reviews have confirmed that sponsorship is systematically related to conclusions (confirmed by research · inherited from the diet canon). [L3:E1·diet L172]

### 6.2 The sweetener verdict

WHO's 2023 conditional recommendation: **do not use non-sugar sweeteners as a means of weight control** (official framing · the guideline exists; WHO itself grades the evidence quality low-to-very-low). [L3:E2] Aspartame: IARC assigns 2B (possibly carcinogenic · limited evidence), and JECFA upheld the ADI at 40 mg/kg body weight unchanged (confirmed by research · the classification and the ADI). 2B ≠ "causes cancer": a can of diet cola contains roughly 200-300 mg, and at everyday amounts the risk face is tiny (still being studied · a conversion inference). [L3:E2] Individual effects of sweeteners on blood sugar and the gut microbiome are small-trial leads (still being studied · not for practice); long-term hard-outcome randomized trials do not exist (not known). [L3:E2·G]

### 6.3 Diet cola in three sentences

① **Better than sugared cola** — the sugar load is gone (still being studied · inference); ② **not equal to a health food** — WHO advises against it as a weight-control tool, and the observational associations are polluted by reverse causation (fairly well established · methodological); ③ **water is the default optimum** (confirmed by research · the default). [L3:E2]

**Summary**: sugary drinks have no "beneficial floor" — swap them whenever you can; sweeteners are the scaffold for quitting sugar, not the destination; three sentences to remember what sugar-free drinks are and are not.

## Chapter 7. How to drink through a day (integration)

**Learning objectives**: be able to fold the whole book's framing into one daily schedule.

Figure 7-1 The "how to drink through a day" timeline (healthy adults · general version)

| Time | Water | Coffee | Tea | Alcohol |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Waking | drink to thirst; no "detox" | OK · paper-filtered drip preferred | no strong tea on an empty stomach | ✗ |
| Morning | dark urine → top up a cup | inside the 3-4 cups/day benefit range | OK · let it cool 3-5 minutes first | ✗ |
| Lunch | small amounts with the meal are fine | caution if reflux-sensitive | **no strong tea with the meal** (iron) | ✗ |
| Afternoon | drink when thirsty | caution after 14:00 — **cutoff 8 h before bed** | let it cool before drinking | ✗ |
| Dinner | small amounts | ✗ | weak tea OK · 1 h after the meal is better | **zero safe level**; flushers go to zero |
| Before bed | small sips, to spare night trips | ✗ | ✗ | ✗ (wrecks the second half of the night's sleep [睡正典C2]) |

Exceptions: exercise/heat — drink to thirst, no chugging (2.2); older adults — blunted thirst sensation, offer water on a schedule (2.1); pregnancy/medication/liver disease, see Chapter 8.

Summary: not one row of this table is a "must" — only boundaries: water's floor is thirst; coffee's ceiling is 400 mg and 8 hours before bed; tea's no-go zones are 65°C and strong tea with meals; alcohol's reasonable amount is zero.

## Chapter 8. Special populations and the medical boundary

**Learning objectives**: be able to recognize the populations who need to follow a doctor's advice rather than read from the book.

Table 8-1 Population-specific framing

| Population | Key framing | Tier and boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Pregnancy/breastfeeding | caffeine ≤200 mg/day; no strong tea with meals (iron); alcohol zero | confirmed by research (regulatory consensus) [L3:B3/C3/D] |
| Liver disease | no amount of alcohol is advised; fluid/protein and other restrictions **follow the doctor's instructions** | medical boundary [L3:D1+临床个体化] |
| People on medication | alcohol interacts with sedatives/painkillers and other drugs; caffeine individualized for those sensitive to blood-pressure/arrhythmia drugs — **follow the doctor's instructions** | medical boundary [L3:B3/D] |
| Iron deficiency/anemia | no strong tea with meals; drink 1 hour after | confirmed by research [L3:C3] |
| Endurance exercise | drink to thirst, not to a schedule; guard against exercise-associated hyponatremia | confirmed by research [L3:A2] |
| Older adults | blunted thirst sensation → offer water proactively on a schedule; watch urine color | fairly well established (exception population · FACT) [L3:A1] |
| Uncontrolled hypertension | sensitive to single caffeine doses; individualize | still being studied [L3:B3] |

**The medical boundary, stated explicitly**: this book's framing is popular science for the general population and does not replace diagnosis or treatment; for pregnancy/breastfeeding, liver disease, kidney disease, and people on medication, beverage choices and total amounts **defer to the treating physician**.

## Chapter 9. FAQ (11 questions)

1. **Do I really need 8 cups of water a day?** Don't count. The authoritative framing is total water intake (food water about 20% included); drink when thirsty + pale-yellow urine means enough. [L3:A1]
2. **A cup of water on an empty stomach in the morning to "detox"?** In people who drink normally, "detox" has no evidence; water on an empty stomach is harmless but not necessary. [L3:A1]
3. **Mineral water, purified water, or boiled tap water — which is better?** Urban tap water, boiled, is enough (microbiologically safe + new plastic-reducing evidence); water filters have no trial evidence for health hard outcomes, and cartridges must be changed diligently. [L3:A3]
4. **How many cups of coffee a day is right?** The 3-4-cup range carries the most benefit evidence; beyond 6 cups it turns (still being studied · observational); the definition of "one cup" is confounded and unresolved — not known. [L3:B1·G]
5. **Does coffee raise cholesterol?** Only unfiltered methods (French press / Turkish) raise LDL via diterpenes; paper-filtered drip is lowest; heavy unfiltered consumers are advised to get blood lipids checked. [L3:B5]
6. **Does tea prevent cancer or cause it?** Tea itself shows no overall association with cancer (prevention does not hold); the real risk is scalding drinks >65°C (2A · harms the esophagus) — let it cool 3-5 minutes before drinking. [L3:C1/C2]
7. **Will tea with meals cause anemia?** Tea polyphenols inhibit plant-iron absorption, and strong tea with meals is an iron-deficiency risk factor; leaving 1 hour after the meal resolves it; iron-deficient and pregnant people take no strong tea at mealtimes. [L3:C3]
8. **Does a little red wine protect the heart?** "The safest level of drinking = zero" is the verdict; the J curve's main artifact is sick-quitter confounding; as health advice it has been overturned. [L3:D1]
9. **I flush but can hold my liquor — can I train it up?** No. Flushing = ALDH2 gene deficiency; it cannot be trained; flushers who keep drinking multiply their esophageal cancer risk; antihistamines that mask the flush do not remove acetaldehyde and are forbidden as an "I can drink" solution. [L3:D2]
10. **Can sugar-free drinks replace water?** Better than sugared ones, but not equal to healthy — WHO advises against using sweeteners to control weight; water is the default optimum. Microbiome effects still being studied. [L3:E2]
11. **Does aspartame cause cancer?** IARC assigns 2B (possibly carcinogenic · limited evidence), which does not mean it causes cancer; the acceptable daily intake of 40 mg/kg leaves a huge margin, and at everyday amounts the risk face is tiny. [L3:E2]

1. What does the authoritative "total water intake" include, and what share is roughly food? — **Drinking water + beverages + food water; about 20%**. [A1]
