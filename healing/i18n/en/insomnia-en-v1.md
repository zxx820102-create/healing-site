# Insomnia Health Canon v1

> **In one sentence**: Insomnia is the disorder of "wanting to sleep, having the time to sleep, yet not sleeping — and genuinely suffering the next day." It is the most common human sleep disorder (globally about 10% of adults meet the insomnia-disorder threshold; 20–30% have symptoms), and its first-line treatment is not a drug but a learnable craft: CBT-I. **Insomnia's most painful spot is the two-way squeeze: on one side, fearing sleeping-pill addiction and gritting it out; on the other, treating melatonin as candy — both ends need an honest ledger.**
>
> **How to read this volume**: ① diagnosis first (which kind of sleeplessness counts as disease) → ② CBT-I (the first-line craft with the strongest evidence) → ③ the sleeping-pill honest account (dependence is real · rational use is also real) → ④ melatonin's true position (when it works · when it is a waste of money) → ⑤ sleep hygiene's two faces (necessary but not sufficient) → ⑥ special populations (older adults / pregnancy / shift work / adolescents) → ⑦ the red-flag chapter and the judgment of six popular claims.

## ① Diagnosis face: which kind of "can't sleep" counts as disease

**Insomnia's three questions (FACT · guideline consensus)**. To judge insomnia, what a doctor actually asks is three things, memorable in plain words as "opportunity · ability · consequence":

- **Is there opportunity?** — Is there enough time and a suitable environment to sleep (staying up until two for work is not insomnia; that is not giving sleep a chance);
- **Is there ability?** — Given the opportunity, sleep still won't come: difficulty falling asleep (>30 minutes), difficulty maintaining (repeated night awakenings), early waking with no return to sleep;
- **Is it worth treating?** — Is there real daytime suffering: fatigue, broken attention, poor mood, work or driving affected.

Only when all three hit (especially the third) is it an insomnia disorder. **"Want to sleep, have time, can't sleep + daytime suffering" is the core**; sleeping little but feeling fine by day is not in this category.

**The iron rule of "everyone must sleep 8 hours" is a pseudo-concept (CONTRADICTED)**. Sleep need varies enormously between individuals: this is genetically determined — members of the discovered "natural short sleeper" gene families (DEC2/BHLHE41, ADRB1, NPSR1, GRM1) sleep only 4–6.5 hours their whole lives with fully intact daytime function (FACT · directly verified this round from the genetics: DEC2 mutation first reported in *Science* in 2009; by 2024 the review had collected all four genes; 2025 *iScience* reported the DEC2 mutation extends healthspan in roundworms). **Telling a born short sleeper they "must sleep 8 hours" = prescribing for a healthy person — what it manufactures is anxiety, not health.**

**Older people's sleep is fragmented by design (FACT)**. Ohayon 2004's all-ages meta of healthy people (journal *Sleep*, cited 2191 times): with age, total sleep time, sleep efficiency, and deep-sleep proportion naturally decline, and nighttime awakenings naturally increase. **An older person waking two or three times a night and sleeping lightly is, in most cases, normal aging — not disease.** The right question is "is the daytime suffering," not "is the night unbroken." Older adults do not need sleeping pills to "sleep the way they slept when young."

**The short-vs-chronic boundary (FACT · ICSD-3/DSM-5 framing)**: ≥3 nights per week, lasting ≥3 months = chronic insomnia disorder; within 3 months = short-term insomnia. This boundary decides the intervention path — short-term insomnia looks for the trigger first (stress, jet lag, a new drug, pain); chronic insomnia calls in the craft of CBT-I.

**"Sleeps little but fine by day" is not insomnia — don't manufacture anxiety (FACT)**. In the diagnostic criteria, "daytime impairment" is a necessary condition. Genuine natural short sleepers exist (proven at the gene level); forcibly diagnosing "I sleep 6 hours" as insomnia and pushing people toward sleeping pills is one of this field's most common harms.

**The China ledger (FACT·relayed)**: the prevalence of insomnia symptoms among Chinese adults is about 38.2% (Chinese Sleep Research Society framing, relayed consistently by multiple authoritative sources); clinically significant insomnia prevalence is about 15%; globally about 10% of adults meet the insomnia-disorder standard and about 20% more have occasional symptoms (Morin 2022 epidemiology review, cited 929). Women, older adults, and shift workers are the high-prevalence groups.

## ② The CBT-I first-line face: the strongest evidence is not a drug, it is a craft

**The guidelines' framing (FACT · directly verified this round)**: the American College of Physicians (ACP) 2016 guideline verbatim: "all adult patients with chronic insomnia disorder should receive cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the initial treatment"; the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) 2021 behavioral treatment guideline (Edinger et al., cited 1012) recommends its components — stimulus control, sleep restriction, etc. — item by item. Drugs are the second tier when CBT-I is unavailable/insufficient — this is the mainstream guidelines' ordering, not a wellness opinion.

**CBT-I's two cores, in plain words**:

- **Stimulus control (the bed is for sleep only)**: when you can't sleep (count about 20 minutes), get up, go do something boring in dim light, return to bed only when sleepy; no matter when you fell asleep, get up at a fixed time; no daytime napping to compensate. It treats the conditioned reflex of "bed = the arena of anxiety." **The negative textbook case is precisely the most common mistake: "if you can't sleep, lie there and force it" — directly falsified by this very intervention (CONTRADICTED)**; forcing sleep while lying down only trains the bed into a trigger for wakefulness and anxiety.
- **Sleep restriction (compress first, then extend)**: first compress total time in bed to near the actual time asleep, manufacturing mild sleep pressure; once sleep turns dense and solid, extend stepwise. **Counter-intuitive but the strongest evidence**: it deliberately makes you sleep even less in the short term — many people want to quit on the spot; but it is the craft that re-compacts fragmented sleep. The core mechanism is raising "sleep efficiency."

**The effect size (FACT · directly verified this round)**: Trauer 2015, *Annals of Internal Medicine* meta (20 RCTs, 1162 people): after treatment, sleep-onset latency shortened by about 19 minutes, time awake at night shortened by about 26 minutes, sleep efficiency rose about 9.9 percentage points; while total sleep time increased only about 7.6 minutes (not statistically significant). **These numbers themselves are what every person with insomnia deserves to be told honestly: CBT-I's victory is not "sleeping longer," it is "the worthless awake time shrinks sharply and sleep density rises"** — which is also why "I did CBT-I and my total hours barely moved" is not failure.

**The long-term benefit is what drugs cannot do (FACT)**: the same Trauer 2015 paper notes the improvements persist through follow-up; by contrast, in sleeping-pill controlled trials the benefit essentially vanishes after discontinuation, with rebound common. The deep logic of the ACP ordering sits here: **CBT-I teaches the body to sleep on its own; a pill is borrowed sleepiness — and it must be paid back.**

**Self-help and digital CBT-I: the accessibility tier, stated honestly (FACT)**: fully automated digital CBT-I (apps/web courses) produces moderate-to-large improvements in insomnia severity (2025 meta: ISI scores about 3.4 points below controls; Hasan 2022 network meta, same direction); legitimate self-help books and structured courses accessible to Chinese readers sit on the same effectiveness gradient, lower tier. **Stated honestly: digital/self-help CBT-I is legitimate, effective, and the most accessible entry point — but its effects are usually smaller than full face-to-face CBT-I; for severe cases, comorbid depression/anxiety, and older adults, professional channels come first.**

## ③ The sleeping-pill honest account: dependence is real, and so is rational use

**Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs (zolpidem etc.) — a hard ledger for older adults (FACT)**: the 2022 review's conclusion is explicit: for insomnia in older adults, **the risks of even short-term use already outweigh the benefits**; multiple metas on Z-drugs and fall-related injuries concur, with dose- and duration-dependence; a Korean cohort study (cited 142) shows zolpidem raises fracture risk in older adults. Fall → fracture → disability is the cost chain of "one sleeping pill" in an older person that is most easily underestimated.

**The FDA boxed warning (FACT · directly verified this round)**: on April 30, 2019, the FDA added a boxed warning to zolpidem, zaleplon, and eszopiclone: complex sleep behaviors (sleepwalking, sleep-driving, sleep-eating, etc.) can occur in a state of incomplete wakefulness, causing severe injury or death, at any dose; anyone with a prior occurrence is contraindicated.

**Half-life and the "hangover feeling" (FACT·relayed)**: sleeping pills divide into long, medium, and short half-lives. Short-acting drugs (zolpidem about 2.5 hours) market "fast sleep onset, little morning residue"; a long-acting benzodiazepine (such as flurazepam) has active metabolites that can linger for dozens of hours — this is the pharmacological ledger under an older person's "still groggy by day, falls the next day." **The "hangover feeling" is not mysticism — it is the drug not finished metabolizing.**

**The real probability of "once you start, it's for life" — dismantled in both directions (MODEL, empirics at both ends)**:

- **Dependence is real**: abrupt cessation in long-term users can cause rebound insomnia, anxiety, tremor — textbook-level physical dependence; "taking more and more" tolerance is also real.
- **But "short-term rational use inevitably addict" does not hold**: Morin 2004, *Am J Psychiatry* RCT (76 older chronic users averaging 19.3 years on medication): after a 10-week intervention, **the "gradual taper + CBT-I" arm had 85% fully off medication**, the taper-only arm 48%, the CBT-I-only arm 54%; combined, 63% achieved discontinuation within an average of 7 weeks. **Older people who had taken the pills for over a decade could mostly still stop safely — "once you start you're hooked for life" is a fatalism refuted by the data (CONTRADICTED).**
- **Safe tapering principles (always defer to your doctor)**: never stop abruptly; slow, graded, sometimes supervised tapering; combining CBT-I during the taper markedly raises success; the Beers criteria and the 2025 joint clinical-toxicology/geriatrics guideline both recommend most older adults taper off benzodiazepines.

**Orexin receptor antagonists (DORA) — a new tier with a different mechanism (FACT, evidence grade stated as-is)**: one mechanism of insomnia is a "wake switch" (the orexin system) stuck open; DORA operates in reverse — **switching off wakefulness instead of forcing sedation**, with theoretically lower addiction concern. The evidence ledger: as of the 2023 meta only 10 double-blind RCTs and 7086 people were poolable (Rocha 2023); efficacy is moderate and generally below CBT-I's long-term performance in comparator trials; the "revolutionary drug" narrative of the modafinil era does not hold. The honest positioning: **a new tier for the middle ground where "neither CBT-I nor traditional drugs fit" — not a wonder drug**; before use, narcolepsy must be excluded (contraindication), under a doctor's guidance.

**The off-label sleep aid face of antidepressants (MODEL · defer to your doctor)**: low-dose doxepin carries AASM 2017 endorsement (sleep maintenance); trazodone, mirtazapine and others are widely used off-label for sleep in practice, but the evidence grades are uneven (AASM grades trazodone as not recommended/insufficient evidence). **They are not the automatic answer of "a safer sleeping pill"** — they are another benefit-risk ledger (antidepressant co-benefits versus orthostatic hypotension, daytime residual effects, weight), accounted per person by a doctor.

## ④ The melatonin true-position face: two real use cases, one vast false market

**Ordinary chronic insomnia: the evidence does not support it (FACT · directly verified this round)**: the AASM 2017 pharmacotherapy guideline (Sateia et al., cited 2144) is explicit: **melatonin is not recommended for difficulty falling asleep or staying asleep in adults** (weak recommendation); the 2023 European insomnia guideline points the same way (melatonin and herbal preparations not recommended). "A melatonin before bed to cure insomnia" is the public default option that mainstream guidelines have rejected.

**Its two genuine territories**:

- **Jet lag (FACT · directly verified this round)**: the Cochrane 2002 systematic review (cited 114): melatonin (0.5–5 mg) is "significantly effective" for preventing/reducing jet lag, with acceptable short-course safety — a legitimate option for cross-time-zone travelers.
- **Circadian phase delay (FACT·relayed + MODEL)**: for late-type adolescents and people with a delayed circadian phase, the problem is not "can't sleep" but "the body clock sits too late"; low-dose melatonin used at **the correct time point** (usually a few hours before target bedtime, calibrated by melatonin's onset marker DLMO) can advance the phase. **Timing matters more than dose** — and this must be calibrated by a sleep specialist, not self-experimented.

**The market chaos of dose and purity (FACT · directly verified this round)**: Erland & Saxena 2017, *J Clin Sleep Med* (cited 396): commercial melatonin supplements were tested — actual content deviated from the label by −83% to +478%; 71% of products deviated more than ±10%; **26% also contained detectable serotonin** (serotonin contamination is useless and potentially risky). The physiological dose is of the 0.3 mg order; the market norm is 3–10 mg — multiples above physiology as the default. Supplements are regulated under different purity requirements than drugs — that is where "same brand, different batch, five-fold apart" comes from.

**The boundary of use in children (mostly UNKNOWN, boundary honest)**: evidence is insufficient on long-term exogenous melatonin's effects on the growth axis and sexual development in children and adolescents; authoritative pediatrics (such as the AAP clinical framing) holds "behavioral intervention first, short-term when necessary, under medical guidance." **Using melatonin gummies long-term as a "sleep-coaxing magic tool" for children is not supported by the evidence face; the boundary is the doctor.**

## ⑤ Sleep hygiene's two faces: necessary, but not sufficient

**The positive face (necessary)**: regular schedule, light management (morning light / avoiding blue light at night), a quiet, dark, cool bedroom — these are sleep's foundation, and every version of CBT-I includes them.

**The negative face (not sufficient — itself an honest conclusion confirmed by meta-analysis)**: Irish 2015, *Sleep Med Rev* review (cited 544): **sleep-hygiene education alone has small, unreliable effect sizes and is insufficient as an insomnia treatment.** "I already watch my sleep hygiene — why am I still insomniac?" — because the foundation is not the house; chronic insomnia needs the craft of CBT-I, and hygiene is only the base.

**The caffeine half-life arithmetic (FACT · directly verified + relayed)**: caffeine's half-life is about 5 hours (individual range 3–7 hours). Drake 2013, *J Clin Sleep Med* (cited 198) measured 400 mg caffeine taken 0, 3, and 6 hours before bed — **all significantly disrupted sleep**; even the 6-hours-before-bed group lost over 1 hour of subjective total sleep. In living-room language: **the day's last cup should sit around the "8 hours before bed" line** (an extrapolation from the verified finding that 6 hours still disturbs sleep, compounded by the half-life); a milk tea at 4 p.m. still has half its caffeine in the blood at 9 p.m. — a midnight bedtime lands right in the line of fire.

**The alcohol-as-sleep-aid trap (FACT · directly verified this round)**: Ebrahim 2013 meta (27 studies, cited 245): alcohol shortens sleep-onset time (the source of its "sleep aid" illusion), but **suppresses REM and fast-wave sleep in the first half of the night, then drives sympathetic activation, more awakenings, and REM rebound in the second half** — structure destroyed, sleep sliced into fragments. **Alcohol is not a sleeping pill; it is a loan shark that lends anesthesia first and collects interest after.** Interlink with the liver-metabolic face (alcohol's metabolic bill is counted elsewhere).

**The posture of "catching up on sleep" (MODEL · relayed, controversy stated honestly)**: weekend revenge catch-up sleep repays some of the weekday sleep debt, but multiple observational studies link "social jetlag" (weekend schedule drift) with worse metabolic markers — the catch-up is not for nothing, but large drift has a price; the two bodies of evidence coexist and no unilateral ruling is issued (CONTESTED). The steadier posture: **fixed wake time beats fixed bedtime; naps limited to 20–30 minutes, before 3 p.m.** — a nap too long or too late eats the night's sleep pressure.

## ⑥ Special populations face: same disease, different ledgers

- **Older adults**: altered sleep architecture is normal aging (Ohayon 2004 all-ages meta, see ①) — do not medicate against a young person's "unbroken night" standard; benzodiazepine/Z-drug fall-fracture boxed warnings apply (see ③); **CBT-I's evidence holds in older adults with no fall cost — its ordering should come even earlier**; an older person on many medications whose "daytime confusion" often owes a hidden debt to one sleeping pill.
- **Pregnancy (women's-canon interlink)**: insomnia is highly prevalent in pregnancy (hormones, fetal pressure, nocturnal awakenings), but **almost all sleeping pills face a tight benefit-risk ledger in pregnancy**: late-pregnancy benzodiazepine use is associated with neonatal floppy infant syndrome, and Z-drug data are similarly limited. Behavioral intervention (CBT-I) is one of pregnancy's few low-risk tiers; any medication use = double confirmation by obstetrics + sleep medicine.
- **Shift workers**: the core contradiction is a circadian clock demanded to run backwards; interventions center on rhythm (fixed rotation direction, timed light exposure, cautiously timed melatonin, napping strategy), with sleeping pills only short-term — long-term use amounts to stamping the body clock's formal appeal "read — no reply."
- **Adolescents**: physiological phase delay (the body clock naturally shifts later by about 1–2 hours) plus early school start times = systematic "social jetlag" — **a child's "can't sleep at night, can't get up in the morning" is to a large extent biology protesting the institution, not laziness (MODEL · multi-source consistent)**. Light management and schedule anchors come first; melatonin requires specialist calibration (see ④); long-term sleep debt and mood disorders fuel each other (mood-canon interlink).

## ⑦ Red-flag chapter: these signals do not wait (find the cause first; don't reach for sleeping pills first)

> The red flags' common logic: **insomnia is a symptom, not the starting point of a verdict. Screen for the treatable disease behind it first; talk sleep aids second.**

1. **Loud snoring + daytime sleepiness → screen for obstructive sleep apnea first; don't prescribe sleeping pills first (FACT·relayed)**: about 30–40% of people with insomnia may have comorbid OSA, and 30–50% of OSA patients have insomnia symptoms (NICE CKS synthesis); giving sleeping pills to a missed OSA patient = adding sedation to someone under-ventilating (resp-canon interlink: the STOP-BANG questionnaire, the CPAP face).
2. **Insomnia + low mood and loss of interest >2 weeks → screen for depression (FACT·relayed)**: insomnia is depression's most common residual symptom and prodromal signal, feeding in both directions; treating only the insomnia without checking mood is painting a leaking boat (mood-canon interlink: mood:red_flags, the safe-asking card). Thoughts that life is not worth living = the mood-clinic red-flag channel immediately.
3. **Injuring people in dreams, punching and kicking, falling out of bed → screen for RBD (FACT · directly verified this round)**: people with REM sleep behavior disorder act their dreams out (the muscle paralysis that should accompany REM fails); the Montreal prospective cohort (Postuma 2015, *Neurology*, cited 354): **30% convert within 3 years, 66% within 7.5 years, to Parkinson disease / Lewy body dementia and other synucleinopathies** — this is a prodromal signal of neurodegenerative disease, and what it needs is a neurology follow-up plan, not sleeping pills (neurology interlink; cross-graph anchors pd:rbd, pd:prodrome).
4. **Restless legs at night, unbearable unless moved, relieved by moving → screen for restless legs syndrome (FACT·relayed)**: RLS is a treatable independent disease (related to iron stores, with its own drug pathway), often mislabeled "insomnia" and given sleeping pills — the whole direction wrong.
5. **Snoring with witnessed apneas, morning headaches, nocturia**: same OSA red flags (resp-canon interlink).
6. **Insomnia after a new drug or new illness**: steroids, some antihypertensives, antidepressants, hormonal drugs and more can disturb sleep — review the medication list first; don't rush to add a drug.

### Falsification face: the judgment of six popular claims (counter-example history is never deleted)

| # | Popular claim | Verdict | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "If you can't sleep, lie there and force it — lying down counts as rest" | **CONTRADICTED** | Stimulus control's positive form is exactly this: forcing sleep while lying down trains the bed into a wakefulness/anxiety trigger; get up when you can't sleep (FACT · Edinger 2021) |
| 2 | "A drink helps me sleep" | **CONTRADICTED** | Ebrahim 2013 meta: the onset illusion + first-half REM suppression + second-half rebound fragmentation; alcohol wrecks sleep architecture (FACT) |
| 3 | "Melatonin for everyone, one before bed" | **CONTRADICTED (outside its true use cases)** | AASM 2017 does not recommend it for ordinary insomnia; jet lag/circadian delay are the real indications; the −83% to +478% supplement purity chaos (FACT) |
| 4 | "You must sleep 8 hours or the body suffers" | **CONTRADICTED** | Natural short-sleeper genes proven; fragmented sleep normal in older adults; diagnosis looks at daytime function, not the clock (FACT) |
| 5 | "Sleeping pills addict you the moment you take them — you can never quit" | **CONTRADICTED (as fatalism) · the dependence face is real** | Both-directions account: physical dependence/rebound genuinely exist; yet older users averaging 19.3 years on pills had 85% successfully stop via taper + CBT-I (Morin 2004) — fatalism and credulity are both wrong |
| 6 | "Poor sleep = the body failed its detox, the brain is piling up garbage" | **CONTRADICTED (as narrative) · replaced by the real function face** | The real evidence: glymphatic clearance during sleep is proven in mice (Xie 2013, *Science*: interstitial space expands during sleep, metabolite clearance strengthens) — but that is an **animal-model mechanism**, and translating it into "one sleepless night = the brain piles up poison" is science-communication distortion; sleep's real functional face (memory consolidation, metabolic homeostasis, emotion regulation) is far larger, and far more honest, than "detox" |
