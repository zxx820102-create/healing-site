# Pain Canon v1 (English) (pain-canon-v1-20260912)

---

> **In one sentence**: chronic pain is a disease in itself, not a symptom to be "endured away"; its mechanism is an alarm system whose sensitivity has been turned up, not a body that is constantly "being damaged." The whole point of modern pain medicine is turning the oversensitive alarm back down — mainly through activity, psychology, and sleep, with medication only as an adjunct; before a red flag, seek care immediately.

---

## ① The acute vs chronic watershed: at three months, pain changes its mode of existence

**[FACT · IASP/ICD-11 framing]** The IASP classification of chronic pain for ICD-11 defines chronic pain as pain **persisting or recurring for more than 3 months**; within it, "chronic primary pain" is formally listed as **a disease category in its own right** — occurring in body regions difficult to attribute to another cause, or conceptualized as a disease entity requiring treatment in itself (Nicholas, Treede et al., *Pain* 2019, PMID 30586068, verified via PubMed this round).

This implies a profound shift:

| | Acute pain | Chronic pain |
|---|---|---|
| Role | **A symptom** (an alarm · signaling tissue damage) | **A disease in itself** (the alarm system itself has malfunctioned) |
| Time scale | Days to weeks · fades with healing | ≥3 months · beyond the healing window |
| Core strategy | Treat the cause · immobilize and protect (as needed) | Treat the system: activity + psychology + sleep + education · drugs only as adjunct |
| "Thoroughly find the lesion" | Often necessary | **Often unnecessary and often unrewarding** (see §the imaging-pain separation) |

**[FACT · prevalence magnitude]** UK systematic review (Fayaz et al., *BMJ Open* 2016, PMID 27324708 · abstract original text verified): pooled chronic pain prevalence **43.5%** (95%CI 38.4–48.6); moderate-to-severely disabling chronic pain 10.4%–14.3%; 14.3% in the 18–25 age group, rising to 62% at 75+. Global face: analyses based on GBD data show chronic pain prevalence and disability burden (YLDs) rising continuously since 1990, higher in women than men, with low back pain and migraine dominating YLDs (*Br J Pain* 2025, PMID 39726775; the low-back-pain special report *Spine* 2025, PMID 40667679, both verified this round). **"About 20% of adults" is the cross-survey magnitude consensus [MODEL]** — individual countries fluctuate between 10% and 40% depending on framing, but "at least one in five people" is the conservative lower bound.

**[MODEL · central sensitization]** The core mechanistic hypothesis of chronic pain is **central sensitization**: elevated excitability of pain pathways within and above the spinal cord, with weakened inhibitory control, so the central nervous system amplifies its response to normal, even harmless, input. The representative exposition is Woolf (*Pain* 2011, PMID 20961685, verified this round · cited 3000+). The mechanistic evidence is solid at the level of animal models and repeatable human phenomena, but **for the individual patient, "I hurt because of central sensitization" remains a model-grade explanation, not a directly biopsied fact** — hence this canon labels it MODEL throughout, and promotion to FACT is banned.

## ② The biopsychosocial model: explaining "alarm sensitivity" in human words

**[FACT · standing of the framework]** The biopsychosocial orientation is the mainstream framework of chronic pain research and practice (Gatchel et al., *Psychological Bulletin* 2007, PMID 17592957, verified this round).

**The plain-language rendering (can be spoken directly to all 8 billion)**:

> Your nervous system carries a **smoke alarm**. Acute pain is "the kitchen really is on fire" — the alarm reports the fire, as it should. Chronic pain is **the alarm dialled to hypersensitivity**: the little heat from a toaster, a draft in the corridor, and it pulls the whole building's alarm. The pain is really hurting — the alarm is ringing, the experience is real; but **hurting does not mean the body is being burned down**.
> Three knobs jointly set the sensitivity:
> - **The body knob**: long inactivity → muscles and joints "rust," the alarm grows more sensitive; regular low-intensity activity → sensitivity falls back.
> - **The psychological knob**: fear of pain ("if I move, it's over") labels every alarm "catastrophe," and the alarm gets louder with practice (the fear-avoidance model, below).
> - **The social/environmental knob**: isolation, insomnia, financial stress, having no one believe "the pain you can't see" — all feed power to the alarm.
> **Treatment is turning all three knobs back together** — which is why the best prescription is often not a drug.

**[MODEL · fear-avoidance]** The fear-avoidance model explains the psychological pathway to chronicity: catastrophizing pain → avoiding activity → functional decline → worse pain → more fear. Clinical reviews of the model: *Clinical Journal of Pain* 2012 (PMID 22673479) and *Pain* 2016 (PMID 27428892), both verified this round. The model's predictions are supported by large volumes of observational research, but it belongs at the model layer, not the law layer.

## ③ The non-drug ladder evidence face: strongest evidence first; where it is incomplete, mark it incomplete

> Ladder-ordering principle: evidence strength × safety × accessibility. Non-drug-first is not sentiment — it is the fact of the table below.

| Intervention | Evidence tier | One-line evidence | Anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Exercise therapy** (chronic low back pain) | **FACT (Cochrane 2021 systematic review · 249 trials)** | Moderate certainty: exercise **may be effective** for pain; small effect size on functional limitation (below the pre-registered minimal clinically important difference threshold) — i.e., "there is benefit, but don't deify it" | PMID 34580864 |
| **Staying active vs bed rest** (acute low back pain/sciatica) | **FACT (Cochrane 2010)** | Acute low back pain: advice to stay active has a small advantage over advice to rest on small pain (SMD 0.22) and function (SMD 0.29); sciatica: no difference or minimal difference between the two. **Bed rest has no evidence advantage whatsoever** | PMID 20556780 |
| **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** (chronic pain) | **FACT (Cochrane 2020 · 59 studies >5000 people)** | CBT has **small-to-very-small** benefits on pain, disability, and emotional distress (most outcomes at moderate certainty); insufficient evidence on adverse events. ACT listed separately: large effect sizes but evidence of very low quality — **cannot be treated as proven** | PMID 32794606 |
| **Mindfulness meditation** (MBSR/MBI) | **FACT (Ann Behav Med 2017 meta)** | Mindfulness meditation has supporting evidence for improvement in pain and depressive symptoms in chronic pain; effect sizes in the small-to-moderate range; long-term follow-up data limited | PMID 27658913 |
| **Physical therapy/structured exercise programs** | **MODEL (directional)** | The systematic reviews underlying the WHO clinical guideline support the benefit-risk face of structured exercise (*J Occup Rehabil* 2023, PMID 37991647 · existence and scope verified this round); differences among specific manual techniques not verified one by one | PMID 37991647 |
| **Pain education** (neuroscience education about pain itself) | **MODEL** | Widely adopted by guidelines as a public component of the interventions above; its independent effect size not separately verified this round — **UNKNOWN** | — |

**Honest summary**: none of the non-drug modalities is a "special-effect drug"; their common character is **real but gentle effects, almost no serious adverse events, and a change to the system (sensitivity) rather than to the symptom numbers**. This is precisely why, when the drug face's (§④) effect sizes are equally gentle, guidelines still place non-drug approaches first: safety and system gain.

## ④ The drug face, an honest ledger: real effect sizes, neither prettified nor demonized

**Paracetamol (acetaminophen)**
- [FACT] Acute low back pain: the PACE trial (*Lancet* 2014, PMID 25064594) — regular or as-needed paracetamol versus placebo **did not shorten time to recovery**; the adherence analysis confirmed it equally ineffective among adherers (*Pain* 2019, PMID 31453982).
- [FACT] Systematic review (*BMJ* 2015, PMID 25828856): low back pain — pain WMD **−0.5 points (0–100 scale, ineffective)**; hip/knee osteoarthritis — −3.7 points, **significant but below the clinically important threshold**; detection of abnormal liver function about 4-fold higher among users (RR 3.8). Cochrane in the same direction (PMID 27271789 low back pain; PMID 30801133 hip/knee OA).
- Plain words: **for low back pain it is essentially a placebo; for osteoarthritis it brings only weak short-term relief.** Still, it remains one of the safer options when there is no alternative — short-term use at label doses, never exceeding them (hepatotoxicity).

**NSAIDs (ibuprofen, diclofenac, etc.)**
- [FACT] Spinal pain systematic review (*Ann Rheum Dis* 2017, PMID 28153830): NSAIDs beat placebo, but **the effect size does not reach the clinically important threshold**; NNT=6 (95%CI 4–10) — on average 6 people must take the drug for 1 to gain a clinically important pain reduction; gastrointestinal reaction risk rises to **2.5-fold** (95%CI 1.2–5.2); the original conclusion: "no simple analgesic currently provides clinically important effects over placebo for spinal pain."
- Plain words: **NSAIDs are "effective but weak"**; short-term as-needed use is reasonable; long-term use must run the gastrointestinal/kidney/cardiovascular arithmetic (that arithmetic varies by population and was not verified item-by-item this round — [MODEL]).

**Opioids (morphine, oxycodone, fentanyl, etc.)**
- [FACT · risk] Systematic review of overdose risk after prescription (*CMAJ* 2023, PMID 37871953 · 28 studies, 23,963,716 patients): **prior overdose OR 5.85**, **OR 2.57 per 90 MME dose increase**, **≥3 prescribers OR 4.68** (moderate-to-high certainty).
- [FACT · crisis face] The US official data report (NCHS Data Brief 457, 2022, PMID 36598401) records drug-overdose deaths climbing continuously across 2001–2021 — with opioids the main driver. **Specific annual death counts were not verified to abstract numbers this round (UNKNOWN); this canon vouches only for the qualitative "continuous climb + opioid-dominated."**
- [MODEL · efficacy face] In chronic noncancer pain, opioids' advantage over placebo is small and offset by the safety burden — this is the common account and the direction of several network metas; the Busse 2018 Cochrane original was not directly verified this round (**UNKNOWN · to be filled**), hence MODEL only.
- Plain words: **in chronic pain, opioids are "high risk, weak benefit"**: not as simple as "take it and you're hooked" (see §⑦ falsification 4), but risk multiplying with dose, with scattered prescribers, and with prior overdose is hard data.

**Gabapentinoids (gabapentin, pregabalin)**
- [FACT] Updated systematic review of misuse and abuse (*Drugs* 2021, PMID 33215352 · EPMC verified, cited 160): misuse/abuse shows a **rising trend and causes real harm**; high-risk populations (prior substance use disorder, etc.) require caution and monitoring.
- Positioning [MODEL]: mainly for neuropathic pain; for general chronic pain the benefit is limited — the positioning is "second line for specific indications," not "a safe universal painkiller."

**Tramadol**
- [FACT · Cochrane official page verified] The Cochrane review *Tramadol for osteoarthritis* (Toupin April et al., 2019, cochrane.org/cochranelibrary, searched and verified this round): in osteoarthritis, tramadol brings about **5%** more people to clinically important improvement than placebo (placebo 10% vs tramadol 15%), accompanied by more nausea, dizziness, and other adverse events. The exact PubMed PMID was not retrieved through this round's channel (**UNKNOWN**; the Cochrane official page is authoritative).
- Plain words: tramadol = weak-opioid mechanism + serotonergic mechanism — **not "a safe waystation"**: it carries the opioid dependence risk face and its own withdrawal and interaction faces.

## ⑤ The Chinese-medicine acupuncture evidence face: written as it is, neither promoted nor demoted

> This chapter's discipline: all traditional-theory content labeled [TCM·Traditional]; clinical evidence written only as the verified RCT/meta status quo; guideline changes specified by "which country, which year, recommended or not."

**The clinical-trial evidence status quo**
- [FACT] **The Vickers 2018 individual-patient-data meta-analysis** (*J Pain*, PMID 29198932 · abstract original text verified, 20,827 patients/39 trials): acupuncture for chronic musculoskeletal pain, osteoarthritis, and chronic headache is **superior to sham acupuncture (effect ≈0.2 SD) and to no-acupuncture controls (≈0.5 SD)**; the effect decays only about 15% at 1 year; the authors' conclusion: acupuncture is effective for chronic pain, **and its effect cannot be fully explained by placebo/expectation effects**. This is the flagship anchor of acupuncture's modern evidence.
- [FACT] **Knee osteoarthritis**: systematic review and updated meta (2012, PMID 22588814 · EPMC verified) reports statistically significant benefit of needling for knee OA pain and function; the separation between effect magnitude and the sham-needle contribution is in tension across trials (to be understood in combination with Vickers's 0.2 SD magnitude — [INFERENCE]).
- [FACT] **Low back pain**: note the contrast — the high-impact evidence lies **not on acupuncture's side** (see §④ "no simple analgesic reaches the clinical threshold" and §③'s exercise/CBT evidence), and guidelines give low back pain acupuncture a **negative recommendation** (below).

**Guideline current status (actually verified this round · 2026-09-12)**
- [FACT] **NICE NG193 (UK · 2021, chronic primary pain)**: **recommends** that **a course of acupuncture** may be offered — the same guideline explicitly recommends against any analgesic drug as long-term core treatment (nice.org.uk original pages searched and verified).
- [FACT] **NICE NG59 (UK · 2016, low back pain and sciatica)**: **"Do not offer acupuncture"** — at this round's verification the page still carried the 2016 framing, and no change to the acupuncture entry was retrieved (nice.org.uk original page).
- [FACT] **NICE NG226 (UK · 2022, osteoarthritis)**: **does not recommend acupuncture** (listed as not-recommended alongside dry needling and electrotherapy; nice.org.uk and contemporaneous commentary verified).
- **Interpretation [INFERENCE]**: the same national guideline system's split — "recommended for chronic primary pain, not recommended for low back pain and osteoarthritis" — reflects **differences in the evidence bodies of each indication**, not institutional attitude; citations must carry the indication, and indication-less generalizations like "NICE endorses acupuncture" or "NICE rejects acupuncture" are banned.

**[TCM·Traditional] Meridian theory**: the meridians–acupoints–promoting qi flow and blood circulation framework is a two-thousand-year practice and theory system, carrying considerable clinical experience and researchable hypotheses (point specificity, needling parameters, etc.). But **"meridians" as anatomical-physiological entities remain an open scientific question (UNKNOWN)**; the Vickers analysis also noted that acupuncture's effect **does not necessarily depend on precise point location**. Respecting tradition ≠ treating tradition as mechanistic proof; acknowledging evidence ≠ treating evidence as tradition's validation. Both sentences stand; merging either one into the other is counterfeit.

## ⑥ The red-flag chapter: these pains are not "endure it" questions — they are emergency questions

> **Hard boundary (non-negotiable)**: if any one of the following appears, the first action is **immediate medical care (mostly the emergency department)**. This canon does not provide, and does not permit introducing in this context, any dietary remedy, supplement, or "regulate-and-observe" alternative. Red flags = first exclude lethal/disabling causes; only then does the chronic pain management path take its turn.

| Red flag | Concrete presentation | Why it is dangerous | Action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Thunderclap headache** | "The worst headache of one's life" peaking within seconds to a minute | Subarachnoid hemorrhage, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome, arterial dissection, etc. (review: *Continuum* 2015, PMID 26252591 · verified this round) | **Emergency department** (CT/lumbar puncture path decided by the physician) |
| **The cauda equina syndrome five** | ① Saddle numbness (the "riding" sensation gone) ② new fecal/urinary incontinence or retention ③ bilateral lower-limb weakness ④ bilateral sciatica-like pain ⑤ sudden sexual dysfunction | Cauda equina compression · the golden decompression window is short (review of the UK's new 2024/25 national diagnostic guideline: *Br J Gen Pract* 2025, PMID 40744753 · verified this round) | **Emergency MRI + surgical assessment** |
| **Nighttime rest pain + unexplained wasting** | Night pain unrelieved by lying flat, unrelated to activity, with unexplained weight loss within 6 months | Tumor (primary/metastatic), infection (osteomyelitis), etc. | **Be seen promptly** (within days; neither emergency-room level nor watch-and-wait) |
| **Cancer history + new pain** | Anyone with any cancer history developing **new-site** progressive pain | Possible bone metastasis/recurrence | **Be seen promptly** · proactively state the cancer history |
| **Band-like thoracic-level pain** | Band-like, level pain encircling the chest/abdomen, with limb numbness, weakness, unsteady gait, or bowel/bladder change | Thoracic spinal cord compression by a thoracic mass/inflammation (the thoracic cord has the least buffer space) | **Emergency department/urgent neurology consultation** |
| **Severe post-trauma pain** | Severe pain appearing or worsening after a fall, car crash, or sports injury (especially with osteoporosis or long-term steroid use) | Fracture (can be a compression fracture without obvious deformity) | **Emergency imaging** |
| **Pain with systemic signs** | Pain + fever and chills; immunosuppression/injection-drug-use history; spinal pain in someone with newly worsening diabetes control | Infectious (spondylodiscitis, abscess) | **Emergency department** |

**Words for the companion**: red-flag assessment is always "better a false alarm." In the chronic pain management system there is no "endure three more days and see" option — once red-flag words appear (thunderclap, saddle, wasting, band-like, trauma), enduring is not strength; it is delay.

## ⑦ The falsification face: the right-and-wrong ledger of six popular beliefs

**1. "Enduring pain is strength" → [CONTESTED · evidence against]**
Catastrophizing pain, suppressing its expression, and over-avoiding activity are one of the psychological pathways to chronicity (the fear-avoidance model · [MODEL] PMID 22673479/27428892); long-term endurance also delays red-flag recognition and proper treatment. Strength = **managing pain by the evidence** (activity, sleep, psychology, seeking care on schedule), not enduring. All endurance gives the system is sensitivity training for the alarm.

**2. "Back pain requires bed rest" → [CONTESTED · evidence against]**
Cochrane evidence ([FACT] PMID 20556780): for acute low back pain, advice to stay active is modestly superior to bed-rest advice; for sciatica there is no difference. The core treatment for chronic low back pain is exercise ([FACT] PMID 34580864). Bed rest's only "gain" is training acute pain into chronic pain. Exceptions: §⑥ red flags and immobilization explicitly required by a physician.

**3. "Pain level = degree of tissue damage" → [CONTESTED · evidence against]**
Imaging systematic review of spinal degeneration in asymptomatic people ([FACT] Brinjikji et al. *AJNR* 2015, PMID 25430861 · EPMC verified, cited 750+): disc degeneration, bulges, and other imaging findings grow steeply with age — **and these people do not hurt**. Conversely, people with chronic pain may show no progressive tissue destruction. Pain is an output of the nervous system, not a seismograph of tissue damage. This is exactly the anatomical basis of the "alarm sensitivity" metaphor.

**4. "Once you take painkillers you can never stop" → [CONTESTED · exaggeration and truth coexist; they must be told apart]**
- **The exaggerated side**: paracetamol and NSAIDs have **no addictive potential** (their problems are liver and gastrointestinal — see §④ [FACT]); between short-term post-operative opioid analgesia as prescribed and developing a use disorder there is likewise no inevitable channel.
- **The true side**: long-term opioid use does carry dependence and withdrawal physiology (tolerance/withdrawal is pharmacological fact); gabapentinoid misuse is on a rising trend ([FACT] PMID 33215352); prescription-opioid overdose risk multiplies with dose and prescriber count ([FACT] PMID 37871953).
- The correct posture: take drugs **by indication, by dose, by course** and return for follow-up — neither "grit through the pain" nor "making meals of the pills." Those who conflate the two are, half of them, frightening patients, and half, feeding the crisis.

**5. "Supplements open the meridians and cure pain at the root" → [CONTESTED · no evidence + a logic red flag]**
- The promise of "curing chronic pain at the root" conflicts with the §① disease model: chronic pain is a system state; there is no switch of "unblock it and it's over" [MODEL].
- The meridian framework's positioning per §⑤ is [TCM·Traditional] + scientific status UNKNOWN; "opening the meridians" as a supplement's mechanism claim has **no clinical evidence body** (UNKNOWN · no verified supporting anchor; prefer an empty cell to an invented one).
- The real risk ledger: delayed red-flag recognition (§⑥), drug–supplement interactions, liver/kidney burden, money. Whoever promises "cure at the root" is, by §⑥'s red-flag logic, the first danger signal — **a red flag for the wallet**.

**6. (Appendix) "Nothing found on tests = you're faking / it's psychological" → [CONTESTED]**
The combination of normal imaging with real severe pain is common in chronic pain and mechanistically explainable (central sensitization [MODEL] + the imaging-symptom separation [FACT] PMID 25430861). "Nothing found" means only "no peripheral lesion found," not "no disease" — chronic primary pain is a disease in ICD-11 ([FACT] PMID 30586068). Telling a patient "it's all in your head" is both a scientific error and a social injury that turns the alarm knob one notch higher.

## Falsifier (this canon's own falsifiable clauses)

- If high-quality evidence shows opioids' efficacy in chronic noncancer pain reaches or exceeds the clinically important threshold with acceptable safety → §④'s opioid section downgraded and rewritten.
- If the Vickers IPDMA is overturned by a replication analysis of equal scale (acupuncture ≈ sham ≈ 0) → §⑤'s flagship anchor downgraded, the NICE NG193 interpretation rewritten.
- If central sensitization is falsified as the main chronicization mechanism → the model-grade statements of §①②③ all downgraded in consequence.
- If NICE revises the NG59/NG226 acupuncture entries → §⑤'s guideline section updated to the new version with the old framing's history retained.
- If exercise/CBT effect sizes reach the clinically important threshold in a new Cochrane update → §③'s "gentle benefit" wording upgraded.
