# The Move Textbook v1 — Dose, the Four Elements, Target Effects, Timing, Side Effects, Individualization

> **Readers and how to use this book**: self-learners and health workers. **How to use it**: every chapter has learning objectives and a summary; the section-end [L3:id] notes lead back to the evidence-layer original (assertion + source + falsifier). This textbook never presents observational evidence as causal, and never presents a single trial as settled — the epistemic tiers stay word-for-word aligned with the L3 canon, zero promotion.

**Safety floor (before any prescription)**: chest pain, pressure, abnormal breathlessness, or fainting during exercise → stop, and move again only after a medical evaluation; soy-sauce-colored urine plus severe muscle pain → seek care immediately; post-injury rehabilitation prescriptions belong to the professional domain — this textbook does not cover them.

## Chapter 0. One-page action version (L1 · zero background required)

> The fuller, supplemented version is the L1 companion `docs/healing/textbook/move-l1-80yi-20260912.md`; this chapter is a condensed transcription of it — same source, same numbers, not word-identical. Key points below.

**Master line**: "from 0 to something" is worth more than "from something to optimal" — ten-odd minutes of brisk walking a day already pays out big; training a lot doesn't shorten life, but it isn't required either.

**Seven action cards**: ① bank 150 minutes of brisk walking a week (able to talk, unable to sing); if that falls short, 30–75 minutes of jogging spread across the week (confirmed by research) ② steps: 8000–10000 a day under 60, 6000–8000 over 60 — "10,000 steps" is a marketing number (confirmed by research) ③ two strength sessions a week training the large muscle groups; for middle-aged and older adults, adequate and regular matters even more (confirmed by research · guideline-level) ④ for mild-to-moderate depression, exercise delivers improvement on the scale of medication; for severe depression or any thoughts of self-harm, seek medical care first (confirmed by research · evidence quality discounted) ⑤ older adults train balance 2–3 times a week (confirmed by research · on some indicators) ⑥ for bone, walking and swimming are not enough — supervised strength plus jump training is what actually banks the deposit (confirmed by research · single trial) ⑦ no ice bath straight after training; use it only when there's a competition next week (confirmed by research · single trial).

**Myth quick-check (six items)**: running wrecks knees (reversed: 3.5% vs 10.2%) ｜ more exercise shortens life (no reliable evidence) ｜ 10,000 steps (plateau at 6000–10000) ｜ the fat-burning zone is optimal (Zone2 falsified) ｜ fasted training slims you faster (no difference) ｜ the 30-minute protein window (total intake is the guiding line).

**Three things nobody knows**: whether training grip strength lowers death; consumer genetic tests predicting training response; the exact day-by-day decay after detraining.

## Chapter 1. Concepts and terms

**Learning objectives**: be able to define intensity, dose-response, and the four elements; read the evidence-grading language and the safety red lines.

### 1.1 How intensity is graded

Let perception do the talking: **moderate intensity** = able to talk, unable to sing (brisk walking); **vigorous intensity** = speech comes in broken phrases (jogging, stair climbing). 150–300 minutes a week of moderate, or 75–150 minutes of vigorous — executing by the perception tiers is enough [L3:A1].

### 1.2 Dose-response and the minimum effective dose

**Dose-response** = the quantitative relationship between activity volume and outcomes. Measured by accelerometer (a wearable device that objectively records movement), total activity volume shows an inverse dose-response with all-cause mortality (the total death rate regardless of cause), while sedentary time is independently harmful (subjective questionnaires underestimate it and confound heavily) [L3:A1]. At the other end, the **minimum effective dose** sits far below the guideline floor: 30–75 minutes of running a week across 2–5 sessions, or steps raised to about 5800/day, already captures most of the relative benefit (quantified: fairly well established) [L3:A1/A2].

### 1.3 The four elements

Aerobic (heart and lungs), strength (muscle), flexibility and balance (fall prevention), NEAT (daily non-exercise activity, see 3.4) [L3:B].

### 1.4 Evidence-grading language (Table 1-1)

| L3 epistemic token | Plain-language tier | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| FACT | confirmed by research | Shown by randomized controlled trials or systematic reviews, with abstracts verified word by word |
| MODEL | fairly well established | Mechanism clear / single trials / guideline-consensus faces hold |
| INFERENCE | still being studied | Observational associations consistent in direction; causality unproven |
| CONTESTED | still contested in the field | High-quality evidence in mutual conflict |
| UNKNOWN | not known | Honestly not known; don't trust any assertion |

### 1.5 Safety red lines (before any action)

① Chest pain/pressure during exercise, exercise-induced syncope, abnormal breathlessness, family history of sudden death → **get a medical evaluation before scaling up** (red-line triggers, not a check-up-package recommendation) [L3:E5]; ② soy-sauce-colored urine plus severe muscle pain → seek care immediately [L3:E2]; ③ people with diagnosed cardiovascular disease work within the cardiac-rehabilitation framework [L3:F2].

### Glossary (in order of appearance)

1. **Moderate intensity**: the exercise tier where you can talk but cannot sing (brisk walking).
2. **Vigorous intensity**: the exercise tier where speech comes in broken phrases (jogging, stair climbing).
3. **Dose-response**: the quantitative curve between activity volume and health outcomes.
4. **Accelerometer**: a wearable that objectively records body movement; more accurate than questionnaires.
5. **All-cause mortality**: total death rate regardless of cause; the hardest endpoint in research.
6. **Hazard ratio (HR)**: the ratio of event risk between two groups; <1 means lower.
7. **Resistance training**: strength training against a load (iron, elastic bands, body weight).
8. **Progressive overload**: the training principle of small, stepwise load increases; consensus-level.
9. **NEAT**: non-exercise activity thermogenesis — daily non-exercise movement (walking commutes, chores, standing).
10. **Mitochondria**: the cell's energy factories.
11. **Effect size (g/d)**: standardized magnitude of effect; 0.2 small · 0.5 medium · 0.8 large.
12. **Network meta-analysis**: statistical pooling that ranks many treatments against one another at the same time.
13. **Acceptability**: whether participants are willing to keep doing it.
14. **Number needed to treat (NNT)**: how many people need the intervention, on average, to avoid 1 additional event.
15. **Bone mineral density (BMD)**: the mineral content of bone; the core osteoporosis indicator.
16. **Slow-wave sleep**: the deep-sleep stage with large, slow brain waves.
17. **Atrial fibrillation**: a rhythm disorder with disordered atrial firing; risk rises in extreme-endurance practitioners.
18. **Rhabdomyolysis**: an emergency in which muscle is severely destroyed and muscle contents spill into the blood.
19. **Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM)**: the most common cause of sudden death in young athletes.
20. **VO2max (maximal oxygen uptake)**: the gold-standard metric of cardiorespiratory fitness.

**Summary**: intensity is measured with the talk test; dose has a curve; each of the four elements keeps its own ledger. Before accepting a conclusion, check its tier; red lines come before prescriptions.

## Chapter 2. Dose: how much is enough, how much is too much

**Learning objectives**: answer "how little movement still helps", "is 10,000 steps really required", and "does training a lot shorten life".

### 2.1 Running: tiny doses, big returns; no further gains past the threshold

Runners' all-cause mortality HR 0.73 (14 studies, 230,000 people); but meta-regression shows **no significant dose-response trend** for frequency, duration, pace, or total volume — past some small threshold, running more is not significantly better, and no worse was seen either [L3:A2]. 55,000 people followed for 15 years: runners had 30% fewer deaths and 45% less cardiovascular death, and 3 more years of life expectancy; **jogging only 5–10 minutes a day, slower than 10 km/h**, was already associated with significant benefit [L3:A2].

### 2.2 Steps: the plateau and the "10,000 steps" myth

47,000 people across 15 cohorts: versus the lowest group (3553 steps/day), 5801 steps HR 0.60, 7842 steps 0.55, 10901 steps 0.47; the **plateau** is clear — 6000–8000 steps/day at 60 and above, 8000–10000 below, and beyond that risk no longer falls clearly. "10,000 steps a day" is a marketing number, not an evidence number [L3:A2].

### 2.3 The overdoing claim, falsified: "too much exercise shortens life" does not hold

For the general population, near the guideline ceiling and at higher volumes, **no reliable evidence of an all-cause mortality uptick has been observed** — "overdoing it shortens life" does not hold for total mortality [L3:A3]. The real costs sit in two smaller ledgers: musculoskeletal injury (see 6.4) and the cardiac local phenotype of extreme endurance — atrial fibrillation risk OR 2.46 in athletes (vs non-athletes · confirmed); higher coronary calcification in older extreme-endurance practitioners (still being studied); all-cause mortality not elevated in elite endurance athletes (still being studied) [L3:A3]. **Guarding against extreme claims**: "marathons hurt the heart" and "any amount is safe" are both over-simplifications — the correct statement = the overall curve is flat with no uptick + the cardiac subgroup shows measurable changes at extreme doses + the clinical significance varies from person to person [L3:A3].

### 2.4 Sitting is a separate ledger

Sedentary time is independently and positively associated with all-cause mortality; break up sitting every 30–60 minutes [L3:A1/B4].

**Table 2-1 Minimum effective dose quick-check**

| Item | A useful amount | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| Brisk walking / moderate intensity | 150 min/week (guideline); benefits already present below it | confirmed by research |
| Jogging | start at 5–10 min/day; 30–75 min/week in total captures the better part | confirmed by research |
| Steps | 6000–8000 (age ≥60) / 8000–10000 (under 60) | confirmed by research |
| Strength | ≥2 sessions/week on the large muscle groups | confirmed (guideline-level) |
| Balance (older adults) | 2–3 balance-challenge sessions a week | confirmed (on some indicators) |

**Summary**: from 0 to something > from something to optimal; overdoing it doesn't shorten life, but injury and the extreme-endurance cardiac ledger are recorded separately.

## Chapter 3. What each of the four elements does

**Learning objectives**: write a prescription for each of the four elements; see through "Zone2 is optimal"; know how to read the grip-strength data.

### 3.1 Aerobic: Zone2 falsified

The claim that "Zone2 (low intensity below the lactate threshold) is the optimal intensity for raising mitochondria and fat oxidation and preventing chronic disease" **does not hold** — a 2025 review is explicit: the evidence does not support Zone2 optimality; for the general population the evidence instead supports higher intensity as more critical for mitochondrial capacity and cardiometabolic outcomes; the Zone2 concept grew out of extrapolating elite high-volume observational data beyond its tier [L3:B1]. Its real value = **a sustainable high-volume base** (low fatigue, high adherence · can be stacked with vigorous work); mixed modes (mostly moderate-low + a little vigorous) agree with every major guideline; the Zone definitions themselves are not unified across experts — pinpointing your zone from a wristband's lactate ranges is false precision [L3:B1].

### 3.2 Strength: grip strength is a gauge of death risk, not a target

High vs low grip strength: all-cause mortality HR 0.69 (38 studies, 1.9 million people); the 17-country, 140,000-person PURE study: **for every 5 kg grip strength falls, all-cause mortality HR 1.16**, with cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, and stroke moving the same way, consistent across countries [L3:B2]. The reading must be kept straight: this is **association strength** (confirmed); "training grip strength itself lowers death" is **not known** — grip is a **stratifier** of risk (a gauge), not a proven **target** (a knob) [L3:B2]. The operations face: consensus = function (gait speed, sit-to-stand) and strength predict outcomes better than muscle quantity alone; the resistance prescription = large muscle groups, ≥2 sessions a week, progressive overload; protein's lean-mass benefit decays with age and works better in those with a training base [L3:B2].

### 3.3 Flexibility and balance: the older adult's fall defense

Tai chi, 22 randomized trials pooled: significant improvement in balance (balance-scale scores, single-leg stance with eyes closed, the Timed Up-and-Go test) and physical function, and reduced fear of falling; fall incidence "trends downward but is not significant on some indicators" — **balance-function improvement = confirmed; fewer falls = confirmed (weak); the hard fracture endpoint = not known** [L3:B3]. Flexibility itself has no mortality evidence; mobility serves function; those at high fall risk avoid stand-alone high-impact work [L3:B3].

### 3.4 NEAT: the underestimated bulk

Daily non-exercise activity (walking commutes, chores, standing, breaking up sitting) is one of the largest variable components of the between-person difference in daily energy expenditure, on the order of several hundred kcal/day (large individual variation; the figure was not verified word-for-word); "standing desks significantly reduce weight" has weak evidence — the direction holds, the magnitude is small [L3:B4].

**Summary**: aerobic keeps the volume (no need to fear higher intensity); strength keeps the muscle (read the grip table, don't worship it); balance keeps your life; daily activity keeps the total account.

## Chapter 4. How much effect exercise has on which targets

**Learning objectives**: for depression, anxiety, cognition, diabetes, and bone density, state the effect size, the evidence tier, and the boundary.

### 4.1 Depression: first-line-treatment scale, but the evidence quality must be stated honestly

A network meta-analysis of 218 randomized trials and 14170 people: walking/jogging effect size g -0.62, yoga -0.55, strength training -0.49, mixed aerobic -0.43, tai chi/qigong -0.42 (vs active controls); effects scale in proportion to intensity; strength training and yoga had the best acceptability [L3:C1]. The honest face, in the same frame: **only 1 study was at low risk of bias, and confidence is low for walking/jogging and very low for the rest** — the direction is credible, the precise values uncertain [L3:C1]. Head-to-head: 141 patients, 16 weeks — remission rate 43.3% for running vs 44.8% for the antidepressant escitalopram (no significant difference); mental outcomes on par; physical-health outcomes (weight, waist circumference, blood pressure, heart rate) better across the board for running; limitations = partial randomization + a patient-preference design [L3:C1]. Clinical placement: for mild-to-moderate depression it can stand **alongside psychotherapy/medication as a first choice, or be combined with them**; in severe depression or with suicide risk, exercise is not a substitute — seek care first [L3:C1].

### 4.2 Anxiety and psychological distress

An umbrella review (97 reviews, 128,000 people): median effect -0.43 for depression, -0.42 for anxiety, -0.60 for psychological distress (vs usual care); the honest face: 77 of the 97 reviews were of critically low quality — direction and magnitude credible, quality discounted [L3:C2].

### 4.3 Cognition and dementia: the association is significant, causality unproven

47 prospective cohorts: high vs low activity — cognitive decline RR 0.65, dementia RR 0.86; the association is significant but modest in size, and observational (causality still being studied) [L3:C3].

### 4.4 Diabetes prevention: a historic trial in which lifestyle won across the board

The DPP (3234 people with prediabetes): the lifestyle intervention (7% weight loss + ≥150 minutes of exercise a week) cut diabetes incidence **-58%**, metformin **-31%**; number needed to treat 6.9 vs 13.9 [L3:C4].

### 4.5 Bone density: only impact + heavy load makes an entry

The LIFTMOR trial (101 postmenopausal women with low bone mass, 8 months of **supervised** high-intensity resistance + impact): lumbar spine bone density +2.9% (controls -1.2%), femoral neck +0.3% (controls -1.9%) [L3:C5]. The reverse face: walking or swimming alone is essentially ineffective for spine and hip bone density (insufficient mechanical loading); anyone with a prior fracture or severe osteoporosis must have medical evaluation + supervision [L3:C5].

**Table 4-1 Target-effect quick-check**

| Target | Magnitude | Tier |
|---|---|---|
| Depression (walking/jogging) | g -0.62; remission rate on par with antidepressants | confirmed · quality discounted (low/very low) |
| Anxiety / psychological distress | -0.42 / -0.60 | confirmed (direction and magnitude) · quality discounted |
| Dementia | RR 0.86 (association) | association confirmed · causality still being studied |
| Diabetes prevention | incidence -58% (lifestyle arm) | confirmed by research |
| Bone density | lumbar spine +2.9% (8 months · supervised · single trial) | confirmed (single trial · supervised premise) |

**Summary**: depression is first-line in magnitude but must be read with the quality discount; diabetes prevention is the hardest-fought battle in the whole field; bone only respects real load.

## Chapter 5. Timing and nutrition interactions

**Learning objectives**: whether evening training wrecks sleep; whether fasted cardio is worth it; whether the protein window exists; whether to use ice baths.

### 5.1 Evening training × sleep: the total account is unharmed; vigorous work within 1 hour before bed is the real trap

Pooling 23 studies: **evening exercise does not harm sleep overall — it actually increases slow-wave sleep (+1.3 percentage points) and shortens light sleep** [L3:D1]. Moderator analysis: vigorous exercise finishing ≤1 hour before bed is associated with slower sleep onset, shorter total sleep, and a raised pre-sleep heart rate — but the effect vanishes when a single study is removed, and it is limited to healthy people: weak evidence [L3:D1]. Morning vs evening: performance peaks mostly in the afternoon–evening; morning training's advantage is adherence (fairly well established) [L3:D1]. The sleep-mechanism details belong to the Sleep volume — not rewritten here.

### 5.2 Fasted cardio: the extra fat burned never lands on the body

A 4-week trial of equal-volume cardio + calorie deficit (20 young women): fasted vs fed — the changes in fat mass and lean body mass showed **no significant difference** [L3:D2]. That acute fat oxidation is higher in the fasted state is true, but **acute oxidation ≠ long-term body composition**; those on diabetes medication have a separate hypoglycemia face (see 7.2) [L3:D2].

### 5.3 The protein "anabolic window": total intake is the guiding line, the window is a footnote

A breakpoint analysis of 49 trials and 1863 people: **below about 1.62 g/kg/day of total protein, resistance training's lean-mass gain rises with intake; past that point it stops rising**; the gain decays with age and is larger in those with a training history [L3:D3]. "A 30-minute anabolic window after training" has no pooled-level support — the day's total and the per-meal distribution (25–30 g per meal — belongs to the Eat volume) dominate; timely protein after fasted training still matters [L3:D3].

### 5.4 Cold-water immersion: faster recovery, less adaptation

A 12-week strength-training trial (21 people, 10 minutes of cold-water immersion after training vs active recovery): the **non-immersion** group gained more strength and muscle (isokinetic work +19%, type II muscle fibers +17%, myonuclei count +26%); the satellite-cell response was likewise blunted by the immersion (single trial · the extrapolation is fairly well established) [L3:D4]. The fork: need recovery for this week's competition/retraining → usable; want to build muscle and strength → avoid immediately after training [L3:D4].

**Summary**: evening training doesn't take the blame for bad sleep (vigorous work within 1 hour excepted); fasted fat-burning is an accounting trick on paper; protein is judged by the day's total; the ice bath is a recovery tool, not a get-stronger tool.

## Chapter 6. The side-effect face: the cost of a good thing

**Learning objectives**: state the ledger of the four accident families (wrong dose / wrong scene / wrong motivation / unassessed underlying condition) and the care-seeking red lines.

### 6.1 Overtraining syndrome

Training-recovery imbalance → persistently falling performance + mood disturbance + immune suppression; no reliable single biomarker, diagnosis is by exclusion, and recovery is counted in weeks to months; high risk = those ramping up for a competition and those "using training to fix their mood" [L3:E1].

### 6.2 Rhabdomyolysis: the classic accident of punishment-style exercise

Among 321 hospitalized physical-training-related cases in military personnel: 60.1% were linked to heat stress; the non-heat-linked cases had higher creatine kinase (the blood marker of muscle injury) [L3:E2]. High-risk scenes: beginners suddenly piling on high-volume eccentric work (downhill running, abrupt squat-volume jumps), dehydration in hot-humid conditions, training while ill (during viral infection), statins + intense exercise, sickle cell trait [L3:E2]. **The care line: soy-sauce-colored urine + severe muscle pain = seek care immediately**. "Punishment-style group exercise" is the classic rhabdo scene — **this is a management accident, not a willpower accident** [L3:E2].

### 6.3 Exercise addiction

Pooling 30 studies: combined detection rate 12.26%; higher in gym-goers and triathletes; 18–30-year-olds about 15 percentage points higher than the middle-aged; most dangerous in the comorbidity face with eating disorders (compulsive exercise + restricted eating lock together) [L3:E3]. The red-line triad: **training through injury, a sacrificed social life, mood held hostage by training**. Rest itself is training [L3:E3].

### 6.4 The knee's counterintuitive truth, and acute injury

25 studies, 126,000 people: hip/knee osteoarthritis prevalence — **recreational runners 3.5% vs sedentary controls 10.2% vs competitive runners 13.3%**; those with under 15 years of running history had significantly lower risk than the controls (OR 0.6) [L3:E4]. How to read it: moderate running is negatively associated with knee degeneration (observational; causality still being studied) — loads within the physiological window stimulate cartilage adaptation; the counterintuitive part is that **the sedentary have worse knees**; only competitive-level volumes flip it to a positive association; prescriptions for diagnosed arthritis are another matter (low-impact first) [L3:E4]. Acute injury rates run 20–50% a year, mostly from ramping up too fast; the "under 10% weekly increase" rule has weak empirical support — the core is total-volume management + a strength reserve [L3:E4]. **Post-injury rehabilitation prescriptions belong to the professional domain; this textbook does not write them.**

### 6.5 Cardiac screening: red-line triggers

26 years of pre-participation screening (including ECG) in Veneto, Italy: athletes' annual sudden cardiac death rate **-89%** (ecological evidence; causal attribution still being studied) [L3:E5]. HCM is the most common cause of sudden death in young athletes; universal ECG screening remains contested in the field (false-positive costs vs benefit) [L3:E5]. **The action line (not a check-up-package recommendation)**: before starting moderate-to-vigorous training, anyone with any of the following histories → get a medical evaluation before scaling up — family history of sudden death, exercise-induced syncope, chest pain during exercise, abnormal breathlessness [L3:E5].

### 6.6 Summary (guarding against list-checking misreads)

Wrong dose (overtraining/rhabdo), wrong scene (heat/illness/medication), wrong motivation (addiction/body image), unassessed underlying condition (the cardiac-screening face) — all four accident families have empirically documented ledgers [L3:E6].

## Chapter 7. Individualization: how to move starting from zero

**Learning objectives**: pick a version by starting point, chronic condition, and age; understand two realities — genetic response differences and adherence.

### 7.1 The zero-base safety ladder and the decision tree (Figure 7-1)

A five-tier ladder: ① walk more every day → ② build up to 150 min/week of brisk walking → ③ run-walk intervals plus a little vigorous work → ④ 2 resistance sessions a week → ⑤ layer on balance and flexibility by age. The rules: **low starting point · slow progression; symptoms/pain are a gate, not a schedule**; red lines get evaluated first; morbid obesity / severe joint degeneration choose low-impact options [L3:F1].

```
Figure 7-1 From zero base to a weekly plan — decision tree

Start: any red lines? (chest pain during exercise / exercise-induced syncope / family history of sudden death / abnormal breathlessness)
├─ Yes → medical evaluation first, start as the doctor directs; diagnosed cardiovascular disease goes through cardiac rehabilitation
│                                            [L3:E5/F2]
└─ No → which cell are you in now?
  [Fully sedentary] → Tiers 1–2: walk more every day → build to 150 min/week of brisk walking (able to talk, unable to sing)
  │   at 60+ also schedule 2–3 balance sessions a week             [L3:F1/F3/B3]
  [Already brisk-walking] → Tier 3: run-walk intervals, or add a small amount of vigorous work
  │   30–75 min of jogging a week in total already captures the better part  [L3:F1/A2]
  [Want to keep muscle / lose fat] → Tier 4: 2 weekly large-muscle-group resistance sessions (start with body weight/bands)
  │   judge protein by the day's total (capped at about 1.6 g/kg/day)    [L3:F1/D3/B2]
  [60+ / afraid of falling] → Tier 5: balance challenges 2–3 times a week (single-leg stance/tai chi)
  │   impact and heavy loads require evaluation + supervision       [L3:F3/B3/C5]
  Universal: low start · slow progression · symptoms and pain are the gate; on diabetes medication: test blood sugar before/after + carry carbs [L3:F1/F2]
```

### 7.2 Chronic-condition modifications

People on diabetes medication (insulin/sulfonylureas): exercise-induced hypoglycemia risk is real — test blood sugar before and after, carry carbs, and avoid long solo sessions timed at the medication's peak effect [L3:F2]. Hypertension: regular aerobic exercise is the first-line non-drug therapy; if uncontrolled, avoid maximal heavy-load breath-holding and extreme intensity; cool down slowly after training [L3:F2]. People with known cardiovascular disease: progress inside the cardiac-rehabilitation framework (professional domain) [L3:F2].

### 7.3 Age modifications

60 and above: the step plateau comes earlier, at 6000–8000; balance rises one tier as a goal; resistance and protein are raised together (the gain decays with age → adequacy and regularity matter more). Young people: the addiction-comorbidity face and the cardiomyopathy-screening face are this age band's own ledger. Middle-aged and older trainees: recovery slows — budget more recovery days; **the yardstick is your own self 3 months ago, not a leaderboard** [L3:F3].

### 7.4 Genes and adherence: two realities

**Genetic response differences**: the HERITAGE family study (473 sedentary adults, 20 weeks of standardized training): VO2max gain heritability 47%; those carrying ≤9 favorable alleles (favorable gene variants) averaged +221 vs +604 ml/min for those with ≥19 — **a threefold response difference to the same prescription** (confirmed); consumer genetic reports' predictive power = not known; the right answer = track yourself objectively (resting heart rate, pace, grip strength) [L3:G1].

**Adherence and detraining**: nearly half drop out within 6 months of an intervention — the lever = acceptability first (strength/yoga best) + groups + starting at the minimum dose; after detraining, cardiorespiratory fitness falls faster than muscle strength (the exact half-life = not known — no invented numbers here); when restarting, drop back one or two tiers on the ladder [L3:G2].

**Summary**: the largest dose you can sustain > the theoretically optimal dose; genes set the starting-point spread, and tracking yourself is the right answer.

## Chapter 8. Frequently asked questions (FAQ · 10 questions)

1. **Is 10,000 steps a day a must?** No. The plateau is 8000–10000 steps under 60 and 6000–8000 over 60; 10,000 steps is a marketing number. [L3:A2]
2. **Does running wreck the knees or not?** Recreational runners' arthritis rate is 3.5% vs 10.2% in the sedentary — moderate running is negatively associated with knee degeneration (observational); only competitive-level volumes flip it positive. [L3:E4]
3. **Does exercising too much shorten life?** On total mortality there is no reliable evidence; the real costs are musculoskeletal injury and the heart's local changes under extreme endurance. [L3:A3]
4. **Is the wristband's "fat-burning zone" (Zone2) the optimal intensity?** No. "Optimal" has been falsified; the value is a low-fatigue, high-volume base, and for ordinary people adding vigorous work matters more. [L3:B1]
5. **Can exercise replace antidepressants?** Mild to moderate: first-line scale — it can stand alongside them as a first choice or be combined; severe, or any thoughts of self-harm: seek care first — exercise is not a substitute. [L3:C1]
6. **Must I drink protein within 30 minutes after training?** No. The day's total (capped at about 1.6 g/kg/day) and the per-meal distribution dominate. [L3:D3]
7. **Does evening exercise hurt sleep?** Not overall — slow-wave sleep actually rises; vigorous work within 1 hour before bed is the weak-signal trap. [L3:D1]
8. **Is an ice bath / cold shower after training good?** Depends on the goal: usable for competition-week recovery; avoided if building muscle and strength (adaptation gets blunted). [L3:D4]
9. **Will training with a grip device make me live longer?** Low grip strength predicts death (confirmed), but whether training grip lowers death is not known — a gauge, not a target; train the whole body's large muscle groups. [L3:B2]
10. **Are walking and swimming enough for osteoporosis?** No — walking or swimming alone is essentially ineffective for bone density; bone wants impact + heavy load, with evaluation and supervision. [L3:C5]
