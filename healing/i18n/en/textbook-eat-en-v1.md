# The Eating Textbook v1 (2026-09-12)

**Readers**: self-learners and health workers. **How to use**: every chapter has learning objectives and a summary; the section-end anchor ([L3:id]) leads back to the evidence-layer source text (assertion + sources + falsifier). This textbook never states observational evidence as causation, and never states mechanistic evidence as a hard outcome — epistemic states are aligned verbatim with L3, zero promotion.

## Chapter 0. The one-page version (L1 · zero-background readable)

> Abridged companion text: this chapter is a condensed transcription of `docs/healing/textbook/eat-l1-80yi-20260912.md`, not word-identical; the companion is the fuller, supplemented version and can be distributed on its own.

**In one sentence: what you eat matters more than when you eat or how many meals you eat. Swapping ultra-processed food for whole foods and eating enough fiber are the two moves with the hardest evidence.**

Hold every claim to the four confidence tiers: confirmed by research (verified in controlled trials) → fairly well established (mechanism clear or single-trial support) → still being studied (observational leads, causation unproven) → not known (don't trust any claim about it).

**Seven action cards**: ① finish dinner 3 hours before bed (confirmed by research); ② eat a good breakfast, keep dinner small and early (fairly well established; the long term still being studied); ③ start from 25-29 g of fiber a day — the highest-fiber people carry 15-30% lower risk of death and chronic disease than the lowest (confirmed by research); ④ 2-6 servings a day of genuinely fermented food — inflammatory markers visibly drop within 10 weeks (confirmed by research · single trial); ⑤ keep ultra-processed food to a minimum — in a calorie-matched trial people unknowingly ate about 500 kcal more per day on it (confirmed by research); ⑥ about 25-30 g of protein per meal; total amount matters more than timing (fairly well established); ⑦ for weight loss pick the way you can keep up — 16:8 is a tool, not magic (confirmed by research: about equal to ordinary calorie restriction).

**Myth quick-check**: 16:8 is almighty (no) ｜ no-eating-after-noon is mandatory (no) ｜ supplements can replace meals (no) ｜ one single superfood (no) ｜ the acid-alkaline body type (food cannot change the acidity of your blood). **Three things not known**: the long-term safety of OMAD; intermittent fasting and lifespan; any effects of noon-cutoff eating specific to women.

## Chapter 1. Concepts and terminology

**Learning objectives**: after this chapter you can define four core concepts — calories and the calorie deficit, macronutrients, glycemic response, UPF — and read this textbook's evidence-grading language.

### 1.1 Calories and energy balance

The calorie unit used here is the kilocalorie (kcal). The first determinant of weight change is energy balance: intake below expenditure = calorie deficit = weight loss. This is not dogma — it is the shared foundation of every weight-loss trial: 16:8 and alternate-day fasting work through the route "the window shortened → you naturally eat less", not through metabolic magic [L3:A1 MODEL].

### 1.2 Macronutrients

Protein, carbohydrate, and fat are together the macronutrients. They supply calories: carbohydrate and protein about 4 kcal per gram, fat about 9 kcal per gram. The fight over macronutrient "ratios" (low-fat vs low-carb) was the biggest diet debate of the past thirty years; Chapter 3 gives the verdict.

### 1.3 Glycemic response and individual variation

Glycemic response = how high and how fast blood sugar rises after a meal. The glycemic index (GI) is a population average for each food — but an 800-person continuous glucose monitoring study found that **the glycemic response to the same food varies enormously from person to person**; a machine-learning model (gut microbiome + blood markers + lifestyle) could predict individual responses and, under blinded validation, lowered individuals' post-meal glucose; but whether "some commercial algorithm works universally" is not known [L3:E1 FACT/UNKNOWN].

### 1.4 Ultra-processed food (UPF)

UPF means food made from industrial formulations: soft drinks, packaged snacks, reconstituted meats, most ready-to-eat products — the signature is a long ingredient list of things a home kitchen cannot make. The contrast: whole foods (natural or minimally processed). UPF is the hardest-evidence item on the quality axis; see Chapter 3 for the full case [L3:C1 FACT].

### 1.5 Evidence grading and epistemic states

This textbook carries over L3's five epistemic states, mapped onto the four plain-language tiers:

| L3 epistemic state | Plain-language tier | What it means |
|---|---|---|
| FACT | confirmed by research | verified in RCTs or systematic reviews, with sources checked word-for-word |
| MODEL | fairly well established | holds at the level of a clear mechanism / a single trial / surrogate markers |
| INFERENCE | still being studied | observational association; direction consistent but causation unproven |
| CONTESTED | still contested in the field | high-quality evidence in mutual conflict |
| UNKNOWN | not known | honestly not known — don't trust any assertion |

### Glossary (in order of appearance)

1. **Kilocalorie (kcal)**: the unit for food energy; the "large calorie" on food packaging is a kilocalorie.
2. **Calorie deficit**: intake persistently below expenditure; the necessary condition for weight loss.
3. **Macronutrients**: the three energy-supplying nutrients — protein, carbohydrate, fat.
4. **Glycemic response**: how high and how fast blood sugar rises after a meal.
5. **Glycemic index (GI)**: the population-average ranking of how fast a food raises blood sugar.
6. **Insulin sensitivity**: how efficiently the body responds to insulin's glucose-lowering signal — the higher the better.
7. **Ultra-processed food (UPF)**: industrially formulated food with a long ingredient list.
8. **Whole foods**: natural or minimally processed food.
9. **Dietary fiber**: plant carbohydrate the small intestine does not digest; it feeds gut bacteria and increases satiety.
10. **Fermented foods**: food fermented by live cultures (yogurt / kefir / kimchi / miso / kombucha).
11. **Lean body mass**: the part of body weight that is not fat, mostly muscle — protect it when losing weight.
12. **Time-restricted eating (TRE)**: confining all eating to a set window, e.g., 16:8 = 16 hours without food, eating within 8 hours.
13. **Alternate-day fasting (ADF) / 5:2**: a pattern alternating "fasting days and eating days".
14. **OMAD**: one meal a day.
15. **Adherence**: how much of the protocol you actually follow — the largest effect modifier in trials.
16. **Surrogate markers**: intermediate markers such as blood glucose and inflammatory markers (≠ outcomes).
17. **Hard outcomes**: real outcomes such as death, heart attack, diagnosed diabetes.
18. **AGEs**: advanced glycation end-products, produced by high-temperature cooking; steaming, boiling, and stewing produce far fewer than deep-frying and grilling.
19. **RCT**: randomized controlled trial — the gold standard of causal evidence.
20. **CGM**: continuous glucose monitoring — seeing your own (not the population-average) glycemic response.

**Summary**: the calorie deficit explains every weight-loss pattern; macronutrient ratio is a debate, quality is a verdict; a GI table is an average, individual data is what is precise; before believing any conclusion, ask which evidence tier it stands on.

## Chapter 2. When to eat (chrononutrition)

**Learning objectives**: be able to answer what 16:8, noon-cutoff eating, and many-small-meals eating each actually achieve, and state the cost of eating late and where its applicable population ends.

### 2.1 The truth about 16:8: no better than calorie restriction

A 12-month RCT (n=139): with the same calorie restriction, whether or not it was packed into an 8-hour eating window, weight loss showed no significant difference (-8.0 vs -6.3 kg); waist circumference, body fat, lean body mass, blood pressure, and metabolic markers likewise showed no difference [L3:A1 FACT]. Mechanistically, TRE's weight loss comes from the **spontaneous calorie deficit** the shortened window creates [L3:A1 MODEL]; its real value is as an **adherence tool** — the rule is simple and no calorie counting is needed [L3:A1 INFERENCE]. Over one year, alternate-day fasting (ADF/5:2) was likewise about equal to daily calorie restriction, with the highest drop-out rate (38%) and the hardest prescription to follow [L3:A2 FACT]; short-term ADF loses lean body mass along with fat, and low-dose protein supplementation failed to prevent it [L3:A2 MODEL].

### 2.2 OMAD and many small meals

The evidence for one meal a day is extremely thin: the only fairly rigorous small trial shows only that weight can drop in the short term; its long-term safety and effectiveness are **not known** [L3:A3 MODEL弱→UNKNOWN]. A single large meal also struggles to give muscle the 25-30 g per-meal protein synthesis stimulus [L3:A3 MODEL]. At the other extreme, "many small meals raise your metabolism" equally lacks hard human evidence: at the same total calories, meal frequency has very little effect on weight loss, and spreading the same calories across more meals only flattens the glucose curve [L3:A4 MODEL].

### 2.3 Earlier vs later: the cost of eating late

A strictly isocaloric randomized crossover trial: moving the same meals to later in the day made people hungrier (hunger P<0.0001), raised the appetite-hormone ratio (ghrelin:leptin), lowered energy expenditure during the waking phase, and shifted adipose-tissue gene expression toward "less breakdown, more storage" [L3:B1 FACT]. The reverse: an early eating window (6-hour window, dinner before 15:00) improved insulin sensitivity, beta-cell response, blood pressure, and appetite without any weight loss (5 weeks, men with prediabetes, small sample) [L3:B1 FACT]. The practical judgment integrated from all of this — **"more earlier, less later; front-load your calories" — does not mean skipping dinner** (long-term hard outcomes still being studied) [L3:B1 INFERENCE].

### 2.4 The dinner window and sleep

Finishing dinner 3 hours before bed improves gastroesophageal reflux (guideline-level) [L3:B2 FACT]. The circadian mechanisms around late eating — melatonin-insulin crosstalk among them — hold but have not reached verdict grade [L3:B2 MODEL]; "people who eat late show worse long-term weight and cardiovascular outcomes" comes from observational evidence, with direction and mechanism in agreement [L3:B3 INFERENCE]. One more cut from the sleep side: high fiber → more deep sleep; high saturated fat → less deep sleep; high sugar → more nighttime awakenings (small-sample inpatient crossover trial) [睡正典C3 FACT]; the faster sleep onset that alcohol buys is paid for with a shattered second half of the night and reduced REM [睡正典C2 FACT].

### 2.5 Protein timing

25-30 g of protein per meal (breakfast included) favors muscle synthesis in older adults; total daily amount dominates, and the post-exercise "anabolic window" is far looser than legend has it [L3:B4 MODEL].

### 2.6 Who should not do fasting patterns

For people with diabetes using insulin or sulfonylurea secretagogues, fasting = a substantial hypoglycemia risk (mechanism confirmed); any attempt must come with a doctor adjusting the medication plus self-monitored blood glucose [L3:A5 FACT]. For people with a history of eating disorders, fasting patterns can trigger relapse [L3:A5 MODEL]. Pregnancy and breastfeeding, adolescents, low body weight/malnutrition, and type 1 diabetes should avoid deliberate fasting [L3:A5 MODEL]. Side-effect list: hunger and irritability in the first two weeks, conflicts with social meals, high drop-out rates, possible loss of lean body mass [L3:A5 FACT/MODEL].

**Summary**: for every "when to eat" flourish, the hard effect never exceeds "total calories + whether you can keep it up"; the only mechanistically clear bonus item is front-loading calories and finishing dinner early. People on the medications above and the populations above do not fast.

## Chapter 3. What to eat (quality-axis priorities)

**Learning objectives**: be able to rank the "what to eat" priorities and understand why the macronutrient-ratio debate is closed.

### 3.1 First priority: swap UPF for whole foods

In an inpatient RCT: UPF meals vs unprocessed meals with calories, macronutrients, sugar, sodium, and fiber **all matched** — under free-eating conditions, people ate **508±106 kcal** more per day during the UPF phase, gaining +0.9 kg over two weeks (-0.9 during the unprocessed phase) [L3:C1 FACT]. Conclusion: the quality axis ranks first — cut UPF, a priority above agonizing over macronutrient ratios and meal counts [L3:C1 INFERENCE].

### 3.2 Fiber and fermented foods

Highest vs lowest fiber intake: 15-30% lower risk of all-cause/cardiovascular death, coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and colorectal cancer; the best zone is 25-29 g/day with more being better; on the RCT side, weight, systolic blood pressure, and total cholesterol fell (185 prospective studies + 58 RCTs) [L3:C3 FACT]. Fermented foods: a 10-week RCT (about 6 servings a day) raised microbiome diversity and lowered inflammatory markers — in the same study, the high-fiber arm did not achieve this; "fermented beats fiber" is not a conclusion; your baseline decides the response [L3:C3 FACT单试验→泛化MODEL；微正典B1]. Actionable: 2-6 servings a day of genuinely fermented food, starting from 1 serving; combine the three fiber kinds (soluble fermentable / insoluble / resistant starch); 30 different plants a week is a reasonable target (observational) [微正典B2].

### 3.3 Cooking methods

Deep-fried food intake is associated with major cardiovascular events (highest vs lowest +28%; for each additional 114 g per week, major events +3% and heart failure +12%) — an observational association [L3:C2 INFERENCE]. High-temperature cooking produces AGEs, and low-AGEs diets improved glucose and lipid markers in RCTs (not hard outcomes) [L3:C2 MODEL]. In practice: steam, boil, and stew as the default; deep-frying and grilling less often.

### 3.4 The macronutrient-ratio debate, decided

A 609-person, 12-month RCT (DIETFITS): healthy low-fat vs healthy low-carb showed no significant difference in weight loss (-5.3 vs -6.0 kg), and neither genotype nor baseline insulin secretion predicted which would work better [L3:D1 FACT]; both arms emphasized quality (vegetables, whole grains, less sugar and refined starch) — the decider was quality and adherence [L3:D1 INFERENCE]. On long-term health: the carbohydrate energy share is U-shaped against mortality, with lowest risk at 50-55%; both <40% and >70% raise it; and low-carb is worse when the carbohydrate is replaced with animal-based foods [L3:D2 INFERENCE]. Lowering saturated fat reduces composite cardiovascular events by about 17-21% [L3:D2 FACT]; the Mediterranean diet (+ olive oil or nuts) lowered major cardiovascular events by about 28-31% in a high-risk population (single-center large trial, robust in direction after re-publication) [L3:D2 FACT附注]; DASH lowered blood pressure by -8~14 mmHg — the magnitude of a first-line drug [L3:D2 FACT]. **The verdict: for weight loss, ratio is irrelevant; for chronic-disease prevention, pattern quality decides. Any "one ratio fits all" lacks sufficient evidence** [L3:D2 组合判定].

### 3.5 Individual variation and funding bias

A generic GI table is only a population average; the most precise data is your own CGM [L3:E1 INFERENCE]. Weight-loss success correlates most strongly with adherence + diet quality (not genes or baseline metabolism) — **"the best diet you can actually stick to" beats "the theoretically optimal diet"** [L3:E2 FACT/INFERENCE]. Be alert: studies exclusively sponsored by food companies are significantly likelier to reach favorable conclusions (one analysis: 4-8 times) [L3:E3 FACT] — when you meet "this food is miraculous", check the funder first.

**The quality-axis priority table**:

| Priority | Action | Evidence tier |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cut UPF; swap in whole foods | confirmed by research |
| 2 | Fiber 25-29 g+/day | confirmed by research |
| 3 | Fermented foods, 2-6 servings a day | confirmed (single trial) |
| 4 | Mediterranean/DASH skeleton + low saturated fat | confirmed by research |
| 5 | Steam, boil, stew as the default; deep-fry and grill less often | fairly well established / still being studied |
| 6 | Carbohydrate at 45-55%, no anxiety-driven extremes | still being studied (U-shape, observational) |

**Summary**: the quality axis overrides the timing axis and the ratio axis. Get UPF down and fiber up first; the rest comes after.

## Chapter 4. Different goals (the decision tree)

**Learning objectives**: be able to pick a starting point from your own goal and contraindications, and know each route's evidence tier.

```
Start: is any contraindication present? (pregnancy/breastfeeding · adolescents · low body weight · eating-disorder history · type 1 diabetes · on insulin or sulfonylureas)
├─ Yes → no fasting pattern of any kind; regular three meals + the quality axis as the starting point; if on medication and wanting to try window eating, a doctor must adjust the medication first + paired glucose self-testing
│        [L3:A5]
└─ No → by main goal:
  [Weight loss]
   ├─ Hate counting calories → 16:8 is workable: effect ≈ calorie restriction (confirmed); its value is being easy to keep up (its tool property still being studied)
   │   inside the window, still eat whole foods [L3:A1+C1]
   ├─ Willing to count → ordinary calorie restriction is enough; no need to force fasting [L3:A1]
   ├─ Torn between → pick the one you can keep up: over 12 months the decider is adherence [L3:E2]
   └─ Under any pattern: secure per-meal protein + resistance training, to prevent losing lean body mass [L3:A2/A3/B4]
  [Metabolic / cardiovascular / blood glucose]
   ├─ Step one is always the quality axis: cut UPF → fiber 25-29 g+ → Mediterranean/DASH skeleton → low saturated fat [L3:C1/C3/D2]
   ├─ Timing-axis bonus: front-load calories + dinner finished 3 h before bed [L3:B1/B2]
   └─ If you have access to a CGM, read your own glycemic response; don't lean on the generic GI table alone [L3:E1]
  [Longevity / healthy aging]
   ├─ No human lifespan RCT exists (not known) → the best current extrapolation: quality axis + no excess + muscle defense
   │   (25-30 g protein per meal · fairly well established) [L3:F+C3+B4]
   └─ Window eating can serve as scaffolding for calorie control; older adults and people at sarcopenia risk do not narrow the window [L3:A3]
  [No particular goal]
   = whole foods as the backbone + fiber and fermented foods always on hand + steam/boil/stew + regular meals with calories front-loaded + no carb extremes + a localized Mediterranean/DASH
     [L3:C1/C3/C2/B1/B2/D2]
```

**Summary**: screen contraindications first, then choose the goal; in weight loss the decider is adherence, in metabolism it is quality, and for longevity there are no shortcuts — only extrapolation.

## Chapter 5. Different populations

**Learning objectives**: know which population you or a family member belongs to, and which version applies.

| Population | Version essentials | Evidence tier |
|---|---|---|
| Pregnancy / breastfeeding | no deliberate fasting; regular meals + the quality axis; folic acid and the rest per prenatal-checkup doctor's orders | fairly well established (clinical consensus) [L3:A5；维正典] |
| Children and adolescents | no fasting patterns (growth phase, and eating-window research does not cover them); whole foods + regular meals | fairly well established [L3:A5] |
| Older adults | do not narrow the eating window; 25-30 g protein per meal to defend against sarcopenia; OMAD not applicable | fairly well established [L3:B4/A3 MODEL] |
| Eating-disorder history | fasting patterns can trigger relapse — avoid; see mental health first, then discuss diet structure | fairly well established (clinical consensus) [L3:A5] |
| Diabetes on medication | insulin/sulfonylureas + fasting = substantial hypoglycemia risk; medication adjustment + self-testing must come first | confirmed by research (mechanism) [L3:A5 FACT] |
| Type 1 diabetes | avoid deliberate fasting (diabetic ketoacidosis risk) | fairly well established [L3:A5] |
| Healthy adults, general version | the Chapter 4 default version applies in full | see each row |

**Where supplements fit**: for routine vitamin needs, **food first** — supplements are a patch for deficiency risk, not a lever for health gains; except for diagnosed deficiency and the few mandatory-supplement scenarios (such as folic acid in early pregnancy), the order is meals first, fruit after — the food-first sequence *(translator calibration: the source's compact motto 先饭后果, rendered per the vitamins canon's food-first principle)* [维正典第五节 FACT].

**Summary**: the core of population differences is not "mysterious constitutions" but medication, the growth phase, and muscle defense — these three decide whether you can fast at all and whether protein deserves the emphasis.

## Chapter 6. FAQ (12 questions)

1. **Is cutting off eating after noon actually good?** There is no "must" about it. It is just one way to create a calorie deficit; mechanistically, more-earlier-less-later has advantages (fairly well established), but the long-term hard outcomes are still being studied; if you cannot keep it up, switch — over 12 months the contest is decided by adherence [L3:A1/B1/E2].
2. **Should you eat breakfast?** "Skipping breakfast harms your health" is unproven, but the mechanism side supports front-loading calories (a bigger breakfast, a small early dinner); if you do eat breakfast, pair it with 25-30 g of protein [L3:B1/B4].
3. **Fruit is so sweet — is its sugar load high?** Whole fruit is a whole food: it brings fiber and strong satiety, standing on the favorable side of the quality axis. What needs limiting is added sugar and fruit juice with the fiber stripped out — the juice point is a common-sense-level inference; L3 lists no standalone assertion for it; read it inside the C1 framework, not as a hard conclusion [L3:C1].
4. **Is soup nourishing?** The L3 baseline does not cover this question (the "not known" tier). The safe framing: the protein is in the meat, not in the broth; a soup's main contents are water, salt, purines, and fat; "all the essence ends up in the soup" lacks supporting evidence.
5. **Can many small meals raise your metabolism?** No. At equal total calories, meal frequency has very little effect on weight loss; it only makes the glucose curve flatter [L3:A4].
6. **During 16:8, does black coffee or unsweetened tea break the fast?** L3 has no standalone assertion (the "not known" tier). In principle, zero-calorie drinks do not break the "window creates the deficit" mechanism [L3:A1 MODEL]; if caffeine disturbs your sleep, move the cutoff earlier per the sleep canon [睡正典C5].
7. **Hungry before bed — what then?** The first-choice fix is to arrange dinner earlier and more substantial (front-loading calories); if you are still hungry, a small protein-type snack is more reasonable than white-knuckling it (a practical inference; the direction "gutting it out harms sleep architecture" has direction-level support from the sleep side) [L3:B1/B4；睡正典C3].
8. **Should you quit carbohydrates?** No need. Mortality is lowest at a 50-55% energy share; both <40% and >70% raise it. What should be quit is refined sugar and ultra-processed carbs — not carbohydrate itself [L3:D2].
9. **Should you take a multivitamin?** Food first: routine needs can be met by meals; supplements are a patch, not a lever; the exceptions are diagnosed deficiency, folic acid in early pregnancy, and whatever a doctor instructs [维正典第五节].
10. **Can intermittent fasting extend life?** Not known. The animal calorie-restriction evidence is strong; no human RCT with a lifespan endpoint exists [L3:F].
11. **How do you judge "the latest study says food X is miraculous/harmful"?** Three checks: who funded it (industry-exclusive sponsorship runs 4-8 times more favorable conclusions), what epistemic state it holds (animal/mechanistic ≠ hard human outcomes), and whether it carries a falsification clause [L3:E3；L3 认识态定义].
12. **Is continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) worth doing?** For understanding your own (rather than the population-average) glycemic response — worth it; but counting on some commercial algorithm to output a universal meal plan — not known [L3:E1].
