# Depression & Anxiety Healing Canon v1 (English)

> **Who this is for**: people currently in a low valley, people with someone in a low valley beside them, and people who want to understand a little in advance.
> **How to read this volume**: no need to read in order. Face 5 (suicide intervention) placed this early is numbering, not an importance ranking — it is the highest-priority chapter; any suspicion should turn there first.
> **Tone promise**: companionship, not judgment; no preaching, no chicken soup; treating "going to see a doctor" as normal a thing as seeing someone about a cold or a broken bone.

---

## Face 1 · The boundary face: depressed mood ≠ major depressive disorder

### 1.1 Mood is weather; the disorder is climate

Everyone feels sad. The low mood, emptiness, and not-wanting-to-move after a failed exam, a breakup, or the death of a loved one are the **normal human response to loss** — moods rise and fall, just as weather has cloud and sun.

Major depressive disorder is not "a more severe sadness"; it is **a disease state with a clear medical boundary**, three criteria of which none can be missing:

1. **Duration**: depressed mood or loss of interest/pleasure on most days, most of the time, for ≥ 2 weeks;
2. **Symptom cluster**: accompanied by multiple of — sleep change, appetite/weight change, fatigue, reduced concentration, self-negation or excessive guilt, psychomotor retardation or agitation, recurrent thoughts of death;
3. **Functional impact**: visible impairment of work, study, housework, or relationships.

[MODEL/clinical consensus] These three boundaries matter because they protect two kinds of people at once: they keep briefly sad people from being mislabeled as diseased, and keep truly ill patients from being waved off with "you're just in a bad mood."

**WHO framing (FACT, verified 2026-09-12)**: about **5.7%** of adults worldwide have major depressive disorder (men 4.6%, women 6.9%, over-70s 5.9%), about **332 million** people in total; WHO also states explicitly: mild, moderate, and severe depression **all have effective treatments**.

### 1.2 Falsification: "strong people don't get depressed"

The error of this sentence is treating a disease as a character trait. Depression has multi-layer correlates — neurochemical (dysregulation hypotheses of the serotonin/norepinephrine/dopamine systems), neural-circuit (abnormal function of reward and default-mode networks), inflammatory, and genetic-susceptibility — and none of them checks whose willpower is stronger. Clinically, highly conscientious, long-term high-functioning people are precisely a common patient portrait: their very "strength" becomes a reason for delayed care ("I can't fall apart").

> The correct understanding: **depression is not evidence of weakness; running while carrying the disease until collapse is, if anything, evidence of will.** Treating illness requires treatment, not "shouldering harder."

### 1.3 Somatization: many people don't know they are on the depression spectrum

[MODEL/clinical consensus · especially important in the Chinese-speaking context] A considerable share of depression patients **never proactively say "I feel low"**; their chief complaints are:

- can't fall asleep, early waking, sleeping but never rested;
- can't eat, bloating, constipation, weight loss;
- migrating pains all over the body, headache, stiff neck and shoulders, palpitations and chest tightness;
- "no energy," "tired."

This is called **somatization**: emotional distress expressed in the language of bodily symptoms. And so large numbers of patients shuttle among gastroenterology, cardiology, pain, and neurology clinics, getting repeated tests that "find no major problem" — and that very "nothing found" is itself a clue. If multiple bodily symptoms coexist with low mood and lost interest for more than two weeks, a psychiatry/psychology visit should come to mind. This is not a "wasted trip" — it is connecting the route to the right department.

## Face 2 · The evidence face: what genuinely works

### 2.1 Exercise: effect sizes reported as they are

**Noetel et al. 2024, published in BMJ (BMJ 2024;384:q1024, PMID 38806193), a network meta-analysis** (178 randomized controlled trials included), is currently the most comprehensive evidence synthesis in this field, and its conclusions should be relayed faithfully as:

- Walking/jogging, yoga, and strength training are the exercise modalities most strongly associated with depression-symptom improvement, **with effect sizes roughly comparable in magnitude to psychotherapy and SSRI medication**;
- Higher intensity trends toward greater benefit, but **low-intensity activity (walking, yoga) also carries benefit**;
- The authors' own at-the-words qualifier: **"confidence in many findings is still low,"** and exercise "could be considered **alongside** psychotherapy and drugs as core treatments."

> **The non-over-promising wording**: exercise is **one of** the evidence-based core treatments, not "a natural miracle cure for all depression." For a severe depressive episode, relying on exercise alone in place of comprehensive treatment is dangerous (see Face 8). Singh et al. 2023's umbrella review in the British Journal of Sports Medicine (PMID 36796860) supports the effectiveness of physical-activity interventions across a wider spectrum (depression, anxiety, psychological distress) — "effective" and "all-purpose" are two different words.

**An executable starting point** [INFERENCE·extrapolated from the evidence above]: start with 10–20 minutes of walking a day; the form you can sustain is the best form. When putting exercise into a treatment plan, tell your doctor — it will be treated as part of the treatment, not a substitute.

### 2.2 Psychotherapy: the cognitive-behavioral face

CBT (cognitive behavioral therapy) is the most-tested psychotherapy in the depression-and-anxiety domain. Butler et al. 2006's meta-analytic review in Clinical Psychology Review (PMID 16199119) confirmed CBT's large effect sizes across multiple disorders and its long-term effects. CBT's core mechanism is not "someone talks you into feeling better"; it is:

- **Identify**: catch automatic negative thoughts ("I'm no good at anything");
- **Test**: check the distance between thought and fact the way one examines evidence;
- **Replace**: build more accurate (not blindly optimistic) alternative thoughts;
- **Behavioral activation**: depression makes people withdraw → withdrawal cuts positive feedback → more depression; breaking the loop starts from "do a little first," not from waiting until you "feel like it."

[MODEL] Mindfulness-based interventions (MBSR/MBCT) have steadily accumulating evidence for preventing depressive relapse and lowering anxiety reactivity; mindfulness is not "emptying the brain" — it is training the attention skill of "noticing a thought without being dragged away by it."

### 2.3 Light therapy: the dedicated key for seasonality

[FACT+MODEL] Seasonal affective disorder (SAD, the depression subtype peaking in autumn–winter) has dedicated evidence for **morning bright-light exposure** (10,000-lux light box · used on waking · usually 20–45 minutes); the Cochrane review team (May et al., abstract PMID 32111489) notes that for **prevention** the evidence remains limited — treatment and prevention are two different sentences. The mechanism relates to the "light–circadian rhythm–mood" pathway (systematically mapped in a 2026 Life review, PMID 41900968). Ordinary depression patients need not buy a light box on their own as an all-purpose lamp, but those with a clear seasonal pattern deserve to discuss it with their doctor.

### 2.4 Sleep deprivation: the fastest effect and the least sustainable method

[FACT+MODEL] A whole night without sleep (or late-half-of-the-night wake therapy) can produce a rapid antidepressant effect **within 24 hours** — one of the fastest-acting interventions in psychiatry; inpatient settings have studies of combined "partial wake therapy + light" protocols (Psychiatry Res 2024, PMID 38688120). But in most patients **the effect dissipates after the next sleep**, and it requires professionally managed settings. It tells us two things: the depressive state can be quickly rewritten by physiological means (it is not a personality set in stone); and — precisely because the fast tool is unsustainable, the long-term plan (medication + psychotherapy + rhythm management) is all the more necessary. **Do not attempt staying awake continuously to "regulate your mood" on your own**; in bipolar disorder, sleep deprivation can trigger mania.

## Face 3 · The medication face, an honest ledger

### 3.1 Onset time and the "first-two-weeks discontinuation trap"

[MODEL/clinical consensus + FACT directional anchor] Antidepressants (such as the SSRIs) **usually need 2–6 weeks for full effect**; somatic symptoms like sleep and appetite may improve first, with mood improvement coming up from behind. Fine-grained onset research is advancing (e.g., the Can J Psychiatry 2026 "2–4 week myth" paper, PMID 42095762, suggesting the improvement trajectory is more complex than the traditional framing).

From this comes a classic danger window: **in the first two weeks on the medication, side effects (nausea, dizziness, sleep disruption, sexual-function effects) arrive before efficacy — and many people conclude at this moment that "the drug doesn't work / this drug isn't for me" and stop on their own.** Take this sentence with you: **"worse first, then better" is the common pharmacodynamic rhythm of this drug class, not a sign you took the wrong pill.** Whether to stop or switch is a decision made with the prescribing doctor, not with the pill box.

### 3.2 Discontinuation syndrome ≠ addiction

[FACT+MODEL] After long-term use, abrupt interruption can bring dizziness, electric-shock sensations, flu-like symptoms, insomnia, irritability — this is **discontinuation syndrome**; Davies et al. 2019's systematic review (Addict Behav, PMID 30292574) specifically examined its incidence, severity, and time course, and noted that clinical guidelines have long under-described discontinuation reactions. Distinguish two things:

| | Addiction (dependence) | Discontinuation syndrome |
|---|---|---|
| Behavioral drive | Craving, urge to escalate, using to "feel high" | No craving; symptoms arise from withdrawal of physiological adaptation |
| Management | Requires addiction treatment | **Slow tapering** — stepping down under a doctor's guidance; symptoms are mostly reversible |

SSRIs do not produce "rewarding craving," so **antidepressants are not addictive drugs**; but "stopping cold" is wrong — the correct posture is always gradual tapering + medical supervision. Saying both sentences in full is what honesty requires.

### 3.3 Falsification: "antidepressants change your personality"

[Falsification·MODEL] The drug acts on symptoms (mood, anxiety, sleep, energy); it does not rewrite "who you are." The clinical observation runs the other way: after symptoms remit, people are more like their original selves — able to laugh again, to care again. Side effects discussed in research, such as "emotional blunting," are **symptom-level and reportable to the doctor for adjustment** — a different matter from "personality being changed." What truly changes the personality trajectory long-term is untreated depression itself (cognitive scarring, loss of social functioning).

### 3.4 Seeking help = normal

Psychiatric and psychological outpatient clinics are ordinary clinics. The act of registering is no different from seeing someone about hypertension or gastritis. Viable paths in the Chinese context: psychology/psychiatry outpatient departments of general hospitals, specialized mental health centers, community-health referrals — **seeing a doctor for depression or anxiety is seeing a doctor, not "being labeled."** Medical records are private; a care-seeking record does not touch your character — it serves only your health.

## Face 4 · The anxiety face: from instinct to disorder

### 4.1 Anxiety's adaptive function and the pathological boundary

Anxiety itself is not disease — it is an early-warning system for threat: moderate pre-exam tension improves performance; vigilance crossing the street saves lives. The pathological boundary reads on three lines [MODEL]:

1. **Intensity/duration mismatched to the real threat** (the alarm persists when there is no threat);
2. **Cannot be switched off by will** (wanting to stop and being unable to);
3. **Avoidance and functional impairment** (shrinking one's life to escape the anxiety).

### 4.2 Panic attacks: "won't kill you, yet utterly real"

[MODEL/clinical consensus] A panic attack is a sudden surge of intense fear with bodily symptoms: palpitations, a sense of choking, chest pain, trembling, numbness, a feeling of imminent death, a "going crazy" feeling. Two honest facts:

- **Physiology**: this is a full-volume alarm storm of the sympathetic nervous system — adrenaline pulses, heart rate and breathing hijacked — **but the heart and body themselves face no corresponding structural danger**. People with repeated attacks have almost all had ECGs, and they are normal; that very "tested and normal" is itself diagnostic information.
- **Self-limited in time**: attacks usually peak within minutes and ease on their own within tens of minutes (textbooks commonly cite 10 minutes to peak, 20–30 minutes to subside). **The sense of imminent death is a symptom, not a prophecy.**

The secondary harm comes precisely from "fear of the attack": starting to avoid exercise, meetings, the subway, being alone — preventive avoidance feeds the next attack. The core treatments for panic disorder (interoceptive exposure within CBT, medication when needed) target exactly this loop.

### 4.3 Direction-level recognition: generalized anxiety and social anxiety

[MODEL · for directional recognition only; diagnosis belongs to the doctor]

- **Generalized anxiety disorder (GAD)**: uncontrollable excessive worry about everyday matters (health, money, family, work), most of the day, for months on end (diagnostic line: 6 months), with restlessness, easy fatigue, poor concentration, irritability, muscle tension, sleep disturbance. Keywords: **a diffuse, un-switch-off-able "what if."**
- **Social anxiety**: the core is fear of being scrutinized plus long rumination after embarrassment; avoiding speaking up, parties, occasions of being watched; the distress is out of proportion to the situation's actual "danger level." Keywords: **a magnifier for "what do others think of me."** The mild social nervousness most people have is not a disorder; it enters the medical view only when avoidance starts to compress life's options.

### 4.4 The core mechanism: avoidance is anxiety's fuel

[MODEL · the most important sentence of this face] The anxiety system earns negative reinforcement from "avoid → anxiety drops immediately" — **every act of avoidance tops up the next attack of anxiety.** Short term it is a painkiller; long term it is fuel. This also explains why "steering clear of it" makes the circle bigger the more you dodge: subway avoidance → detour avoidance → not daring to leave the house. The direction for breaking it is **graded exposure**: re-approach the avoided thing on a tolerable gradient, letting the nervous system recalibrate its "danger registry." The same principle applies to depression's behavioral withdrawal (2.2) — **withdrawal and avoidance are the same engine shared by two classes of disorders.**

## Face 5 · The suicide-intervention face (highest-priority chapter)

> **Thoughts of ending your life right now? Reach a crisis line now — mainland China: call 12356 (national psychological assistance hotline, 24 hours, free), or go to the nearest hospital emergency department.** Elsewhere: your local emergency number, or findahelpline.com (US 988; UK/Ireland Samaritans 116 123). *(Translator calibration; source anchor = 12356.)* Thoughts are symptoms, not destiny; leave one more night — with treatment and companionship they can change.

### 5.1 Asking directly will not "plant the idea" — the evidence is clear

[FACT] "Does asking about suicide make a person suicidal?" is one of the most thoroughly studied questions in this field. Dazzi et al. 2014's systematic review in Psychological Medicine (PMID 24998511) concluded: **asking about suicidal ideation does not increase or induce suicidal thoughts and behavior**; studies in adolescents likewise show that asking does not cause negative emotional reactions (Violence and Victims 2010, PMID 21061872). The evidence in the other direction is stable: people asked seriously and carefully generally feel understood — the channel of help-seeking opens rather than closes.

**How to ask** (calm, direct, no detours):

- "Have you been so tired lately that you've had thoughts of not wanting to live?"
- "Have you thought about hurting yourself?"

Listen to any answer without judgment. **Follow-up probing (plan? means? timing?) belongs to professional assessment; the first step you can take is to surface the thoughts and accompany the person toward help.**

### 5.2 The warning-signs list

[MODEL/clinical consensus] **Verbal signals**: direct or indirect goodbyes ("you'll be better off without me," "everyone will relax once I'm gone"), settling affairs, discussing methods of dying, a sudden expression of "relief." **Behavioral signals**: giving away treasured possessions, withdrawing from friends and family, a social cliff, searching for suicide methods, farewell-note-style writing, giving up treatment. **State signals**: severe depression, drastic insomnia change, surging alcohol/substance use, after a major loss or humiliation event. Multiple signals stacked = act the same day; do not wait.

### 5.3 Falsifying the "calm = danger passed" fallacy

[MODEL · this face's most counter-intuitive item] **"They look calm today / they smiled = the danger has passed" is a dangerous misjudgment.** Clinical consensus runs the other way: a person whose resolve is made may suddenly calm down, even lighten up, because "the pain is about to end"; such calm is not improvement — it may be the signal of having stopped struggling. Therefore: **a calm period does not lift the alert**; companionship and help-seeking actions are not downgraded before any warning sign has dissolved. By the same logic, the turning point of suicide risk often falls in "the first weeks of getting better" — mood not yet recovered while the capacity for action has returned: precisely the high-danger window.

### 5.4 The help line and the emergency line

- **12356**: the national psychological assistance hotline [FACT·verified 2026-09-12: established by the National Health Commission document of 2024-12-25, unified nationwide from May 1, 2025, free, 24 hours, providing psychological counseling, emotional support, and crisis intervention].
- **Immediate emergency line**: actively carrying out self-harm, has already ingested medication/substances, has a concrete plan and means and safety cannot be guaranteed → **call 120 directly or go to the nearest hospital emergency department** — do not phone around to discuss first.
- Psychiatric-specialty hospital emergency/crisis-intervention clinics; students can add the school counseling center and advisor systems.

### 5.5 The posture of companionship

[MODEL] For a person with suicidal thoughts, effective companionship comes down to three things:

1. **Be present, without judgment**: don't say "how could you think this way"; say more "thank you for telling me, I'm here";
2. **No debating, no preaching**: this is not the moment to correct anyone's "view of life" — debating only proves "nobody understands me";
3. **Push professional help and remove dangerous means**: accompany the registration, sit in on the visit; in the acute phase, reducing access to means at hand (medication, sharp objects) is an evidence-based suicide-prevention action.

The most powerful thing you can say is not an argument but: **"I've caught this thought; let's go find professional help — I'll go with you."**

## Face 6 · The stigma face

### 6.1 Stigma = one of the main causes of delayed care

[FACT] Clement et al. 2015's systematic review in Psychological Medicine (PMID 24569086, 144 studies included) quantitatively confirmed: stigma against mental illness is a **significant barrier to help-seeking delay and help-seeking avoidance**, with public stigma, internalized stigma (self-stigma), and structural stigma all three lines in effect. The fear of "others learning how I see myself" blocks the registration machine earlier than the symptoms do.

### 6.2 The harm mechanism of the "you're so dramatic / you overthink" discourse

[MODEL] The damage path of this popular discourse is precise and dissectible: it redefines the disease as a **character flaw** → the patient experiences symptoms as **personal failure** → internalized stigma ("I really am too fragile") → concealment of symptoms, delayed care → longer course, loss of social functioning → worse presentation → the discourse seems "even more right." A self-sealing loop. **The loop-breaking point: translate "you're just being dramatic" back into what it actually is — that is the disease's symptoms speaking, not the person's character casting a vote.**

### 6.3 The family culture of the Chinese context: both faces, as they are

[MODEL · neither romanticized nor demonized] Family culture in this domain is a double-edged sword:

- **Protective face**: the family is the first field of help-seeking and the main force of care; family support has empirical value for treatment adherence and recovery companionship; "being known by the family" is sometimes a safety net.
- **Harmful face**: mental illness is experienced as a **face event for the whole family** ("it would sound bad if it got out"); treatment decisions may be taken over by family authority; symptoms get digested by family narratives ("think positive," "think of your parents") as discourse forms of delayed care; women's and adolescents' symptoms are more easily overwritten by family narrative.

The honest posture: acknowledge the value of family support while returning the "treatment decision" to the patient and the professional doctor; the companion handles transport and logistics, not diagnosis or judgment.

## Face 7 · The red-flag chapter

### 7.1 Green line (see a doctor; can be arranged calmly)

- Low mood / loss of interest **lasting > 2 weeks** and affecting work, study, life (all three boundary criteria hit);
- Bodily symptoms with multiple unrevealing workups + a persistently gray mood baseline;
- Repeated insomnia and early waking beyond two weeks;
- Persistent numbness toward things previously loved.

### 7.2 Yellow line (see a doctor soon; counted in weeks)

- Panic attacks **frequent** (multiple times a week) or beginning clear avoidance of everyday situations;
- Anxious worry uncontrollably occupying most of each day;
- Sleep/appetite markedly disturbed and worsening;
- Self-regulation (exercise/routine/talking it out) executed for 2–4 weeks with no sign of improvement.

### 7.3 Red line (must-go-to-hospital; act the same day)

- **Any self-harm thought or suicidal thought** (including "fleeting" — even a fleeting thought deserves to be caught);
- Existing self-harm behavior, ingestion of medication, or a made plan/method;
- Command hallucinations, or firm belief of being watched and persecuted (may not be unipolar depression; needs urgent evaluation);
- Others reporting that the person is settling affairs / giving away treasured possessions.

### 7.4 Two special populations: how the signals differ

[MODEL] **Adolescents**: irritability (losing temper, agitation) can replace "low mood" as the dominant color; a sudden grade drop, school refusal, abnormal social-account behavior (sudden wiping/clearing, farewell-style posts), bodily complaints (stomachache, headache) with frequent absences, traces of self-harm (concealed scratches) — all may be depression's dialect of expression. Asking adolescents about suicidal ideation likewise does not "induce" it (PMID 21061872); parents, please ask directly and gently.

**Older adults**: more often presenting as "somatization + cognitive change" — declining memory, slower movement, quantities of "this hurts, that feels off," misattributed to "just aging / getting muddled"; suicide risk in late-life depression is systematically underestimated, and older men's suicide mortality stays high among all age groups. An older person "suddenly becoming quiet, settling matters, organizing belongings" must be taken seriously. Late-life depression is often entangled with chronic disease and drug interactions — bring the complete medication list to the visit.

## Face 8 · The falsification face (six widespread myths)

> Each item: myth → verdict → honest correction.

1. **"Just think positive and it'll pass"** → **falsified (as treatment advice)**. Depression is a medical state requiring intervention, just as "think positive" does not cure pneumonia; this sentence's only mechanism of action is to imply "your suffering is because you're inadequate" — a second injury. The companion's replacement sentence: "I'm here; we'll figure this out together."

2. **"Depression = being dramatic / weak will"** → **falsified**. The disease has bio-psycho-social multi-layer mechanisms (1.2); high-functioning, high-conscientiousness people are precisely the high-incidence group; "insisting on holding on without seeking care" is the very evidence of will being consumed by the disease. Verdict: this is stigma discourse, not a medical statement.

3. **"Exercise cures all depression"** → **falsified (as an over-promise)**. Exercise has real effect sizes (BMJ 2024 NMA), but the original text itself qualifies "confidence low-to-moderate," positioning it as "one of the core treatments alongside psychotherapy and drugs" — **one of**. For severe episodes and people at suicide risk, substituting exercise for comprehensive treatment is dangerous behavior. Correct version: exercise is part of the prescription, not the whole prescription.

4. **"Once you start antidepressants you're on them for life"** → **falsified**. The common course is continuing consolidation for 4–9 months or more after acute treatment of a first episode; only multiple relapses call for longer maintenance; whether and when to taper is the doctor's individualized decision based on relapse risk, and tapering must be slow (3.2). The real traps are instead "stopping on your own just as it starts working" and "stopping abruptly" — both directions are true; only together are they honest.

5. **"Psychotherapy is just paying someone to chat"** → **falsified**. Evidence-based psychotherapy (CBT et al.) is a structured treatment plan: assessment, case formulation, intervention techniques, outcome follow-up, with ample meta-analytic evidence (Butler 2006). "Chatting" is the vehicle, not the content — just as calling surgery "just cutting" ignores the whole of medicine. Verdict: confusing professional treatment with everyday comfort.

6. **"Asking about suicide induces suicide"** → **falsified (evidence clear)**. Systematic-review conclusion: asking about suicidal ideation does not induce suicidal thoughts and behavior (Dazzi 2014, PMID 24998511); adolescent studies in the same direction (PMID 21061872). Not asking is the real risk — it leaves the person in silence. Verdict: this is the most widely spread of repeatedly falsified myths, and this canon answers it with a full highest-priority face.

## Closing: how to use this canon

- For yourself: the three boundaries (time/symptom/function) help you judge "should I see a doctor" — if in doubt, go; the cost of going in the wrong direction is far below the cost of delay.
- For others: the companionship trio — **being present without judgment, listening without preaching, pushing professional help**.
- For everyone: treat "asking one 'are you okay?'" and "accompanying one registration" as this era's cheapest and most precious healing actions.

> Unknowns and boundaries (honesty clause): depression's exact neural mechanism remains open (the monoamine hypothesis is only one hypothesis); the exercise-modality evidence in network meta-analysis carries limited confidence; the panic-attack time window is textbook-consensus framing, not a precise constant; this canon contains no individual dosing decisions — that belongs to the in-person visit.
