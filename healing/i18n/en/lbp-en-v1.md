# Low Back Pain Canon v1 (a healing-universe volume)

> One-line master framing [FACT]: low back pain is today's number-one cause of disability worldwide, yet **the great majority of back pain has no identifiable structural cause and is self-limiting** — the real danger signals are the few "red flags," and the most common harm is not the pain itself but the systematic misjudgment of "should I move, should I get imaged, should I take pills." This page's seven faces: epidemiology → mechanism → red flags → self-management → falsification → action cards → evidence anchors.

---

## §1 Epidemiology face: how common, how heavy

**1.1 The world's number-one disability cause** [FACT]
In the GBD 2021 framing, low back pain is the leading global cause of YLDs (years lived with disability), with the burden rising continuously across 1990–2020 (PMID 37273833). The Lancet low back pain series: global LBP YLDs grew 54% from 1990 to 2015, mainly from population growth and aging, with the largest increases in low- and middle-income countries (PMID 29573870).

**1.2 The GBD-framing definition** [FACT]
GBD defines low back pain as "pain between the 12th rib and the gluteal fold, lasting ≥1 day," with epidemiological data across different definitions harmonized by network meta-regression (PMID 37273833). Note: this is a **symptom-based** definition, not a structural diagnosis — the definition itself embodies the "nonspecific" stance [INFERENCE].

**1.3 Prevalence on three time scales** [FACT (directional)]
Point prevalence about 7–8%, one-year prevalence about 1/3, lifetime prevalence up to around 80% — study framings differ considerably, but "almost no one escapes it" is the stable conclusion (PMID 29573870 · 21982256 · 24315140). It occurs in all age groups and in low-, middle-, and high-income countries alike (PMID 29573870). Precise age-stratified numbers vary by framing [UNKNOWN: this page carries no unverified numbers].

**1.4 Who is at higher risk** [FACT]
People in heavy manual occupations, those with physical and mental comorbidities, smokers, and people with obesity report the most low back pain (PMID 29573870, abstract verbatim). The attributable burden of smoking on low back pain has been estimated across 204 countries (PMID 40709036).

**1.5 Serious causes are a tiny share** [FACT]
A Sydney primary-care inception cohort of 1172 acute low back pain cases: only 11 cases (0.9%, of which 8 fractures) of serious spinal pathology confirmed within 12 months — malignancy, infection, ankylosing spondylitis etc. together under 1% (PMID 19790051). **The same study**: 80.4% of patients carried at least 1 red flag (median 2) [FACT] → inference: red flags are common while serious disease is rare, so any single red flag's positive predictive value is low [INFERENCE]. This is the quantitative bedrock of every statement in this page's red-flag and self-management faces.

## §2 Mechanism face: where the pain comes from

**2.1 Nonspecific low back pain = the norm, not a dismissive diagnosis** [FACT]
For nearly all people with low back pain, no specific nociceptive source can be identified; only a minority have a clear pathological cause — vertebral fracture, malignancy, infection, etc. (PMID 29573870 abstract verbatim; see also the two Lancet reviews 21982256 · 27745712, same framing). "Nonspecific" does not mean "nothing was found" — it means **that under current evidence structural attribution itself is untenable or unnecessary** [MODEL].

**2.2 The imaging–pain dissociation** [FACT]
Degenerative imaging changes of the spine increase steeply with age in asymptomatic populations (disc degeneration, bulges, high-intensity zones of the nucleus pulposus are widespread in asymptomatic adults; PMID 25430861, cited 750+). **Aging seen on imaging is not the cause of pain**; the imaging–symptom dissociation is one of the most robust findings in pain science [FACT]. The two Lancet reviews reach the same direction: low back pain is often over-attributed to aging changes on imaging (PMID 21982256 · 27745712) [FACT].

**2.3 The acute-phase natural history: self-limiting but not universally resolved** [FACT]
Meta-analysis of inception cohorts: pain and disability in acute low back pain improve most markedly in the first 6 weeks after onset, then plateau; a substantial proportion still have mild-to-moderate pain or disability residual at 12 months (PMID 22586331). **"It is self-limiting" and "everyone recovers" are two different sentences** [INFERENCE].

**2.4 The road to chronicity: not just a tissue problem** [MODEL]
The biopsychosocial model: tissue, psychological (catastrophizing, fear-avoidance, mood), and social (work, compensation, family support) layers jointly shape the pain experience and chronicization (the two Lancet series, same framing; PMID 29573870 · 29573872). The fear-avoidance cycle is one of the core behavioral pathways of chronicization: catastrophizing → activity avoidance → functional decline → worse pain → more fear (a fear-avoidance node already exists in the pain graph, relayed here) [MODEL]. Sleep, mood, and low back pain feed each other: pain disturbs sleep, insomnia lowers pain threshold, mood amplifies nociception — directional [MODEL] (the sleep face is relayed via the insomnia graph's canon node; this page carries no unverified effect sizes).

**2.5 Pain is the nervous system's output, not a seismograph of injury** [MODEL]
The central perspective allied to the imaging–pain dissociation: in the chronic phase the peripheral tissue contribution falls while central sensitization and altered pain-modulation networks contribute more [MODEL]. This mechanistic framework explains why "it still hurts after the structure was fixed" and "it hurts with no structure found," but it cannot explain all phenomena [INFERENCE].

**2.6 Recurrence is the norm** [FACT (directional)]
Low back pain runs an episode–remission–recurrence course; the very premise of prevention research is a high recurrence rate (PMID 26752509 inclusion logic; 29573872). Precise recurrence figures vary by definition [UNKNOWN].

## §3 Red-flag face: when "wait and see" is not allowed

> The purpose of red flags is **to screen out that <1%** (§1.5), not to manufacture panic. Having a red flag ≠ having serious disease; having a red flag = a doctor should judge.

**3.1 Cauda equina syndrome (the emergency within emergencies)** [FACT]
The combination of saddle anesthesia (numbness where you sit on a saddle), new bowel or bladder incontinence or retention, weakness of both legs, bilateral sciatica-like pain, and sudden sexual dysfunction points to cauda equina compression — **immediate emergency care** (same-day imaging plus surgical assessment; NICE NG59 framing, corroborated by the pain graph's redflag_cauda_equina node). The midsagittal diameter of the lumbar spinal canal is smaller in cauda equina patients than in disc-herniation patients without cauda equina involvement (objective support; PMID 29023556). **Unilateral symptoms weigh less, bilateral symptoms weigh more** is a reasonable formalization of clinical intuition [INFERENCE].

**3.2 Unexplained weight loss + cancer history** [FACT]
Unexplained weight loss, new low back pain in a person with a cancer history, or persistent night pain unrelated to posture and activity — suggests malignancy (PMID 29573870 lists malignancy among the definite causes to consider; NICE NG59 red-flag framing). In a person with a tumor history, back pain is not "the old problem acting up again" [INFERENCE]. The aging-care graph already has a "weight-loss red flag" node (relayed from the agc graph).

**3.3 Fever + spinal pain** [FACT]
Fever with focal spinal percussion tenderness or progressive pain suggests discitis, epidural abscess, and other spinal infections — higher risk in the immunocompromised, people with diabetes, injection drug use, or recent spinal procedures (PMID 29573870 lists infection among the definite causes; relayed from the infection graph's redflags node). **Fever + back pain ≠ the backache of a cold**: persistent fever with focal, fixed back pain means same-day care [INFERENCE].

**3.4 Trauma and bone quality** [FACT]
Low back pain after defined trauma (falls, car crashes, heavy crushing loads) is evaluated on the fracture pathway; in older adults, sudden back pain after trivial force (bending, sneezing) raises suspicion of an osteoporotic vertebral compression fracture — one that may itself "not hurt much" and be overlooked (relayed from the osteoporosis graph's vf-silent · myth-massage-vf nodes: violent massage/tuina on an osteoporotic spine is forbidden) [FACT].

**3.5 Progressive neurological deficit** [FACT]
Progressive leg weakness, an expanding numbness field, foot drop / gait dragging — neurological function is going downhill; treat as an emergency (NICE NG59 framing; 29573870 the same).

**3.6 The right mindset for red flags** [INFERENCE]
Read §1.5's numbers in reverse: 80.4% have red flags but 0.9% have serious disease — red flags are the sieve for **whether to seek care**, not the verdict on **whether disease exists**. A single red flag (e.g., "recently a bit thinner") mostly maps to benign explanations; red flags **in clusters** or **progressing** rise steeply in weight [MODEL].

## §4 Self-management face: what you can manage yourself, and how

> Applicability precondition: none of §3's red flags is present, pain and disability are within tolerable range, and the overall trend is improving. If any one fails → seek care.

**4.1 First principle: keep moving, do not take to bed** [FACT]
Cochrane systematic review: for acute low back pain, the "stay active" advice is slightly better than "bed rest" on pain and disability (SMD of about 0.2 magnitude), with no difference for sciatica; bed rest has no evidence advantage on any outcome (PMID 20556780). The classic Finnish cohort trial points the same way: daily activity beats bed rest (PMID 7798072). **Bed rest is not a treatment — it is an accelerator of chronicization** [CONTESTED (contrary to the popular belief of "a hundred days of bed rest for injured bones and sinews")].

**4.2 Exercise: the double mainstay of treatment and prevention** [FACT]
Exercise therapy for chronic nonspecific low back pain: Cochrane 2021 pooled 249 trials — pain probably improves (moderate certainty), function improves but the effect size is small (below the minimal clinically important difference threshold; PMID 34580864). Prevention direction: exercise (often combined with education) is the only intervention shown to reduce the risk of low back pain recurrence; education alone, back belts, and insoles show no consistent benefit (PMID 26752509). **"Exercise ≠ damaging the back" is the evidence-side conclusion** [CONTESTED]. Which exercise is optimal [UNKNOWN — head-to-head differences across forms are unstable; the one you can sustain is best (INFERENCE)].

**4.3 The honest account of drugs** [FACT]
- **Paracetamol (acetaminophen)**: the large PACE RCT — for acute low back pain it did not speed recovery versus placebo (PMID 25064594); pooled evidence for spinal pain points the same "ineffective" way (PMID 25828856).
- **NSAIDs**: effective for spinal pain but with an effect size below the 10/100 minimal clinically important threshold (PMID 28153830) — "somewhat useful, but don't expect it to solve it" [INFERENCE]. NSAIDs carry gastrointestinal/kidney/cardiovascular burdens (honest-account nodes already exist in neighboring graphs, relayed); they are not "safe enough to take casually."
- **Opioids are not first-line**: systematic reviews in chronic low back pain show weak efficacy evidence, short trials with high dropout, and no improvement in return-to-work outcomes (PMID 25561513); the ACP guideline is explicit that first-line is non-drug care plus NSAIDs/muscle relaxants etc., with opioids considered only when other options fail and benefit outweighs harm (PMID 28192789; the 17909209 earlier guideline points the same way) [FACT].
- **Muscle relaxants, antidepressants, injections, supplements** each occupy very different evidence positions; this page does not expand the details [UNKNOWN preserved] — decisions go through §6 and a discussion with your doctor.

**4.4 The timing logic of imaging** [FACT]
In acute/subacute low back pain without red flags, **routine imaging does not improve outcomes**: an RCT (380 cases) of rapid MRI replacing plain radiography showed no clinical benefit and a tendency toward more downstream tests and treatments (PMID 12783911); ACP high-value-care advice: no imaging routinely without red flags (PMID 21282698; 28192789 the same direction). The logic chain: imaging findings ≠ cause of pain (§2.2) → imaging indications are driven by red flags and treatment decisions, not by "let's take a look just in case" [INFERENCE]. The screening graph's "screening ≠ diagnosis" and "more tests is a myth" nodes are structurally interlocked corroboration (relayed from the chk graph).

**4.5 Sleep, mood, and recovery-phase management** [MODEL]
During recovery, maintain daily roles while allowing pain to exist; pain fluctuation ≠ injury progression (§2.5); sleep deprivation amplifies the pain experience — make sleep part of recovery management (directional MODEL; effect sizes not carried on this page). The comorbidity channel of mood and pain: see the mood graph nodes, relayed.

**4.6 Pregnancy and special populations** [INFERENCE / relayed citation]
Low back pain in pregnancy is common and medication options are limited — see the women's-health graph's pregnancy node (relayed from the fem graph); in older adults, rule out bone-quality problems first (§3.4) before discussing exercise (relayed from the agc graph). This page carries no specific plans for special populations [UNKNOWN].

## §5 Falsification list (each with its falsifier)

> Epistemic discipline: each item is a statement that can be overturned, with "what evidence appearing would overturn it."

**F1 [CONTESTED] "Back pain needs rest — lying down heals it faster."**
Claim: bed rest is an effective treatment for acute low back pain.
Falsifier: already overturned in reverse — if new high-quality evidence showed bed rest outcomes superior to staying active, this item would flip; current Cochrane and cohort evidence (PMID 20556780 · 7798072) points the opposite way.

**F2 [CONTESTED] "The disc herniation/degeneration found on imaging is the cause of the back pain."**
Claim: a stable causal correspondence exists between aging changes on imaging and low back pain.
Falsifier: if imaging degeneration rates in asymptomatic populations stopped rising with age, or imaging findings stably corresponded to pain location/intensity at the individual level, this item would flip; the current systematic review in asymptomatic populations (PMID 25430861) shows widespread changes dissociated from symptoms.

**F3 [FACT, falsifiable] "The vast majority of low back pain has no specific structural cause."**
Falsifier: if a new diagnostic technology could find an actionable structural cause in most people with low back pain and intervening on it beat controls, this item would be downgraded and §2.1 rewritten; the current Lancet series states "nearly all" cannot be identified (PMID 29573870).

**F4 [FACT, falsifiable] "Serious pathology is <1% in primary-care acute low back pain."**
Falsifier: if cohorts from other regions/populations (e.g., direct-referral populations) were systematically higher, then §1.5's 0.9% would apply only to primary-care inception populations and the red-flag logic would need population-level recalibration (PMID 19790051's domain = Sydney primary-care inception cohort).

**F5 [CONTESTED] "Exercise wears the back out; with back pain you should move less."**
Falsifier: if prevention/treatment RCTs consistently showed exercise increases low back pain occurrence or worsens pain, this item would flip; current evidence points the opposite way (PMID 34580864 · 26752509). Note the domain: nonspecific low back pain after red flags are excluded; structural instability etc. needs individualization [INFERENCE].

**F6 [FACT, falsifiable] "Paracetamol works for acute low back pain."**
Refuted record: the PACE RCT and the spinal pain meta both fail to support it (PMID 25064594 · 25828856). Falsifier: a new RCT in acute low back pain showing benefit, and replicated.

**F7 [FACT, falsifiable] "Opioids are a first-line drug for low back pain."**
Refuted record: weak efficacy evidence + safety burden + guidelines positioning them non-first-line (PMID 25561513 · 28192789). Falsifier: new RCTs showing clear long-term functional benefit with acceptable safety, and guideline revision.

**F8 [FACT, falsifiable] "Even without red flags you should get imaged — find it early, worry early (and be reassured)."**
Refuted record: early imaging does not improve outcomes and increases downstream testing (PMID 12783911 · 21282698). Falsifier: a new RCT showing early imaging in red-flag-free populations improves pain/disability/patient-relevant outcomes.

**F9 [MODEL, falsifiable] "Chronic low back pain is mainly psychological (or the counter-proposition: purely structural)."**
Both polarized propositions are too simple: biopsychosocial is a multi-factor framework, not a "psychosomatic suggestion" theory. Falsifier: if any single-factor model could explain a large share of chronic low back pain variance and deliver replicable intervention advantages, the framework would be downgraded to a footnote of that factor (framework itself: 29573870 · 29573872).

**F10 [INFERENCE, falsifiable] "Recurrence is high enough that prevention is worth doing."**
Falsifier: if the prevention meta-update showed exercise has no effect on recurrence, §4.2's prevention paragraph would be deleted (currently PMID 26752509 supports effectiveness).

## §6 Action cards (the discuss-with-your-doctor frame)

> This card carries no doses and specifies no regimens — every "whether, which one, how long" below is a **question list** for discussing with your doctor.

**Card A | Back hurts right now, no red flags (the self-management window)**
1. Run through §3's red-flag list (60 seconds); any hit → Card E.
2. Keep active: keep walking the walks you can walk, keep doing the daily things you can do; pain within a tolerable range does not mean damage (§4.1).
3. Avoid continuous bed rest; for relief, brief posture adjustments suffice — no "absolute rest."
4. If considering medication: bring questions to your doctor/pharmacist — "which one, for how long, does it fit my gastrointestinal/kidney/blood-pressure situation" (§4.3's honest account).
5. Keep one observation line: pain 0–10, activity capacity, sleep — week 6 is the natural-history judgment node (§2.3).

**Card B | No improvement at 6 weeks (booked visit, not emergency)**
1. Bring your observation-line records (onset, trend, what worsens/relieves).
2. Questions to discuss with your doctor: Have my red flags been assessed? Do I need imaging — **why or why not** (§4.4)? Should my work/sleep/mood be part of the plan (§2.4)?
3. Discuss with your doctor the specific form and progression path of exercise therapy (§4.2: no verdict on form; the sustainable one wins).

**Card C | Chronic low back pain (>3 months; watershed framing relayed from the pain graph)**
1. Shift the goal from "eliminate the pain" to "function and participation" — discuss achievable goals with your doctor.
2. Discuss: the exercise plan, the behavioral/psychological pathway (which link the fear-avoidance sits at — mood/pain graphs, relayed), and the true benefit-burden ratio of drugs (§4.3).
3. Beware the "structural repair narrative": surgery/injections/supplements each occupy different evidence positions; ask "what is the evidence for this intervention for **my combination of imaging findings + symptoms**" (§2.2 · §4.4; evidence-position details are UNKNOWN on this page).

**Card D | Back pain in older adults (rule out bone first, then talk training)**
1. Sudden back pain after trivial force → screen for vertebral compression fracture first (§3.4).
2. Combine the exercise pathway with sarcopenia/fall-risk assessment (relayed from the agc graph nodes).
3. Medication review: in people on multiple medications, NSAID/muscle-relaxant burdens are higher — discuss deprescribing with your doctor (relayed from the agc graph's polypharmacy node).

**Card E | Red flag positive (the emergency pathway)**
1. Any combination of the cauda equina pentad (§3.1) → emergency department immediately; do not wait for an appointment.
2. Fever + spinal pain, post-trauma, progressive weakness, cancer history + weight loss (§3.2–3.5) → same-day emergency/urgent care pathway.
3. At care, state clearly: the red-flag items, the onset timeline, medication history — clustering and progression of red flags are the weight-critical facts (§3.6).

**Card F | The universal seven questions for discussing with your doctor**
① What is my most likely explanation? ② Have all red flags been run through? ③ Would imaging/tests change management — and if not, why do them? ④ What is the first-line intervention and how strong is the evidence? ⑤ Under what circumstances must I come back (time points + symptoms)? ⑥ Do my other conditions/medications conflict with this plan? ⑦ If we first observe conservatively — observe what, for how long?
