① Why does my stomach hurt?

One-sentence answer. Most stomach pain traces back to stomach acid, irregular eating, stress, tobacco and alcohol, and certain painkillers; for pain that keeps coming back, testing for Helicobacter pylori first is the sensible entry point.

By situation

  1. Burning pain when the stomach is empty, after spicy food, or after drinking — with acid reflux: keep meals regular, stop eating 2–3 hours before bed, go easy on tobacco, alcohol, and strong tea.
  2. Pain that shows up during long stretches of stress, with tests finding nothing serious — functional pain is common: a regular eating window plus a way to bring stress down (see EN4-4, the anxiety card).
  3. Pain after taking aspirin or ibuprofen-type painkillers — the medicine itself may be irritating the stomach. Don't stay on it on your own; ask the doctor to adjust.
  4. Pain that keeps recurring, especially with stomach disease in the family — test for H. pylori; if it's positive, follow the doctor's eradication plan exactly.

Red flags (emergency department now)

  • Severe abdominal pain with a belly that is rigid, hard like a board → emergency department, to rule out perforation or an acute abdomen.
  • Vomiting blood, or black, tarry, coffee-ground stools → emergency department, on the gastrointestinal-bleeding pathway.

Evidence & boundaries. The life advice above is model-level candidate (not a diagnosis); the red-flag lines come from the acute-emergencies face; diagnosis, eradication plans, and all medication decisions belong to doctors.

② Why am I always tired?

One-sentence answer. The most common tiredness is not a hidden serious disease — it is too little sleep, too little movement, food that doesn't fit, and too much pressure. But tiredness that persists also deserves checks for anemia, thyroid problems, and sleep apnea.

By situation

  1. Late nights / sleep debt — repair sleep regularity first: a full 7–8 hours (evidence grade A).
  2. Sedentary tiredness that rest doesn't fix — get moving at low intensity: a daily brisk walk, gentle aerobic work (evidence grade A).
  3. Tiredness with a pale face, or (for women) heavy periods — think anemia: get a complete blood count at a clinic. Don't iron-supplement on your own.
  4. Heavy snoring, sleepy all day despite enough hours in bed — check for sleep apnea; get a medical evaluation.

Red flags (immediate emergency help)

  • Sudden difficulty breathing → immediate emergency help.
  • Tiredness together with chest pain → treat as an emergency.

Evidence & boundaries. Life advice is model-level; the mechanism picture of chronic fatigue syndrome is thin and honestly marked unknown; the medical-evaluation face takes priority; anemia treatment and medication belong to doctors.

③ What to do when you can't sleep

One-sentence answer. Hold regularity first: a fixed wake time, natural daylight during the day, screens off for the hour before bed; if your mind won't stop when you lie down, just watch your breathing — and if sleep is still poor after three weeks, see a doctor.

By situation

  1. Hard to fall asleep, mind racing — spend ten minutes before sleep simply watching the breath rise and fall; let thoughts arrive and pass without chasing them (stillness lets the system reset; it is not forceful mind-emptying).
  2. Enough hours in bed but still sleepy all day, plus snoring — get checked for sleep apnea.
  3. Shift work / jet lag — the rhythm itself is disrupted: anchoring a fixed wake time plus morning light works better than forcing yourself into bed early.
  4. Can't sleep because of worries — sort them into "things I can act on / things I can't", write them down to handle tomorrow (see EN4-4, the anxiety card).

Red flags (immediate emergency help)

  • Waking at night gasping, with sudden breathing difficulty or chest pain → immediate emergency help.

Evidence & boundaries. Behavioral advice is model-level candidate; this card makes no suggestion about sleeping pills of any kind — prescribing and stopping them belongs to doctors.

④ How do I calm anxiety / actually relax?

One-sentence answer. Anxiety is mostly the "imagined danger" alarm firing: slow the breath, write the worry down and split it into "can act / can't control", move your body, see real people — and when it starts to eat your daily life, get professional help.

By situation

  1. The disaster movie is playing in your head before anything has happened — sort the suffering into exaggerated / paid-in-advance / not yours to carry, then ask one question: "is anything actually broken, right now?"
  2. Racing heart, shaky hands, can't sit still — use the breath as a lever: slow, deep exhales (breathing is the only top-down switch of the autonomic nervous system — the heart-rate-variability mechanism).
  3. The same kind of worry circling back again and again — write the vague feeling into clear sentences. Written clearly, it stops "attacking you" and becomes "a thing that can be handled".
  4. Fear over big things you cannot control, big enough to kill appetite and sleep — honestly mark those as "can't control". The feeling stands down, and the energy moves back to one small step you can take.

Red flags (act now)

  • Thoughts of harming yourself, in any form → contact a crisis line or go to the emergency department now: 988 (US) or your local crisis line; in mainland China 12356 (24 hours).

Evidence & boundaries. Life methods are model-level; diagnosis and medication belong to the professional face.

⑤ How do I bring cholesterol down?

One-sentence answer. Get one lipid panel for a baseline; lowering cholesterol rests on years of "real food + daily movement + less visceral fat"; when numbers are high or cardiovascular risk exists, medication goes by the doctor's instructions — never stop it on your own.

By situation

  1. Mildly high on a checkup, nothing else wrong — swap the food to real food: less fried food and refined sugar, a regular eating window (evidence grade A, epidemiological); move every day (evidence grade A).
  2. Also high blood pressure, diabetes, or smoking — risks stack. Get an overall cardiovascular assessment; don't fixate on a single number.
  3. Heart attacks at a young age in the family, or yellowish thickenings on the tendons found at exam — suspect familial high cholesterol: seeing a doctor is a must; diet alone will not be enough.
  4. On medication, numbers now normal, tempted to stop — the normal numbers are the medicine working. Whether to stop, and how, belongs to the doctor; stopping on your own tends to rebound.

Red flags (call emergency services now)

  • Chest pain with a squeezing, pressing quality, radiating to the arm or jaw → call emergency services immediately.

Evidence & boundaries. Lifestyle advice is model-level candidate; specific lipid-lowering plans and target values are beyond what this card can responsibly claim — medical care first (doctor / registered dietitian).

⑥ How do I lose weight without gaining it back?

One-sentence answer. The key to no rebound is not crash dieting — it is switching to habits you could keep for a lifetime: real food, a regular eating window, daily movement, enough sleep. Losing slowly is losing fast.

By situation

  1. Stuck in a repeat diet-and-rebound cycle — crash methods are a strong sustained stimulus; the body adapts and desensitizes. Switch to a small, sustainable version: start with "half of every meal is real food".
  2. The task feels too big, no idea where to start — slice it to a piece five minutes can finish, and move first, think after (great things start from the fine-grained).
  3. More and more self-blame; one slip and you quit — self-condemnation works against health; self-acceptance is what makes change sustainable.
  4. Weight gain with heavy snoring and daytime sleepiness, or darkening in skin folds — get checked for sleep apnea and for the blood-sugar / metabolic face.

Red flags (emergency pathway)

  • After extreme dieting: palpitations, fainting, sudden difficulty breathing → emergency-care pathway, don't wait.

Evidence & boundaries. Everything here is model-level candidate; weight-loss medication and surgery belong to professional evaluation.

⑦ What to do about high blood pressure?

One-sentence answer. One high office reading doesn't count — measure at home, morning and evening, and record it as a line; after diagnosis, flatten the whole line with less salt, regular movement, enough sleep, and medication as prescribed. Never stop by feel.

By situation

  1. Borderline high on a checkup (prehypertension) — lifestyle first: daily gentle aerobic movement plus leaving fallow gaps for an over-tense system, then measure again.
  2. Diagnosed and on medication — three things: keep moving so the vessels flex with the daily rhythm; let deep sleep leave the vessels a repair window; keep sodium and stress stimuli intermittent. Watch the rhythm curve of your home readings, not any single number.
  3. High at a young age, or many family members with it — get evaluated for secondary causes. This is not "just an age thing".
  4. Normal on medication, tempted to stop — normal numbers mean the treatment is working. Adjusting or stopping belongs to the doctor; stopping by feel rebounds, and can rebound dangerously.

Red flags (call emergency services now)

  • Chest pain with a squeezing quality, radiating to arm or jaw → call emergency services immediately.
  • Sudden one-sided weakness, slurred speech, or facial droop → emergency help now, and note the exact time symptoms began (the clot-busting window).
  • Sudden difficulty breathing → emergency help now.

Evidence & boundaries. The rhythm narrative is model-level candidate; specific medication choices and target values belong entirely to the professional face.

⑧ What is love — and how do you keep a long relationship alive?

One-sentence answer. A good long relationship doesn't run on never fighting; it runs on repair outpacing wear: say specific thank-yous, open arguments well, pause when flooded, and accept that some disagreements are here to stay.

By situation

  1. Fighting over small things until it blows up — watch the ratio of positive to negative interactions: in stable couples, positive interactions run roughly five times the negative ones. Give one specific thank-you every day — thank the actual thing, not generic praise.
  2. Every opening is an accusation, every reply a counterattack, drifting into mutual silence — the antidotes to the four destructive patterns: criticism → start with "I feel + I need"; defensiveness → own your share; contempt → deliberately grow appreciation; stonewalling → agree on how long the pause lasts, and come back on time.
  3. Mid-fight, heart pounding, mind overheating — above a heart rate of about 100, people genuinely cannot hear each other. Pause at least 20 minutes and do something truly calming (not suppressed sulking), then return to the conversation.
  4. Love worn thin by daily life — wear is constant; healthy means the repair rate exceeds the wear rate. Keep a fixed 15-minute weekly "state of us" meeting: one thank-you, one issue, and book next week's.

Red flags (act now)

  • Thoughts of self-harm in either partner → crisis line or emergency department now: 988 (US) or your local crisis line; in mainland China 12356 (24 hours).
  • Violence or control in the relationship → seek professional help (this card does not apply to that situation).

Evidence & boundaries. Mostly observational model-level (a few items are fact-level observations or physiology); couples therapy, trauma, and any violence situation belong to professionals.

⑨ How do I read a thyroid nodule report?

One-sentence answer. A nodule is not cancer — the great majority of nodules never cause harm in a whole lifetime. Read the TI-RADS grade on the report first, set the follow-up rhythm by the grade, and don't frighten yourself.

By situation

  1. Report says TI-RADS category 1–2 — mostly benign appearance; routine re-checks at the intervals the doctor advises are enough.
  2. Report says category 3 — probably benign; usually needs periodic follow-up (the interval is the doctor's call).
  3. Report says category 4 or above — needs further evaluation. Bring the full report to the visit; don't delay, and don't panic (category 4 ≠ cancer).
  4. Any grade, plus functional symptoms (palpitations, intolerance of heat, fatigue, weight change) — add a thyroid-function test; get an endocrinology evaluation.

Red flags (seek care promptly)

  • A hoarse voice, a neck swelling that grows fast, or difficulty breathing or swallowing → seek care promptly.

Evidence & boundaries. Model-level knowledge tidy-up; grade interpretation and follow-up plans all belong to doctors — this card only helps you read what the report is saying.

⑩ Low back pain — do I need an X-ray?

One-sentence answer. Most low back pain settles on its own within 4–6 weeks — graded activity beats bed rest; imaging has clear indications, and without red-flag signs, back pain is usually watched and treated first. No rush to scan.

By situation

  1. A twist, a dull sore ache, no leg symptoms — graded activity first: walk if you can walk, move if you can move, and avoid long bed rest (recovery is actually faster that way).
  2. Pain past 4–6 weeks, not fading or getting worse — medical evaluation; the doctor decides whether imaging is needed.
  3. Recurrent sitting-related back pain — look at work posture and core strength first: the chair, how you stand, a daily brisk walk. Look at these before looking at films (see the pain canon volume).
  4. Any red-flag line below — no watching-and-waiting; emergency department now.

Red flags (emergency department now)

  • Leg weakness, numbness in the saddle area (the perineum), or loss of control of bowel or bladder → emergency department now.
  • Back pain after an injury, or with unexplained weight loss, or waking at night from the pain → emergency department now.

Evidence & boundaries. Model-level; whether to image is decided by doctors, by indication — this card only offers a sense of time and of activity before the visit.
